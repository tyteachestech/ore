# Near and Far Transfer in Cognitive Training: A Second-Order Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Sala, G., Aksayli, N.D., Tatlidil, K.S., Tatsumi, T., Gondo, Y., & Gobet, F. (2019)
- **Study Type**: Second-order meta-analysis (meta-analysis of meta-analyses)
- **Sample**: n=1,555 effect sizes, k=332 primary studies, N=21,968 participants, drawn from 10 first-order meta-analyses
- **Journal**: Collabra: Psychology, 5(1): 18
- **DOI**: 10.1525/collabra.203

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Far transfer is null after correction**: Across all three models (WM training alone, WM training by population, WM + video games + music + chess + exergames), once publication bias and placebo effects are controlled for, the grand mean far-transfer effect equals zero with true variance also equal to zero (σ² = 0). The paper's summary: "everything is distilled to a parsimonious answer and a single number: zero."
  - Model 2 (WM far transfer, corrected, active controls only): g = 0.01 (Table 8)
  - Model 3 (all training types, corrected, active controls only): g = 0.00 (Table 12)

- **Finding 2 — Uncorrected far transfer appears spuriously positive**: Before controlling for placebo and publication bias, Model 2 omnibus = g = 0.12 (95% CI [0.08, 0.16], k=119) and Model 3 omnibus = g = 0.13 (95% CI [0.10, 0.16], k=233). These "effects" vanish when active controls are used and publication bias is corrected.

- **Finding 3 — Near transfer from WM training is real but modest**: WM training produces genuine near-transfer effects to similar memory tasks across all populations, with effects ranging from g = 0.14 (adults, corrected) to g = 0.49 (TD children, corrected, active controls).
  - Grand mean (Model 1, uncorrected): g = 0.30
  - Grand mean (Model 1, corrected): g = 0.21
  - Omnibus mean (Model 1, uncorrected): g = 0.32 (95% CI [0.26, 0.38], k=99)
  - Omnibus mean (Model 1, corrected): g = 0.19 (PEESE estimate)

- **Finding 4 — Near-transfer effect is moderated by population**: True variance (σ²) accounts for 25–75% of between-meta-analysis variance in Model 1, confirming genuine population differences.
  - TD children: g = 0.45 (uncorrected), g = 0.50 (PEESE corrected), active control g = 0.49
  - LD children: g = 0.55 (uncorrected), corrected ~g = 0.26
  - Adults: g = 0.20 (uncorrected), g = 0.17 (corrected), active control g = 0.14
  - Older adults: g = 0.29 (uncorrected), g = 0.16 (corrected), active control g = 0.16

- **Finding 5 — No training type produces far transfer**: Individual training-program results (all corrected, active controls where available):
  - Action video games (adults): g = -0.01
  - Non-action video games (adults): g = 0.00
  - Video games (older adults): g = -0.03
  - Music training (children): g = -0.02
  - Chess training (children): g = 0.01
  - Exergames (older adults): g = -0.02

- **Finding 6 — The control group type fully explains observed heterogeneity**: In Models 2 and 3, the between-meta-analysis true variance (τ²) observed in uncorrected models (comparing all controls) disappears to zero once only active control comparisons are used. Placebo inflation — not genuine training effects — was driving apparent differences across meta-analyses.

- **Finding 7 — Near-transfer may not represent true cognitive enhancement**: Authors note (citing Shipstead et al., 2012) that near-transfer tasks likely share overlapping perceptual/conceptual features with trained tasks. People become better at a class of similar tasks, not cognitively enhanced. This interpretation is consistent with the absence of far-transfer effects.

**Heterogeneity — Far Transfer Models**: τ² = 0.000 in virtually all corrected active-control models. True variance (σ²) between adjusted meta-analytic means = 0 in all far-transfer models. This means the differences across training programs and populations in far transfer are entirely due to second-order sampling error — a single null effect explains all of them.

**Publication bias magnitude**: Corrected and uncorrected grand means differ by no more than 0.10g (0.05g in active-control models) across all three models. Bias is present but the core finding holds at both the corrected and uncorrected level in the active-control analysis.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Common elements theory (Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901)**: The dominant explanation for results. Near transfer occurs because trained tasks and transfer tasks share perceptual/conceptual features. Far transfer fails because the overlap is insufficient. Skill acquisition is fundamentally domain-specific.

- **Moderator — Population type (near transfer)**: Genuine moderator. TD children benefit most from WM near transfer (g ≈ 0.49 corrected), then LD children, then older adults, then adults. Proposed mechanism: cognitive plasticity and learning pace differ by developmental stage.

- **Moderator — Control group type (far transfer)**: The strongest moderator. Studies using passive controls (e.g., watching videos) show inflated far-transfer effects. Studies using active controls (engaging, cognitively demanding alternative activities) reduce far-transfer effects to near zero. This is the placebo confound — expectancy effects + test familiarity inflate apparent benefits when controls are insufficiently engaged.

- **Population type as far-transfer moderator**: No evidence of moderation. Far-transfer effects are null across TD children, LD children, adults, and older adults equally once corrected. True between-meta-analysis variance σ² = 0 in all far-transfer models.

- **Training type as far-transfer moderator**: No evidence of moderation. WM training, action video games, non-action video games, music, chess, and exergames all produce zero far transfer when corrected. σ² = 0 across all.

## Study Design Details

- **Design**: Second-order meta-analysis (meta-analysis of 10 first-order meta-analyses), plus omnibus first-order meta-analysis as sensitivity check
- **Included meta-analyses**: 10 total — 4 WM training meta-analyses split by population (TD children: Sala & Gobet 2017b; LD children, healthy adults, older adults: Melby-Lervåg et al. 2016 subsamples; older adults: Sala et al. 2018a) + 6 additional for other training types
- **Effect size metric**: Hedges' g (corrected SMD), pre-post between-group design
- **Publication bias methods**: PET-PEESE (primary), trim-and-fill as fallback when PET-PEESE produced implausible estimates
- **Active control definition**: Required engaging, cognitively demanding alternative activity (e.g., non-adaptive training, visual search); passive controls (watching videos, questionnaires) classified as non-active
- **Follow-up**: Post-training only; no follow-up data included (insufficient availability)
- **Statistical independence**: Enforced — no study appears in more than one first-order meta-analysis
- **Dependency correction**: Cheung & Chan's (2014) samplewise-adjusted correction; sensitivity analyses using Robust Variance Estimation (RVE)
- **Open data**: OSF project at https://osf.io/qk2vu/

## Limitations

- **No follow-up effects**: Only immediate post-training effects measured. Persistence of near-transfer effects (and whether they eventually generalize) is unknown.
- **No measurement error correction**: First-order meta-analyses did not correct for measurement error. Authors argue this is negligible for far-transfer (effects are already near zero) but could slightly affect near-transfer estimates.
- **Arbitrary corrected estimate selection**: Choosing between PET, PEESE, and trim-and-fill estimates involves judgment. Authors acknowledge this but argue consistency across methods validates the conclusions.
- **Limited primary studies in some cells**: Chess (k=9), exergames (k=11), and video games in older adults (k=10) have small k, reducing precision and power of publication bias analysis.
- **Brain-training programs excluded**: No eligible first-order meta-analysis on commercial brain-training (e.g., Lumosity) existed at time of publication. Simons et al. (2016) reached similar null conclusions for brain training separately.
- **No clinical/special populations**: Findings explicitly restricted to general populations. Authors note they cannot exclude benefits in, e.g., cancer survivors.
- **Only immediate outcomes**: Excluded follow-up data due to scarcity. Long-term effects on domain-specific performance remain an open question.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — children (TD and LD), adults, and older adults across multiple training program meta-analyses
- **Relevance to adult async video learners (Tyler's context)**: High. The "adults" subgroup in WM training (k=31, g=0.20 uncorrected, g=0.17 corrected) is directly applicable. More importantly, the core finding — that domain-specific learning does not generalize automatically — is highly relevant to instructional design for tutorial creators.
- **Red flags**: None for the core finding. The specific near-transfer effect sizes come from structured cognitive training programs, not tutorial watching, so direct effect size application is not appropriate. The theoretical implication (skills don't auto-transfer) is the relevant take.
- **Context flag**: Some of the near-transfer data comes from children (K-12 context). The far-transfer null finding, however, is consistent across all adult populations and is the guidebook-relevant claim.

## Conclusions

Authors conclude: "the lack of generalization of skills acquired by training is thus an invariant of human cognition." Near transfer is real and consistent but likely reflects improved task performance on structurally similar tasks rather than genuine cognitive enhancement. Far transfer is null across all training types and all populations once publication bias and placebo effects are removed. The practical implication: "professional and educational curricula should focus on domain-specific knowledge rather than general and allegedly transferable skills."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Common Elements Theory** (Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901)
  - Components: Transfer likelihood is a direct function of shared features between source and target domains
  - Research alignment: Explains why near transfer occurs (high feature overlap) and far transfer does not (insufficient overlap). Corroborated by this second-order meta-analysis.

## Extraction Notes

**Instructional design implication (Tyler's context)**: This paper is a strong constraint on tutorial design goals. Viewers who watch a Mac/AI tutorial will get better at the specific thing taught. They will NOT automatically transfer that skill to novel contexts unless the tutorial explicitly builds bridges (common elements, analogies, explicit transfer prompts). "Watching my tutorial makes you better at this topic" is well-supported. "Watching my tutorials makes you better at tech in general" is not supported.

**The placebo effect finding is practically significant**: The apparent benefits in poorly controlled cognitive training studies evaporate when a real active control is used. Tutorial creators should be skeptical of any claims that "learning X skill trains your brain for Y" — this is the cognitive training mythology this paper dismantles.

**Effect size caution**: The near-transfer g values (0.17–0.49 depending on population and correction) are from WM training programs — structured, repeated cognitive exercises, not passive video watching. Direct extrapolation of those numbers to tutorial learning is not valid. The theoretical conclusion is valid; the specific numbers are not.

**Paper quality**: High. Open data, sophisticated publication bias correction (PET-PEESE + trim-and-fill), active vs. passive control distinction, second-order meta-analytic method properly applied. Authors are transparent about limitations and the degree of analytical judgment involved. The core finding is robust across all sensitivity analyses.

**Convergent evidence note**: The authors note that even years of formal schooling produce only modest effects on general cognitive ability (Detterman, 2016; Ritchie et al., 2015). This makes the cognitive training literature's optimistic claims even less plausible.
