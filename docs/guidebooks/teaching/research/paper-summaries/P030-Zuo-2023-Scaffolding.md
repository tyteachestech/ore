# The Effects of Using Scaffolding in Online Learning: A Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Zuo, M., Kong, S., Ma, Y., Hu, Y., & Xiao, M. (2023)
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=83 effect sizes from 32 articles; total N across studies approximately 5,166 participants (summed from Table data)
- **Publication window**: Studies published 2011–2021
- **Journal**: Education Sciences (MDPI, open access)
- **DOI**: 10.3390/educsci13070705
- **Funded by**: Collaborative Innovation Center for Informatization and Balanced Development of K-12 Education, MOE China; Hubei Province — note K-12 emphasis in funder mission

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall effect**: Scaffolding significantly improves online learning performance: SMD=0.53 (95% CI [0.41, 0.66]), z=8.42, p<0.001. Classified as medium effect by Cohen's convention. High heterogeneity (I²=78%, Q=368.19, p<0.001) justifies moderator analysis.

- **Finding 2 — Scaffold type is a significant moderator** (Q(3)=9.41, p=0.02):
  - Conceptual scaffolding: d=0.85 (95% CI [0.59, 1.11]) — large effect, significant
  - Meta-cognitive scaffolding: d=0.49 (95% CI [0.30, 0.69]) — medium effect, significant
  - Procedural scaffolding: d=0.47 (95% CI [0.21, 0.72]) — medium effect, significant
  - Strategic scaffolding: d=0.26 (95% CI [−0.03, 0.55]) — non-significant (p=0.08)

- **Finding 3 — Learning outcome type is a significant moderator**:
  - Affective domain: d=0.67 (95% CI [0.29, 1.06]) — largest effect
  - Meta-cognitive domain: d=0.63 (95% CI [0.33, 0.92])
  - Cognitive domain: d=0.48 (95% CI [0.35, 0.61]) — smallest but still medium

- **Finding 4 — Learning mode**: Collaborative online learning shows larger scaffolding benefit (d=0.68, 95% CI [0.52, 0.85]) than individual online learning (d=0.49, 95% CI [0.33, 0.64]). Difference not statistically significant at group level (Q(1)=2.88, p=0.09).

- **Finding 5 — Grade level**: No significant difference across grade levels (Q(2)=0.29, p=0.86):
  - Elementary: d=0.45 (95% CI [0.09, 0.80])
  - Secondary: d=0.57 (95% CI [0.26, 0.87])
  - College: d=0.54 (95% CI [0.40, 0.67])

- **Finding 6 — Discipline**: No significant between-group difference (p=0.54), but descriptive ordering: chemistry (d=0.79) > mathematics (d=0.66) > computer science (d=0.65) > educational technology (d=0.61) > science (d=0.45) > language and literature (d=0.38). All disciplines show positive, significant effects.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — cognitive load reduction**: Scaffolding helps learners overcome disorientation and cognitive overload inherent in online learning, freeing capacity for actual learning.
- **Mechanism — autonomy support**: Scaffolding enables learners to fully utilize their autonomy within their Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD), achieving goals otherwise impossible.
- **Mechanism — self-regulation**: Interactive self-regulating scaffolding specifically shown to improve self-regulation level and initiative in online contexts.
- **Mechanism — collaborative coordination**: In group settings, scaffolding helps teams set goals, monitor processes, evaluate, and reflect — explaining the larger collaborative effect.

- **Moderator — scaffold type**: Statistically significant (p=0.02). Conceptual scaffolding (clarifying knowledge structure and logical connections) outperforms others substantially. Strategic scaffolding (task-completion alternatives) shows no significant effect — authors note this may reflect insufficient k=10 studies rather than true null.
- **Moderator — learning outcome domain**: Not formally tested for significance between groups, but affective outcomes (motivation, satisfaction) show consistently larger effects than cognitive knowledge outcomes.
- **Moderator — learning mode**: Collaborative > individual, though not significant at p=0.09. Directionally consistent with collaborative scaffolding theory.
- **Non-moderators**: Grade level (p=0.86) and discipline (p=0.54) do NOT significantly moderate the effect — scaffolding works broadly regardless of learner age or subject.

## Study Design Details

- **Study type**: Random effects meta-analysis (Review Manager 5.3)
- **Search**: 2011–2021, databases: Web of Science, ProQuest, ERIC, EBSCO, ScienceDirect, Springer, Google Scholar; plus manual search of top journals (Computers & Education, JCAL, ETRD, BJET)
- **Inclusion criteria**: (1) scaffolding vs. non-scaffolding in online learning, (2) 2011–2021, (3) English language, (4) experimental or quasi-experimental design, (5) reportable quantitative data (n, M, SD)
- **Exclusion**: 2 studies excluded for blended (not purely online) learning environment
- **Final corpus**: 32 articles, 83 effect sizes, from initial pool of 2,379 records
- **Coding reliability**: Two independent coders; discrepancies resolved by discussion until consistent scheme formed; 10% double-coded initially
- **Effect size metric**: SMD (standardized mean difference), Cohen's d conventions used
- **Publication bias assessment**: Funnel plot only — no Egger's test, no trim-and-fill reported

## Limitations

- **English-only corpus**: Excludes potentially significant non-English literature, especially given study origin (Chinese university); may over-represent WEIRD populations
- **Funnel plot only for bias**: No Egger's test or trim-and-fill analysis reported — publication bias assessment is weak
- **Strategic scaffolding underrepresented**: Only k=10 studies; non-significant result (p=0.08) may be underpowered rather than a true null effect
- **Sparse affective/metacognitive outcome data**: k=15 and k=9 respectively — small cells make those estimates less stable
- **No fading/fade-out analysis**: No data on whether scaffolding effects persist after removal of supports — critical for long-term instructional design
- **Quantitative designs only**: Cannot capture qualitative nuance of how scaffolding is experienced
- **K-12 funder orientation**: Research funded by a K-12 education ministry — corpus may skew toward K-12 contexts despite college dominance in grade-level data (k=50 of 83 effect sizes are college)
- **No intervention duration data**: No analysis of short vs. long scaffolding interventions — cannot assess whether effects are stable over time
- **Quasi-experimental designs included**: Variable allocation concealment quality; risk of confounding

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — elementary (k=12), secondary (k=21), college (k=50) learners across multiple disciplines; overwhelmingly in formal educational settings
- **Relevance to async video tutorial context (Tyler)**: Low-to-Medium. The "online learning" in this meta covers LMS-based scaffolding (quizzes, forums, prompts, knowledge outlines) in structured courses — not self-directed video consumption. The mechanism (guiding learners through challenging material, reducing disorientation) is directly relevant, but the delivery modality differs substantially.
- **Red flags**: No adult self-directed learner studies; no YouTube/creator platform studies; no informal learning contexts
- **Most applicable finding for Tyler's context**: Conceptual scaffolding (clarifying knowledge structures and logical connections) has the largest effect — directly maps to explanation design, visual anchors, concept maps, and "knowledge outline" moments in tutorials

> **MOOC/Higher-Ed Context Flag:** The majority of included studies (k=50/83) involve college/university-level online learning platforms. Closer to Tyler's audience than K-12, but still differs from self-initiated YouTube consumption — learners in this corpus are enrolled students with institutional accountability. Weight findings accordingly, particularly for affective/motivational outcomes.

> **K-12 Context Flag:** 33 of 83 effect sizes come from elementary and secondary school contexts. Elementary and secondary findings (d=0.45, d=0.57) are included in the overall pooled estimate. This slightly inflates apparent generalizability to adult learners.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that "scaffolding had a significant role in improving students' online learning performance with an overall effect size of 0.53." They identify discipline, learning outcome type, and scaffolding type as significant moderators. Practical recommendation: instructional designers should match scaffold type to activity — conceptual scaffolding when learners encounter new concepts; meta-cognitive and procedural scaffolding for structured task support; consider AI-driven adaptive scaffolding as a responsive, personalized implementation. The non-significant grade-level finding is highlighted as evidence that "instructional designers can set up scaffolding for online learning among students of different grade levels."

## Comparison with P029 (Doo et al. 2020)

P029 (Doo, M.Y., Bonk, C., & Heo, H., "A Meta-Analysis of Scaffolding Effects in Online Learning in Higher Education," *International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning*, 2020, 21, 60–80) is cited in this paper as reference [21].

**Overlaps:**
- Both are meta-analyses of scaffolding in online learning
- Both report significant positive overall effects
- Both analyze scaffold type and discipline as moderators

**Key differences:**
- **Scope**: Doo et al. restricted to higher education only; Zuo et al. includes K-12 through college
- **Effect size range**: Doo et al. reported effects ranging 0.660–1.072 (substantially higher than Zuo's 0.53 overall). This discrepancy likely reflects: (a) higher-ed focus in Doo (college effect in Zuo is 0.54, consistent), and (b) different inclusion criteria and study corpus
- **Time range**: Doo et al. appears to have an earlier search window; Zuo et al. covers 2011–2021
- **Moderators**: Doo et al. also found scaffold type, source of scaffolding, and disciplines as significant moderators — consistent with Zuo

**Conflict note**: The substantially higher effect size range in Doo (0.66–1.07) vs. Zuo's overall 0.53 should be flagged during Stage 2 conflict resolution. Zuo's estimate is more conservative and likely more reliable given broader k=83 corpus and more recent literature. **Use Zuo's 0.53 as the primary estimate for guidebook claims, with Doo as corroborating evidence that the effect is real but cite the more conservative figure.**

## Extraction Notes

**Usefulness for Tyler's guidebook:** Medium-high for the instructional design chapter, specifically around scaffolding as a concept for tutorial structure. The conceptual scaffolding finding (d=0.85) is the most actionable — it directly supports practices like knowledge outlines, concept maps, and explicit "here's how this fits together" moments in video tutorials.

**Caution on population transfer:** This entire corpus is formal educational scaffolding (embedded in LMS, delivered by teachers/instructional designers). Tyler's YouTube context is fundamentally different — learners are self-selected, self-paced, and unaccountable. The mechanism (reducing disorientation, guiding through ZPD) likely still applies, but the delivery modality and learner motivation are not equivalent. Frame findings as "scaffolding principles" rather than "scaffolding research proves YouTube tutorials should do X."

**Strategic scaffolding null finding:** The non-significant result for strategic scaffolding (d=0.26, p=0.08) is worth flagging — this type (providing alternative ways to complete tasks) is closest to what many tutorial creators intuitively do (showing multiple methods). The null may reflect underpowering rather than a true null, but worth noting.
