# The Curse of Knowledge in Reasoning About False Beliefs

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Birch, S.A.J. & Bloom, P., 2007
- **Study Type**: Experimental (between-subjects, single study)
- **Sample**: n=155 undergraduate students (69 male, 82 female, 4 unspecified), Yale University introductory psychology course
- **Design**: 3-condition between-subjects experiment using a modified false-belief displacement task
- **DOI**: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01909.x
- **PMID**: unknown

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Adults show curse-of-knowledge bias in false-belief reasoning (plausible condition)**: Adults who knew the violin had been moved to the red container (knowledge-plausible condition) assigned significantly higher probability to that container than adults who did not know (ignorance condition): 34% vs. 23%.
  - Effect size: d = 0.472, p_rep = .95 (t(105) = −2.42)
  - Companion effect: probability assigned to correct (false-belief) container dropped from 71% to 59%: d = 0.459, p_rep = .95 (t(105) = −2.35)

- **Finding 2 — Plausibility moderates the bias completely**: Adults who knew the outcome but could not construct a plausible rationale for the protagonist to act on that knowledge (knowledge-implausible condition) showed NO significant bias. Probability to the known container: 19% vs. 23% in ignorance condition (n.s., t(97) = −1.44). Probability to the correct false-belief container: 73% vs. 71% (n.s., t(97) = −0.21).
  - This was not merely a ceiling effect — plausibility was validated by the ignorance condition showing 23% for the plausible container vs. 2% for the implausible one.

- **Finding 3 — The bias is not simply about having any knowledge**: Knowing the outcome did not automatically produce bias. The bias required both (a) specific outcome knowledge AND (b) a plausible account for why the protagonist might look at the known location. Without the plausible account, knowledge had no measurable contaminating effect.

- **Finding 4 — Adult bias mirrors (but is smaller than) child bias**: This study establishes that the curse of knowledge is not unique to children with presumed conceptual deficits — it operates in fully competent adult reasoners. Authors note prior work (Birch & Bloom, 2003) showed 3- and 4-year-olds are MORE susceptible than 5-year-olds, and 5-year-olds more than adults, suggesting a developmental continuum rather than a qualitative threshold.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Knowledge-plausible vs. ignorance — probability assigned to known (false) container**: d = 0.472 (t(105) = −2.42, p_rep = .95)
- **Knowledge-plausible vs. ignorance — probability assigned to correct (false-belief) container**: d = 0.459 (t(105) = −2.35, p_rep = .95)
- **Knowledge-implausible vs. ignorance — probability assigned to known container**: n.s. (t(97) = −1.44, p_rep = .95)
- **Knowledge-implausible vs. ignorance — probability assigned to correct container**: n.s. (t(97) = −0.21, p_rep = .95)

**Note on p_rep**: This paper uses p_rep (probability of replication) rather than p-values — a now-deprecated APA reporting convention from 2005–2008. p_rep = .95 corresponds approximately to p < .05. Effect sizes (d) are directly interpretable.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Primary mechanism — knowledge contamination of perspective-taking**: Having specific outcome knowledge automatically biases probability estimates about what a naive person knows or will do. The knower cannot cleanly suppress their own knowledge state when modeling another's ignorant state. This is framed as a cognitive bias, not a conceptual deficit.

- **Moderator — plausibility of outcome**: The curse operates only when the knower can construct an implicit rationale for why the naive agent might coincidentally act in accord with the knower's knowledge. Without that rationale, the bias is suppressed. Interpretation: adults may partially resist the curse by testing their biased response against a "does this make sense?" check — and failing that check blocks the bias.

- **Developmental moderator (cross-study)**: The bias is stronger in younger children (3-4 year olds > 5-year-olds > adults), suggesting the mechanism persists across development but with improving (not perfect) suppression. This makes it a developmental continuum, not a conceptual stage transition.

- **Proposed additional moderators (discussion, not tested)**: Authors suggest plausibility is "likely only one of a number of ways subjects may fall prey" to the bias — implying other implicit rationales for biased responding could also activate the curse.

## Study Design Details

- **Task**: Modified Wimmer-Perner displacement task. Vicki puts violin in blue container, leaves. Sister moves violin (to red or purple, depending on condition), rearranges all four containers. Question: where will Vicki look?
- **Key departures from standard false-belief task**:
  1. Continuous probability ratings (not categorical yes/no) — more sensitive
  2. Four containers instead of two — enables plausibility manipulation
  3. Containers rearranged post-displacement — creates plausible vs. implausible landing sites
- **Control/Comparison**: Ignorance condition (n not specified per cell, total N=155 across 3 conditions, approximately ~50 per cell)
- **Follow-up**: Single session, no follow-up
- **Attrition**: Not reported
- **Blinding**: Not applicable (questionnaire study)

## Limitations

- **Student-only sample**: All participants were Yale undergraduates in introductory psychology. Convenience sample with high verbal ability. Generalizability to non-student adults is assumed but not tested.
- **Single experimental paradigm**: One task (violin/containers). Effect may be specific to this type of spatial displacement scenario. Does not directly test curse of knowledge in knowledge-sharing, teaching, or communication contexts.
- **No manipulation check for plausibility ratings in knowledge conditions**: Plausibility was validated in the ignorance condition only. We don't know whether participants in knowledge conditions actually experienced the plausibility manipulation as intended.
- **Small estimated cell sizes**: ~50 per condition. Adequate for detecting medium effects but underpowered for subgroup analyses or interaction terms.
- **p_rep reporting**: The paper uses the now-deprecated p_rep metric. Effect sizes are interpretable but p-values cannot be exactly recovered; approximate p < .05 for the significant comparisons.
- **Ecological validity gap for teaching contexts**: The task models a single-event displacement with complete ignorance. Expert-novice gaps in teaching involve accumulated, tacit knowledge — a more chronic and diffuse form of the bias. Transfer of these specific findings to instruction requires inference.
- **No direct measure of mechanism**: The plausibility account is compelling but the study does not measure what participants were actually doing cognitively (e.g., whether they were generating explicit rationales). The mechanism is inferred from the pattern of results.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (Yale, intro psych pool)
- **Relevance to async video teaching (Tyler's context)**: High — the curse of knowledge is directly relevant to expert explainers. Tyler's tech expertise creates exactly the conditions this study describes: specific knowledge that is hard to suppress when modeling a less-informed viewer.
- **Red flags**: Student sample, single US institution. Not a clinical population. No meaningful exclusion concerns for this guidebook.
- **K-12 flag**: Not applicable — adult sample throughout. The developmental comparison cited (Birch & Bloom, 2003) involves children, but P035 itself is an adult study.

## Conclusions

Authors conclude: "One's own knowledge can be a curse when reasoning about beliefs that differ from one's own." The bias is demonstrated in competent adult reasoners with no conceptual deficit, establishing that curse-of-knowledge effects are not a developmental artifact but a persistent feature of adult cognition. Critically, the bias is modulated by plausibility — "knowledge becomes a more potent curse when it can be combined with a rationale (even if only an implicit one) for inflating one's estimates of what others know."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Curse of Knowledge** (term from Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989; applied here to false-belief reasoning)
  - Components: (1) having specific outcome knowledge, (2) being unable to suppress that knowledge when modeling a naive perspective, (3) bias amplified when a plausible rationale for the biased response exists
  - Research alignment:
    - Plausibility moderation -> tested directly in this study
    - Developmental gradient -> established across Birch & Bloom (2003) + present study
    - Connection to hindsight bias -> theoretical link noted, not tested here

## Extraction Notes

This is a tight 5-page experimental paper — single study, three conditions, one task. It should be read as the adult-competence anchor for the curse-of-knowledge literature: it proves the bias is not just a children's phenomenon but persists in adults under the right conditions.

**Key implication for Tyler**: Tyler cannot simply "try harder to think like a beginner." The bias is automatic and operates even when the person is motivated to model a naive perspective. What this study adds beyond the generic "curse of knowledge" claim is the plausibility mechanism — the bias is worst when the expert can construct a seemingly reasonable story for why the naive learner might already know the thing. In tutorial contexts, this maps directly to the moment when a creator thinks "any reasonable person would have seen this" — that rationalization activates the curse.

**Companion paper**: Pair with P019 (Tullis, adult curse of knowledge in educational contexts) for a fuller picture. This paper provides the mechanistic and developmental grounding; Tullis provides the instructional application.

**Effect size note**: The ds here (~0.46–0.47) are medium effects in a single-session lab paradigm with a relatively transparent manipulation. Real-world effects in spontaneous teaching (where no one is prompting reflection) may be larger and more persistent.
