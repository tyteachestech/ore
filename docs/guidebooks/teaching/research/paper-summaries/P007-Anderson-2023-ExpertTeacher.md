# Building the Expert Teacher Prototype: A Metasummary of Teacher Expertise Studies in Primary and Secondary Education

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Anderson, J. & Taner, G., 2023
- **Study Type**: Systematic metasummary (qualitative synthesis using Sandelowski & Barroso methodology)
- **Sample**: 106 study-sets (reports), 1124 expert teachers, 16 countries, yearspan 1983–2021
- **Methodology breakdown**: 78% qualitative, 12% quantitative, 9% mixed methods
- **Level breakdown**: 29% primary/elementary, 62% secondary, 8% both levels
- **Subject breakdown**: 22% varied subjects, 24% maths, 9% science, 9% general elementary, 8% foreign/second language, 8% physical education
- **Geographic skew**: 59% USA, 8% China, 5% UK — predominantly higher/upper-middle income countries
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100485
- **Funding**: UK Economic and Social Research Council (ES/P000771/1)

> **K-12 context flag:** This study is exclusively K-12 classroom research (primary and secondary teachers). All 106 studies involve school-age learners in formal classroom settings. Transfer to adult async video / YouTube tutorial creation is plausible for certain principles (reflection, adaptive expertise, learner knowledge) but not established. Weight with appropriate reduction and translate deliberately.

## Key Findings

The paper presents findings across 6 domains. Agreement Count (AC) = number of independent study-reports where both coders agreed the theme was present. Threshold for inclusion in prototype: AC >= 5.

### Domain 1: Knowledge Base (5 of 16 themes met threshold)

- **PCK (Pedagogical Content Knowledge) well developed**: AC=17, ranked 1st at both primary and secondary. The single most-agreed-upon knowledge characteristic. Defined as knowing not just your subject but how to teach it — representations, examples, analogies, student misconceptions.
- **Extensive knowledge about learners (general and individual)**: AC=16, ranked 1st at both levels. Expert teachers know their learners' characteristics as a type AND as individuals (personal needs, challenges).
- **Extensive subject/content knowledge**: AC=13, ranked 1st primary / 3rd secondary.
- **Extensive, integrated knowledge base (wide range of topics)**: AC=11.
- **Extensive knowledge about curriculum**: AC=6.

### Domain 2: Cognitive Processes (6 of 14 themes met threshold)

- **High awareness of what's happening in class**: AC=12, ranked 1st at both levels. Constant monitoring of learner behaviour, progress, and need for support.
- **Extensive and automated cognitive processes/heuristics**: AC=8. Expert teachers have routinized many processes, freeing cognitive capacity for adaptive responses.
- **Primary concern with student learning/on-task behaviour**: AC=6.
- **Able to make informed decisions in class**: AC=6.
- **Regularly engages in progressive/experimental problem solving**: AC=6. Consistent with Bereiter & Scardamalia's "progressive problem solving" — learning from experimentation when confronted with the unexpected.
- **Able to predict potential problems**: AC=5.

### Domain 3: Beliefs (8 of 32 themes met threshold)

- **Relationships/rapport as important**: AC=9, ranked 4th primary / 1st secondary. The most prominent interpersonal belief.
- **Treating learners as individuals with diverse needs and backgrounds**: AC=7.
- **Belief in constructivism**: AC=7, ranked 1st primary / 6th secondary.
- **Sense of moral duty or mission towards learners**: AC=6.
- **Engaging learners as important**: AC=6, ranked 1st secondary.
- **Facilitating development as human beings/future citizens**: AC=5 (secondary only).
- **High expectations/setting high challenges for learners**: AC=5.
- **Accepting primary responsibility for learning**: AC=5.

### Domain 4: Personal Attributes (5 of 13 themes met threshold)

- **Passion for profession/work as teacher**: AC=12, ranked 4th primary / 1st secondary.
- **Care for/love their learners**: AC=12, ranked 1st primary / 4th secondary.
- **Positive self-image/self-confidence/self-efficacy/identity**: AC=12.
- **Strong desire to succeed/ambitious/motivated**: AC=8.
- **Resilience (and persistence)**: AC=8.

### Domain 5: Professionalism (10 of 18 themes met threshold)

- **Reflects extensively**: AC=21, ranked 1st at both levels. The most agreed-upon professionalism theme by a wide margin.
- **Continuous/lifelong learners/striving to improve**: AC=16, ranked 1st primary / 3rd secondary.
- **Helps colleagues as teacher educator (incl. mentoring, informal peer support)**: AC=16.
- **Dedicated/hard working/committed**: AC=14.
- **Collaboration, PLCs, CoPs important**: AC=13.
- **Reflects critically** (self-questioning, problematising practice): AC=13, ranked 6th primary / 2nd secondary.
- **Leaders (school and/or wider)**: AC=10.
- **Interest in CPD/INSET/in-service qualifications**: AC=6.
- **Share resources/ideas with colleagues regularly**: AC=6.
- **Challenges self incl. through experiments, risks, innovation**: AC=6.

### Domain 6: Pedagogic Practice (39 of 89 themes met threshold — largest domain)

Top-ranked themes (AC >= 12):

- **Displays flexibility/improvises when teaching (adaptive expertise)**: AC=20, ranked 1st at both levels. Ability to respond appropriately to learning as it happens.
- **Engages learners through practices/content/activities/strategies**: AC=17.
- **Links learning to/builds on learners' lives and schemata**: AC=17, ranked 1st primary / 3rd secondary.
- **Scaffolds learning effectively**: AC=15.
- **Has clear routines and procedures**: AC=14 (especially primary, ranked 4th).
- **Continually assessing throughout lesson/dynamic assessment**: AC=13.
- **Considers learners' needs when planning**: AC=12.
- **Creates positive, supportive learning environments**: AC=12.
- **Makes regular use of collaborative/cooperative learning**: AC=12.

Notable lower-ranked themes relevant to this guidebook:
- **Develops HOTS (higher-order thinking skills, incl. creativity and critical thinking)**: AC=10.
- **Develops learners' study skills/autonomy/metacognition**: AC=9, ranked 4th primary / **35th secondary**. Authors note this as a notable gap — underdeveloped at both career stages relative to its theoretical importance.
- **Formative assessment is central to practice**: AC=8.
- **Reflects interactively** (while teaching): AC=8.
- **Monitors learners (circulating) during activities**: AC=9.

### Metacognition Gap — Specific Callout

The authors note the stark discrepancy in "Develops Ls' study skills/autonomy/metacognition" rankings (4th primary vs 35th secondary) as a notable finding. Combined with the broader absence of metacognitive instruction themes in the high-agreement findings, this suggests metacognitive instruction is consistently underdeveloped across all career stages, even among teachers identified as experts.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

This is a qualitative metasummary — no quantitative effect sizes are reported. The agreement count (AC) is the primary metric, functioning as a proxy for frequency/salience rather than effect size.

**Inter-rater reliability**: Cohen's kappa k = 0.440 (95% CI [0.418, 0.461]), p < .001. Authors describe this as "moderate agreement." Overall observation agreement was 90.5%.

**Threshold criterion**: AC >= 5 (both coders independently assigned the theme to the same report). 73 themes total met this threshold.

No effect sizes, no confidence intervals, no p-values for individual findings. Evidence of a particular feature's importance is purely frequency-based within the 106 study-sets.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Reflection enables adaptive expertise**: Expert teachers' flexibility/improvisation (AC=20) is attributed partly to their habitual interactive reflection — reflecting while teaching (AC=8), not just after. This automaticity is what makes adaptation possible without overwhelming cognitive load.
- **Learner knowledge enables prediction**: High awareness of class state (AC=12) is linked to extensive individual learner knowledge (AC=16) — knowing individuals allows prediction of problems before they escalate.
- **PCK integration**: Expert knowledge is described as "integrated" — subject knowledge, pedagogical knowledge, and learner knowledge are fused, not siloed.
- **Structure enables flexibility**: Paradox highlighted — ETs have both clear routines/procedures (AC=14) AND high flexibility (AC=20). Structure is the foundation that makes improvisation safe.
- **Primary vs. secondary differences**: Interpersonal/affective themes rank noticeably higher at secondary level (building meaningful relationships, cultivating mutual respect/trust, sensitivity to emotional environment, making lessons enjoyable). Metacognition themes, adapting core curriculum materials, and peer tutoring rank higher at primary.
- **Reflection is more prominent at secondary**: Reflection AC=16 secondary vs AC=4 primary. Authors attribute this to differences in professional culture and research focus rather than actual practice differences.

## Study Design Details

- **Methodology**: Sandelowski & Barroso (2007) metasummary adapted with two additional criteria: independent agreement criterion and threshold criterion.
- **Coding**: Two-stage. Stage 1: first author developed 182 coding themes inductively. Stage 2: both authors independently coded all 106 reports, generating agreement counts.
- **Quality filtering**: Metasummary intentionally does NOT pre-filter by quality — includes all qualifying studies. Quality signal comes from higher agreement counts (more studies = more reliable).
- **Expert identification**: Studies required (a) sufficient experience (nominally 5+ years) AND (b) some attempt beyond experience alone to justify expert status (social recognition, performance criteria, professional group membership, awards).
- **Exclusions**: Studies of tertiary/adult teachers were excluded at Stage 2 screening (n=443 excluded total).

## Limitations

- **Exclusively K-12**: All 106 studies involve school teachers at primary or secondary levels. Authors explicitly excluded tertiary/adult education studies. No findings apply directly to async video instruction or creator contexts.
- **Geographic and income skew**: 59% USA studies. No African or South American contexts met inclusion criteria. Only 3 studies from lower-income countries. Prototype reflects higher/upper-middle income country expertise.
- **Moderate inter-rater reliability**: Kappa = 0.44 (moderate). The high overall agreement (90.5%) is partially an artifact of the large number of absent codes per report — the rarer a theme, the easier it is to agree it's absent.
- **Agreement count threshold is arbitrary**: AC=5 is carefully chosen but not theoretically derived. Authors acknowledge themes with AC=4 may be real and important.
- **Qualitative majority**: 78% of included studies are qualitative (mostly case studies). Findings reflect what researchers chose to investigate and what teachers are able to articulate, not necessarily objective behavioral frequencies.
- **Only two coders, one institution**: Coding framework generated primarily by one researcher (first author). Frame reflects their disciplinary background (applied linguistics).
- **Non-expert comparison absent**: The prototype describes expert teachers but does not systematically compare with novices or average teachers — making it difficult to determine which features are truly distinctive to expertise vs. general teacher characteristics.
- **No causal claims possible**: Metasummary establishes frequently co-occurring features of teachers called "experts" — not mechanisms of how expertise develops.
- **Subject-specific ET characteristics not captured**: General (non-subject-specific) characteristics only. Subject-specific PCK findings (e.g., what expert maths teachers do differently from expert science teachers) are underdeveloped due to sample size constraints.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: K-12 school teachers (primary and secondary), predominantly USA-based.
- **Relevance to Tyler's context (YouTube tutorial creator, adult async video)**: Low-to-Medium. The fundamental classroom context (live, interactive, relationship-based, captive audience, formal curriculum) differs substantially from async one-directional video instruction.
- **What transfers**: The principles around reflection, continuous learning, adaptive expertise, learner knowledge, PCK (knowing your audience's mental models), and linking new content to existing schemata have strong theoretical grounding in cognitive science and likely transfer across teaching contexts.
- **What does NOT transfer**: Interpersonal relationship-building strategies (ACsustained over months), classroom management, collaborative learning activities, peer tutoring, dynamic assessment, formal routines/procedures, scaffolding in the interactive sense — all assume a live, relational, repeated-contact context.
- **Red flags for scope**: K-12 teachers have captive audiences, institutional authority, weeks or months of relationship context, and can monitor comprehension in real time. Tyler has none of these.

## Conclusions

Authors' conclusion (paraphrased): The expert teacher prototype that emerges from the 106 studies is primarily but not wholly "learner-centred." Key consistent findings are the importance of PCK, extensive learner knowledge, reflection, improvisational/adaptive performance, and strong interpersonal relationships. The prototype is offered as a "family resemblance sketch" — a set of frequently co-occurring features, not a checklist of necessary and sufficient criteria. Expert teachers are not all alike and none are likely to exhibit ALL features.

Direct quote on reflection: "ETs were frequently found to reflect extensively (AC = 21) and critically (including, for example, their willingness to problematise or question their practice) (AC = 13), with several reports that they also challenged themselves through progressive problem solving" (p. 10).

Direct quote on prototype nature: "this prototype should not be seen as a list of necessary and sufficient criteria for expertise, but only as a list of frequently shared features – the 'family resemblances' of expert teachers" (p. 13).

## Named Frameworks (Referenced)

- **Expert Teacher Prototype** (Sternberg & Horvath, 1995): Family resemblance model. This paper operationalises it empirically for the first time at K-12 level.
- **Adaptive Expertise** (Hatano & Inagaki, 1986; Crawford, 2007): Expert teachers learn from experimentation and improvise appropriately — contrasted with "routine expertise."
- **Progressive Problem Solving** (Bereiter & Scardamalia, 1993): Experts engage with genuine challenges at the edge of their competence rather than routinizing everything.
- **PCK** (Shulman, 1987): Pedagogical Content Knowledge — subject + how to teach it — confirmed as core to ET cognition.
- **Reflection-in-Action** (Schon, 1983): Interactive reflection while teaching, enabling adaptive responses.

## Extraction Notes

**For the Teaching Guidebook, this paper is most useful for the Deliberate Practice / Expert Development chapter** (Appendix C — Deliberate Practice System) and potentially a chapter on "What Expert Instructors Know That Beginners Don't."

**The K-12 flag must be heavy and explicit** in any chapter use. The population mismatch is fundamental — these are classroom teachers with captive audiences, institutional relationships, and live interaction. Tyler teaches via async YouTube to voluntary adult viewers who can click away at any moment.

**The most transferable insights for Tyler:**

1. **Reflection is the #1 professionalism marker** (AC=21). Self-review after publishing videos, structured critique of past tutorials, and questioning one's own explanatory choices are the expert behaviours most likely to drive improvement. This directly supports the deliberate practice chapter.

2. **Learner knowledge matters enormously** (AC=16). Knowing your audience's mental models, prior knowledge, and individual variation is what enables expert explanation. For Tyler: knowing the 50th-70th percentile tech user deeply enough to predict where they'll get stuck.

3. **PCK framing**: The concept of PCK — knowing not just your subject but how to teach it, including the right analogies, representations, and common misconceptions — is highly relevant to Tyler's context even though it comes from formal education. This is the bridge between subject expertise (Mac/AI) and teaching expertise.

4. **Adaptive expertise over routine expertise**: The flexibility/improvisation theme (AC=20) translates to Tyler's context as: don't script everything to death, develop the fluency to adjust an explanation mid-video when you sense it isn't landing, rather than just executing a pre-planned script.

5. **Metacognitive instruction is underdeveloped even among experts**: The low ranking of "develops learners' study skills/autonomy/metacognition" (35th at secondary) signals this is a genuine gap in practice. For a guidebook trying to help Tyler make his viewers better learners, this finding is a useful prompt — it's something even expert teachers don't do well, and it may be something async video can actually do better (explicit metacognitive prompts, self-testing prompts built into video structure).

**What to ignore from this paper**: Classroom management, interpersonal relationship strategies, cooperative learning structures, peer tutoring, dynamic in-class assessment, formal routines/procedures, and all findings that assume live repeated contact with a known group of learners.

**Priority score context**: The 4.5 priority score (H=2 heavy) reflects domain fit for deliberate practice, but the population mismatch substantially limits direct applicability. Use as conceptual scaffolding for the expert development chapter, not as primary evidence for any specific recommendation Tyler should follow.
