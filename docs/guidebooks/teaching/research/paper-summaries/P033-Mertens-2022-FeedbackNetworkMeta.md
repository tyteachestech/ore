# Effects of Computer-Based Feedback on Lower- and Higher-Order Learning Outcomes: A Network Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Mertens, U., Finn, B., & Lindner, M. A. (2022)
- **Study Type**: Network meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=77 studies, k=97 independent samples, m=163 effect sizes total (m=115 lower-order, m=48 higher-order); mean N=89 per study (range 15–452); 58% undergraduate students, 21% middle school children; studies from 10 countries, published 1968–2021
- **DOI**: 10.1037/edu0000764
- **Journal**: Journal of Educational Psychology, Vol. 114, No. 8, 1743–1772

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — EF is the top-ranked feedback type for both outcome categories**: Elaborated Feedback produced large effects for lower-order learning (g=0.71, p<.001) and medium effects for higher-order learning (g=0.46, p<.001) vs. no feedback. P-score (probability of being best) = .98 for lower-order, .89 for higher-order.

- **Finding 2 — AUC is second-ranked for both outcomes**: Answer-Until-Correct produced medium effects for lower-order (g=0.55, p<.001) and medium effects for higher-order (g=0.41, p=.039). P-score = .70 lower-order, .78 higher-order.

- **Finding 3 — KCR is third**: Knowledge of Correct Response showed medium lower-order effects (g=0.49, p<.001) and small, nonsignificant higher-order effects (g=0.22, p=.062). P-score = .57 lower-order, .47 higher-order.

- **Finding 4 — KR is last and weakest**: Knowledge of Results showed small lower-order effects (g=0.24, p=.026) and small, nonsignificant higher-order effects (g=0.14, p=.346). P-score = .25 lower-order, .31 higher-order.

- **Finding 5 — All feedback types beat no feedback for lower-order learning**: Every feedback type produced significant positive effects vs. no feedback condition for lower-order outcomes. For higher-order outcomes, only EF and AUC were statistically significant.

- **Finding 6 — Substantial heterogeneity throughout**: Lower-order: tau^2=0.13, I^2=72.9%. Higher-order: tau^2=0.12, I^2=71.6%. Wide prediction intervals indicate individual study results vary considerably.

- **Finding 7 — Consistency assumption violations for some comparisons**: Direct vs. indirect evidence paths showed significant inconsistency for AUC vs. EF and AUC vs. KCR (lower-order, ps<.002); for higher-order, inconsistency for AUC vs. KCR, EF vs. KCR, EF vs. NoFB (ps<.006). Subgroup analyses were necessary.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Why EF works**: EF provides explanations, hints, or metacognitive scaffolding beyond correctness information. This helps learners encode new information, build conceptual knowledge, and reorganize existing knowledge networks — particularly valuable for learners who need more information to correct errors successfully.

- **Why AUC works (especially for higher-order)**: AUC activates learner's related semantic network by prompting active engagement with the problem. Repeated attempts may improve encoding of desired knowledge (mastery learning principle). The mechanism for higher-order effects: initial failure encourages rethinking prior responses, potentially promoting deeper processing.

- **Why KR underperforms**: KR does not provide sufficient information to modify domain knowledge or task strategy. It may help for simple recognition tasks but cannot support error correction in ways that transfer.

- **Prior knowledge moderates differently by outcome**:
  - Lower-order: Both low and high prior knowledge learners benefit from all feedback types (EF consistently largest)
  - Higher-order: Low prior knowledge learners need EF or AUC for significant effects; high prior knowledge learners benefit from KCR and EF but not KR or AUC

- **Sample source moderator**: Feedback slightly more effective for undergraduates/adults than school children. Middle school children did not significantly benefit from any feedback type on higher-order outcomes. EF was the only feedback type with positive lower-order effects for middle school.

- **Test format moderator (lower-order)**: Constructed response items amplify EF effects dramatically (g=1.11 vs. g=0.55 for MC). KCR and KR are equally effective with constructed response but not MC. Generation effect may explain this: open-ended responding requires more effortful memory encoding.

- **Learning domain moderator**: No single feedback type dominates across all domains. EF shows large effects for general knowledge and constructed-response mathematics; AUC and KCR show large effects for language learning; science shows a different pattern favoring AUC.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Computer-based feedback of four types — KR (right/wrong only), KCR (correct answer provided), EF (explanation, hints, metacognitive information, or worked examples), AUC (multiple attempts until correct). Control = no feedback.
- **Control/Comparison**: Network meta-analysis uses both direct evidence (head-to-head comparisons) and indirect evidence (inferring relative effects from common comparators). NoFB is reference condition.
- **Typical study design**: Pretest (optional) → Learning phase with feedback → Posttest without feedback. Most studies used between-subjects designs.
- **Study settings**: Controlled lab (k=49 schools, k=39 of 47 undergraduate studies); online (k=5); homework (k=1)
- **Interrater reliability**: Cohen's kappa ≥.97 for feedback type coding; κ≥.95 for outcome coding; κ≥.76 for subgroup classifiers
- **Publication period**: 1968–2021 (literature search updated August 2021)
- **Study not preregistered**

## Limitations

- **Scope limited to four feedback types**: Intelligent tutoring feedback, scaffolding, concept maps, and person-delivered feedback were excluded. Results cannot generalize beyond KR, KCR, EF, and AUC.
- **Immediate feedback only**: Exact timing was often unreported in primary studies; database limited to effects of immediate computer-based feedback. Delayed feedback effects may differ, especially for higher-order learning.
- **Consistency assumption violations**: Direct and indirect evidence inconsistent for several comparisons (AUC vs. EF, AUC vs. KCR lower-order; multiple comparisons higher-order). Network estimates for those comparisons should be interpreted with caution.
- **High heterogeneity**: I^2 ~72% for both outcomes. Prediction intervals are wide — individual study effects vary substantially. Positive treatment effects "may not be robust in every context."
- **Publication bias signals**: Funnel plot asymmetry and Egger's test significant for lower-order (p<.001) and higher-order (p=.016) outcomes, suggesting small-study bias. Studies producing large effect sizes for KCR vs. EF or EF vs. AUC comparisons may be overrepresented.
- **23% of studies flagged for potential reporting bias**: Selection of reported results dimension of RoB2 raised concerns for nearly a quarter of included studies (no preregistration common in educational research).
- **Thin subgroup data for some cells**: Primary school children (m=5), high school on higher-order (m=4), some domain × feedback combinations too sparse for reliable conclusions.
- **Motivational outcomes not captured**: Only posttest performance was analyzed; feedback effects on motivation, confidence, and metacognition are not addressed.
- **No far-transfer outcomes**: Higher-order outcomes represent near-to-middle transfer only; the database did not include far transfer tasks.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — 58% undergraduates, 21% middle school children, remainder adults and high school. All educational settings.
- **Relevance to Tyler's context (adult async video)**: Medium-High. The adult and undergraduate findings are directly relevant to Tyler's audience (adult knowledge workers). The computerized feedback context maps reasonably well to quiz/practice elements in async learning. K-12 findings are flagged below.
- **Red flags**: A substantial portion of the database involves school-age learners. Findings from middle school and primary school subgroups should not be directly applied to Tyler's adult audience.

> **K-12 context flag:** Many included studies used school-age populations (K-12 ~21% of sample). The overall network estimates mix these with undergraduate/adult data. For Tyler's context, prioritize the undergraduate and adult subgroup findings from Table 4 over the overall network estimates.

## Conclusions

"Our results consistently indicate that EF feedback was, in general, the most effective feedback type for improving both lower- and higher-order learning outcomes. AUC and KCR feedback were somewhat effective in terms of enhancing learning outcomes, whereas simple KR feedback had little benefit for learners." The authors recommend EF or AUC when the goal is higher-order learning, and note that KR feedback "cannot be recommended on the basis of the current findings" for transfer tasks. For lower-order learning where EF is too resource-intensive to implement, KCR and AUC are reasonable alternatives, particularly for undergraduate students.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Bloom's Taxonomy / Revised Taxonomy / Webb's Depth of Knowledge**: Used to classify lower-order (recall, recognition, comprehension) vs. higher-order (application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation, transfer) outcomes. All three taxonomies collapsed into binary lower/higher classification for empirical tractability.
  - Lower-order -> remember, understand, recall and reproduction
  - Higher-order -> apply, analyze, evaluate, create, strategic/extended thinking

## Extraction Notes

This is the most directly actionable feedback paper in the set for Tyler's purposes. The network meta-analytic design is a genuine methodological advance over standard pairwise meta-analyses — it allows simultaneous ranking of all four feedback types using both direct and indirect evidence, which prior papers could not do.

**Key practical signal for Tyler**: EF > AUC ≈ KCR >> KR. This is a clear ranking. For async video, the relevant question is: when Tyler includes a practice question or check-for-understanding moment, what form should the feedback take? The data strongly favor explanatory/elaborated responses over simple right/wrong.

**Triangulation with other papers in set**:
- Wisniewski et al. (P001): overall feedback d=0.48/0.99 (task/learning distinction). Compatible — this paper adds the type-by-outcome decomposition.
- Brummer et al. (P009): g=0.41 for written corrective feedback (language domain). The language subgroup here shows KCR, AUC, and EF all effective for lower-order.
- Van der Kleij et al. (P006): d=0.49. Their earlier pairwise analysis is superseded by this network approach, which includes an updated database with 61% new studies.

**Caution on high heterogeneity**: The I^2 ~72% means the average effect sizes are real but the variance around them is large. EF's effect on any given platform/topic/population could land anywhere in wide prediction intervals (lower-order: [-0.03, 1.45]; higher-order: [-0.29, 1.21]). The EF consistency advantage — robust across most subgroups — is arguably more important than the point estimates.

**Publication bias**: Both funnel plots and Egger's tests indicate asymmetry. Small studies with large effect sizes for KCR vs. EF comparisons may inflate the apparent gap. Weight-function model did not find a significant difference between adjusted and unadjusted models (ps=.801/.988), providing some reassurance, though this method cannot model the network structure.
