# The Power of Feedback Revisited: A Meta-Analysis of Educational Feedback Research

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020)
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis of primary studies (NOT a meta-synthesis; primary studies coded directly)
- **Sample**: k=435 studies, 994 effect sizes, N>61,000 participants
- **DOI**: 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087
- **Source**: Meta-analyses searched: 32 existing meta-analyses from Visible Learning research lineage; final set drawn from primary studies within those

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall effect is medium but deeply misleading on its own**: After outlier removal (35 extreme values excluded), the average weighted effect of feedback on student learning is d=0.48 (95% CI [0.44, 0.51]). However, I^2=83.40% (post-outlier-removal), meaning ~83% of variance is true heterogeneity, not sampling error. A single "feedback works" claim is not defensible without specifying feedback type.

- **Finding 2 — Feedback type is the dominant moderator**: The type of feedback explains the most variance. The hierarchy from Table 3:
  - High-information feedback: d=0.99 (95% CI [0.82, 1.15]), k=42
  - Corrective feedback: d=0.46 (95% CI [0.39, 0.55]), k=238
  - Reinforcement or punishment: d=0.24 (95% CI [0.06, 0.43]), k=39
  - High-information feedback is ~4x more effective than reinforcement/punishment and ~2x more effective than corrective feedback.

- **Finding 3 — High-information feedback defined**: Feedback classified as high-information when it contained (a) task-level information, (b) knowledge of performance, AND (c) information on self-regulation — monitoring attention, emotions, or motivation during learning. This is the full Hattie & Timperley feed-up/feed-back/feed-forward model operationalized.

- **Finding 4 — Outcome type matters**: Feedback effects differ by what is being measured:
  - Cognitive outcomes (achievement, retention, test performance): d=0.51 (95% CI [0.46, 0.55]), k=597
  - Motivational outcomes (intrinsic motivation, locus of control, self-efficacy, persistence): d=0.33 (95% CI [0.23, 0.42]), k=109
  - Physical/motor outcomes: d=0.63 (95% CI [0.34, 0.92]), k=19
  - Behavioral outcomes: d=0.48 (95% CI [-0.09, 1.06]), k=30 (CI crosses zero — non-significant)

- **Finding 5 — Feedback direction**: Who gives feedback to whom matters:
  - Teacher to student: d=0.47 (95% CI [0.43, 0.51]), k=812
  - Student to teacher: d=0.35 (95% CI [0.13, 0.56]), k=27
  - Student to student (peer feedback): d=0.85 (95% CI [0.59, 1.11]), k=16
  - The peer feedback finding is the largest but based on only 8 studies — treat with caution.

- **Finding 6 — Channel does not matter significantly**: Feedback channel (oral, written, video/audio, computer-assisted) was NOT a statistically significant moderator (Q_B=5.12, df=2, p>0.05). The delivery medium is less important than what the feedback contains.

- **Finding 7 — Research design inflates estimates**: Pre-post designs produce d=0.63 (95% CI [0.56, 0.69]) vs. controlled designs d=0.42 (95% CI [0.37, 0.46]). The more rigorous controlled design estimate is the more trustworthy number.

- **Finding 8 — Motivational feedback can backfire**: 21% of motivational outcome effect sizes were negative. 86% of those negative effects came from uninformative feedback (rewards, punishments). Consistent with self-determination theory — controlling feedback reduces autonomy and self-efficacy. Feedback that is low-information and evaluative can harm motivation.

**Comparison with Visible Learning synthesis:**
- This meta-analysis: d=0.48
- Hattie (2009) synthesis: d=0.70
- Hattie & Timperley (2007) synthesis: d=0.79
- The large gap is attributed to (1) exclusion of duplets (same studies counted multiple times in syntheses), (2) use of random-effects vs. fixed-effects model (which inflates in synthesis), (3) different study inclusion standards.

**Publication bias:**
- Egger's test: z=9.52, p<0.001 for journal articles (asymmetric funnel) — publication bias detected in journal articles
- Dissertations: z=1.03, p=0.30 — no asymmetry
- Normal-quantile plot is markedly non-linear, indicating data does not come from a normal distribution — extreme values are real

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Information content is the active ingredient**: The defining feature of effective feedback is the amount and type of information it contains. High-information feedback (task + performance + self-regulation cues) produces nearly 4x the effect of simple reinforcement. The mechanism is that richer feedback helps learners reject erroneous hypotheses, build better information-processing strategies, and understand what to do next.

- **Autonomy-undermining as negative mechanism**: Controlling feedback (rewards, punishments) can reduce intrinsic motivation by undermining autonomy and self-efficacy (Ryan & Deci, 2000). This explains why 86% of negative motivational effects come from uninformative/controlling feedback forms.

- **Task complexity and proficiency level**: Referenced from Hattie & Timperley (2007) — task-level feedback is where most teacher feedback lands, but the most growth happens when feedback addresses process and self-regulation. However, this moderator was not directly tested in the current study's coding scheme.

- **Cognitive vs. motivational outcomes separate**: The paper suggests that feedback has different mechanisms for cognitive vs. motivational outcomes. What works for learning achievement does not equally work for boosting motivation — and can actively harm it if the form is controlling.

- **Feedback channel non-significant**: Whether feedback is oral, written, or computer-delivered does not significantly moderate effectiveness — the modality is not the mechanism.

## Study Design Details

- **Procedure**: Searched 32 existing meta-analyses from the Visible Learning research corpus. Identified 732 primary studies, removed 118 duplets (a key methodological contribution), applied inclusion criteria, reviewed 129 full texts, yielded 435 final studies with 994 effect sizes.
- **Inclusion criteria**: Required (a) empirical comparison of feedback intervention vs. control, (b) sufficient statistics to compute effect sizes, (c) student learning outcome (achievement, motivation, behavior), (d) identifiable educational context (kindergarten through university).
- **Model**: Random-effects model throughout (Hedges & Vevea, 1998), using RVE (robust variance estimation) for non-independent effect sizes within studies.
- **Coding reliability**: Krippendorff's alpha 0.81–0.99 across 6 moderator variables.
- **Outlier removal**: 35 extreme values (3.5% of total) removed via standardized residuals >2. This reduced overall d from 0.55 to 0.48.
- **Study period**: Median publication year 1985. Only 15% of effects from last 15 years — heavy skew toward older studies.
- **Populations sampled**: Kindergarten through university; not limited to K-12. Includes higher education and adult learners.

## Limitations

- **Severe heterogeneity remains**: Even after moderator analysis, heterogeneity within subgroups stays very high (I^2 80–89%). Moderators explain between-group variance but leave within-group variance largely unexplained. The average d within any category is still an imprecise guide.
- **Old study base**: Median publication year 1985. Digital learning environments, async video, and online tutorials are essentially absent from this dataset. Technology-mediated feedback in creator/YouTube contexts is not represented.
- **Population ambiguity**: Studies span K-12 through university but the coding scheme does not separate these finely. K-12 vs. adult learner differences in feedback reception are unanalyzed.
- **Feedback type categories are coarse**: The three-level coding (reinforcement, corrective, high-information) loses nuance within each category. Corrective feedback in k=238 studies is a wide umbrella.
- **Pre-post inflation**: 56% of effects are from controlled studies (713/994), but 24% are pre-post (244 effects) — the latter inflate estimates.
- **No direct test of task complexity**: The Hattie & Timperley hypothesis that feedback effectiveness varies by cognitive complexity level of the task was not operationalized as a moderator.
- **Student-to-student feedback**: Only k=8 primary studies (16 effect sizes) — the d=0.85 estimate for peer feedback is high-variance and should not be over-interpreted.
- **Conflict of interest**: John Hattie is a co-author and the paper explicitly responds to the Visible Learning research (Hattie's own body of work). This creates an inherent tension — the paper both critiques and defends the Visible Learning framework.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — K-12, higher education, some adult learning; spans kindergarten through university professors
- **Relevance to async video tutorial context**: Medium. The findings on feedback TYPE and information content are highly transferable — the mechanism (information richness) is not context-dependent. However, in Tyler's context, feedback is asynchronous and indirect (comments, analytics, likes) rather than real-time instructional feedback. The direct applicability requires translation.
- **K-12 context flag**: The majority of studies in this corpus are classroom-based. The feedback direction finding (teacher > student) is almost entirely a K-12/classroom phenomenon. Effect sizes for k=812 teacher-to-student effects include a substantial K-12 proportion.

> **K-12 context flag:** The bulk of this meta-analysis draws from classroom settings (K-12 and higher ed), with teacher-to-student feedback being the dominant condition (k=812 of 994 effects). Transfer to adult async video creation is plausible for the mechanism-level findings (information richness as active ingredient) but the specific effect sizes should not be applied directly to Tyler's context.

- **Red flags**: None for exclusion. No clinical populations, no addiction/compulsive behavior overlap. Appropriate for guidebook inclusion with context flags applied.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that feedback is a complex, differentiated construct whose average effect (d=0.48) masks extreme heterogeneity. The central finding is that the information content of feedback — specifically whether it addresses task, process, AND self-regulation — is the primary determinant of effectiveness, with high-information feedback producing nearly double the effect of corrective feedback and 4x the effect of simple reinforcement. The authors explicitly argue that treating all feedback as equivalent, as some synthesis approaches do, is a methodological error that produces misleading averages. "Feedback must be recognized as a complex and differentiated construct that includes many different forms with, at times, quite different effects on student learning."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Hattie & Timperley (2007) Feedback Model**
  - Components: Feed-up (Where am I going?), Feed-back (How am I going?), Feed-forward (Where to next?) × four levels: task, process, self-regulation, self
  - Research alignment: High-information feedback in this paper operationalizes the full three-question model at task + process + self-regulation levels. The "self" level (praise/personal feedback) is associated with reinforcement/punishment category — lowest effectiveness.
  - Task-level: covered by corrective feedback (d=0.46)
  - Process + self-regulation level: defining features of high-information feedback (d=0.99)
  - Self/person level: associated with reinforcement/punishment (d=0.24)

## Extraction Notes

This is the most methodologically rigorous paper in the feedback domain for this guidebook. Several things make it especially valuable:

1. **The duplet problem**: The authors remove 118 duplicate studies that were counted multiple times across meta-analyses — this is why the Visible Learning d=0.79 collapses to d=0.48. This is a critical methodological point worth flagging explicitly in the guidebook: the "feedback is powerful" claim in pop-education literature is inflated by double-counting.

2. **For Tyler's context specifically**: The non-significance of feedback channel (oral vs. written vs. computer-assisted) is directly actionable — it means comment-section feedback, video response, community posts, and in-video prompts are roughly equivalent in delivery channel. What matters is information richness, not format.

3. **The motivational backfire finding** (86% of negative motivational effects from uninformative/controlling feedback) has implications for how Tyler thinks about comments and community management — empty validation ("great video!") may not build the kind of engaged learning community that actually improves viewer comprehension.

4. **Conflict of interest note**: Hattie is both a co-author and the primary target of the methodological critique (his own Visible Learning synthesis produced d=0.79 vs. this paper's d=0.48). The paper is credible in its methodology, but note this context when citing the Visible Learning comparison.

5. **The feedback type hierarchy is the most actionable takeaway**: reinforcement (d=0.24) < corrective (d=0.46) < high-information (d=0.99). Translated to tutorial design: "Good job" < "That step was wrong, here's the right answer" < "Here's why that was wrong, what it tells you about your mental model, and how to approach similar problems differently."
