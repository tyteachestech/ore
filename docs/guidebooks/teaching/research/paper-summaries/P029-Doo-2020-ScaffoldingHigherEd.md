# A Meta-Analysis of Scaffolding Effects in Online Learning in Higher Education

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Doo, M.Y., Bonk, C.J., & Heo, H. (2020)
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis (random effects model)
- **Sample**: k=64 effect sizes from 18 journal articles; N=4,852 participants total (mean per study=71.82, range 31–158); age range 18–33.2 years, mean age 21.57 (SD=3.45)
- **Publication window**: 2010–2019
- **Countries represented**: US (42.2%), Canada (14.1%), Turkey (12.5%), Taiwan (10.9%), Germany (7.8%), Greece (6.25%), Netherlands (3.13%), South Korea (3.13%)
- **Journal**: International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning
- **DOI**: 10.19173/irrodl.v21i3.4638

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall effect**: Scaffolding in online higher education produces a large, statistically significant effect on learning outcomes: g=0.866 (95% CI [0.660, 1.072]), z=8.242, p<.001, N=64. Classified as large by Cohen's conventions (>.8). High heterogeneity (I²=91.0%, Q=699.991, p<.001) justified sub-group analysis.

- **Finding 2 — Meta-cognitive outcomes show the largest benefit** (Q(df=2)=16.493, p<.001):
  - Meta-cognitive domain: g=1.600 (95% CI [1.198, 2.001]), k=14, n=1184 — very large effect
  - Cognitive domain: g=0.652 (95% CI [0.415, 0.890]), k=41, n=2693 — medium effect
  - Affective domain: g=0.672 (95% CI [0.166, 1.179]), k=9, n=975 — medium-large effect
  - Authors interpret the large meta-cognitive finding as reflecting higher-ed students' greater engagement in self-regulation and higher-order thinking compared to K-12 learners.

- **Finding 3 — Scaffold type is a significant moderator** (Q(3)=11.584, p<.05):
  - Meta-cognitive scaffolding: g=1.104 (95% CI [0.864, 1.344]), k=39, n=2788 — very large
  - Conceptual scaffolding: g=0.964 (95% CI [0.287, 1.640]), k=5, n=338 — large
  - Procedural scaffolding: g=0.393 (95% CI [0.021, 0.766]), k=15, n=884 — small-medium
  - Strategic scaffolding: g=0.440 (95% CI [-0.235, 1.115]), k=5, n=210 — NON-SIGNIFICANT (p=.202)

- **Finding 4 — Scaffold source is a significant moderator** (Q(2)=7.979, p<.05):
  - Peer scaffolding: g=1.813 (95% CI [1.124, 2.503]), k=5, n=480 — very large (but interpret with caution: k=5 only)
  - Instructor scaffolding: g=0.837 (95% CI [0.433, 1.242]), k=15, n=677 — large
  - Computer/embedded scaffolding: g=0.764 (95% CI [0.526, 1.010]), k=44, n=3063 — medium-large
  - Note: Computer scaffolding was the most commonly studied source (k=44), reflecting prevalence of technology-mediated online learning. Authors explicitly note computer scaffolding approaching instructor-level effectiveness has significant implications for scalable online learning.

- **Finding 5 — Research design as moderator** (Q(2)=7.057, p<.05):
  - Experimental design: g=1.045 (95% CI [0.804, 1.286]), k=43, n=3511
  - Non-experimental design: g=0.702 (95% CI [-0.062, 1.467]), k=4, n=632 — not significant
  - Quasi-experimental design: g=0.422 (95% CI [0.023, 0.820]), k=17, n=709
  - The large gap between experimental and quasi-experimental results suggests design quality substantially inflates apparent effect sizes.

- **Finding 6 — Learning discipline as moderator** (Q(5)=32.995, p<.001):
  - Communications: g=1.905 (95% CI [1.475, 2.336]), k=10 — largest
  - Computing: g=1.135 (95% CI [0.634, 1.635]), k=8
  - Education: g=0.846 (95% CI [0.454, 1.238]), k=12
  - Science: g=0.485 (95% CI [0.076, 0.893]), k=11
  - Language and literature: g=0.478 (95% CI [0.118, 0.838]), k=16 — smallest significant effect
  - Other: g=0.516 (95% CI [-0.011, 1.043]), k=7

- **Finding 7 — Geographic location as moderator** (z_Diff=2.515, p<.05):
  - US studies: g=1.162 (95% CI [0.856, 1.468]), k=27, n=2341
  - Non-US studies: g=0.641 (95% CI [0.374, 0.908]), k=37, n=2511
  - US studies produced significantly larger effects. Authors note US studies also had larger sample sizes, which complicates interpretation.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — ZPD and higher-order cognition**: Scaffolding enables learners to exceed their current independent performance ceiling. In higher education, where tasks require more self-regulation and higher-order thinking, scaffolding's ceiling-extension effect is greater than in K-12. Authors explicitly state "benefits seem to increase as learners age."
- **Mechanism — meta-cognitive activation**: Meta-cognitive scaffolding prompts monitoring and reflection on the learning process itself. The very large meta-cognitive outcome effect (g=1.600) suggests this activates a feedback loop — scaffolding improves self-regulation, which then improves task performance.
- **Mechanism — social learning**: Peer scaffolding's very large effect (g=1.813) aligns with Vygotskian social learning theory — shared knowledge construction through intersubjectivity exceeds what individual or computer-delivered support can produce. However, k=5 limits confidence.
- **Moderator — scaffold type**: Statistically significant (p<.05). Meta-cognitive and conceptual outperform procedural; strategic is null. Critical distinction: meta-cognitive (monitor/reflect) and conceptual (identify themes/connections) types yield far stronger outcomes than procedural (use tools) or strategic (alternative task approaches).
- **Moderator — scaffold source**: Statistically significant (p<.05). Peer > instructor > computer, but the gap between instructor and computer is small (g=0.837 vs. g=0.764), and computer scaffolding has the largest evidence base (k=44).
- **Moderator — research design**: Experimental designs produce g=1.045 vs. quasi-experimental g=0.422 — a 2.5x difference. This is a major red flag for the inflated overall effect size.
- **Moderator — discipline**: Communications (g=1.905) and computing (g=1.135) benefit most from scaffolding; language/literature (g=0.478) and science (g=0.485) benefit least. Statistically significant between-group difference.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Scaffolding vs. no-scaffolding (control group) in online higher education settings
- **Control/Comparison**: Non-scaffolded online learning condition
- **Search period**: 2010–2019
- **Databases**: Academic Search Complete, Education Source (EBSCOhost), ERIC (ProQuest), PsycINFO, JSTOR, Google Scholar; plus manual journal search
- **Inclusion criteria**: (a) scaffolding in online higher ed, (b) English-only, (c) published 2010+, (d) undergraduate or graduate population, (e) quantitative outcomes sufficient for effect size calculation
- **Exclusion**: 2 studies excluded for "unstable learning environments"; 23 excluded for insufficient effect size data
- **Coding reliability**: Single coder (first author only) coded twice with 5-month interval; 4 errors corrected. No inter-coder reliability analysis possible.
- **Effect size metric**: Hedges' g (converted from Cohen's d to minimize small-sample bias)
- **Software**: Comprehensive Meta-Analysis 3.0 (Windows)
- **Scaffold duration**: Range 80 minutes to 10 weeks — very wide variance not analyzed as moderator
- **Distribution**: 60.9% meta-cognitive scaffolding; 23.5% procedural; 7.8% conceptual; 7.8% strategic (highly unequal cell sizes)

## Limitations

- **Single coder**: No inter-coder reliability — coding errors cannot be independently estimated. This is a significant methodological weakness.
- **Funnel plot only for bias**: Authors explicitly acknowledge "more studies with large effect sizes were included" and admit missing studies likely exist. No Egger's test or trim-and-fill analysis. Publication bias is likely.
- **English-only corpus**: Excludes non-English literature, potentially biasing toward US/Western findings (US studies show nearly double the effect of non-US studies).
- **Small cell sizes for several moderators**: Conceptual scaffolding k=5, strategic k=5, peer source k=5, non-experimental k=4. These estimates are statistically unstable.
- **Strategic scaffolding underpowered**: Non-significant result (p=.202) with k=5 — likely underpowered rather than a true null.
- **No fading/fade-out analysis**: No data on whether effects persist after scaffolding is removed — the core question for sustainable learning design.
- **No duration/dosage analysis**: Scaffolding periods ranged from 80 minutes to 10 weeks — no analysis of whether intervention length moderates effects.
- **Design confound**: Experimental designs (k=43) dominate and produce 2.5x larger effects than quasi-experimental designs, inflating the overall g=0.866 estimate.
- **Highly homogeneous discipline representation**: Language/literature (25%) and education (18.8%) represent nearly half the corpus — disciplines with lower effects — while communications (only 10 studies) produced the highest effects. Discipline distribution may distort the overall estimate.
- **Higher-ed only but very small sample sizes per study**: Mean n=71.82 per study is very small for online learning, which typically involves large class sizes. Lab-like conditions may inflate effects.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Undergraduate and graduate students (higher education), ages 18–33.2 years, across 8 countries. Formally enrolled in online courses.
- **Relevance to Tyler's async video tutorial context**: Low-to-Medium. This corpus covers scaffolding embedded in structured online courses (LMS-based prompts, instructor-provided supports, peer discussion scaffolds) — not self-directed consumption of YouTube tutorials. Mechanistic transfer is plausible (guiding attention, reducing cognitive disorientation, prompting reflection), but delivery modality and learner accountability differ fundamentally.
- **Strongest applicable finding for Tyler**: The meta-cognitive scaffolding result (g=1.104) suggests that prompting learners to monitor their own understanding — rather than just delivering content — produces the largest gains. In tutorial terms: pause-and-reflect moments, explicit "does this make sense?" checks, and "here's how you'd know if you understand this" prompts have strong evidence backing.
- **Red flags**: No informal/self-directed learning contexts. No YouTube or creator platform studies. All learners are institutionally enrolled with accountability structures (grades, deadlines). Effects likely attenuated in voluntary viewing contexts.

> **MOOC/Higher-Ed Context Flag:** All 64 effect sizes come from higher education online learning environments. This is the closest formal-education analog to Tyler's context, but important differences remain — enrolled students have institutional accountability, instructors, and structured deadlines that YouTube viewers do not. Weight findings for mechanism validity (what works) rather than effect size magnitude (how much).

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that "scaffolding in an online learning environment has a large and statistically significant effect on learning outcomes" (g=0.866). They identify scaffold type (meta-cognitive and conceptual strongest), scaffold source (peers > instructors > computers), and learning outcome type (meta-cognitive domain largest) as key moderators. The discussion emphasizes that scaffolding benefits increase with learner age and maturity, and that computer-delivered scaffolding approaching instructor-level effectiveness has major implications for scalable MOOCs and online learning. Authors recommend future research address timing and fading of scaffolding, and call for AI-based adaptive scaffolding as the next frontier.

## Comparison with P030 (Zuo et al. 2023)

Both are meta-analyses of scaffolding in online learning, with significant overlap in scope and conclusions.

**Key differences:**

| Dimension | P029 Doo (2020) | P030 Zuo (2023) |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Scope | Higher education only | K-12 through college |
| k | 64 effect sizes, 18 articles | 83 effect sizes, 32 articles |
| Overall effect | g=0.866 (large) | SMD=0.53 (medium) |
| Search window | 2010–2019 | 2011–2021 |
| Top scaffold type | Meta-cognitive (g=1.104) | Conceptual (d=0.85) |
| Strategic scaffolding | g=0.440, p=.202 (null) | d=0.26, p=.08 (null) |
| Peer scaffolding | g=1.813 (very large, k=5) | Not separately analyzed |
| Coder reliability | Single coder (weakness) | Two coders |
| Pub bias assessment | Funnel plot only (weak) | Funnel plot only (weak) |

**Conflict note on overall effect size**: Doo reports g=0.866 vs. Zuo's SMD=0.53 — a substantial gap. Likely explanations: (1) Doo's higher-ed-only restriction may select for stronger designs; (2) Doo's corpus is dominated by experimental designs (k=43/64) which produce g=1.045, while quasi-experimental designs yield only g=0.422; (3) Zuo covers K-12 populations where effects may be somewhat smaller (though grade-level differences are non-significant in Zuo). The discrepancy is also consistent with publication bias — Doo explicitly acknowledges this risk.

**Recommendation**: Use Zuo's SMD=0.53 as the primary conservative estimate for guidebook claims. Cite Doo for the higher-ed adult learner subgroup finding (college n=50 in Zuo: d=0.54, consistent with Doo's larger corpus) and for the metacognitive scaffolding dominance pattern, which is replicated across both metas. Do not cite Doo's g=0.866 overall without noting the design-quality confound and publication bias acknowledgment.

**Convergent signals across both metas:**
- Scaffolding reliably improves online learning outcomes (effect is real)
- Meta-cognitive and conceptual scaffolding consistently outperform procedural and strategic
- Strategic scaffolding is consistently null or near-null (both p>.05)
- Computer-delivered scaffolding is effective and scalable
- Higher-order/meta-cognitive outcomes benefit most

## Extraction Notes

**Usefulness for Tyler's guidebook**: Medium-high for the instructional design chapter. The meta-cognitive scaffolding dominance (g=1.104) is the most actionable finding — "monitor your understanding" prompts, reflection triggers, and "here's how you'd know if you got this" moments in tutorials have strong theoretical and empirical backing. The finding that scaffolding benefits increase with learner age directly applies to Tyler's adult audience.

**Inflation concern**: The g=0.866 overall estimate is likely inflated by (a) experimental design dominance, (b) publication bias (acknowledged by authors), and (c) English-only/US bias. The more conservative Zuo estimate of 0.53 should be preferred for any guidebook claim about "how much" scaffolding helps. The "what kind" findings (scaffold type moderators) are more robust than the overall magnitude.

**Single-coder weakness**: The coding reliability issue is a real methodological limitation. With only 18 articles and k=64, any systematic coding error would have outsized impact. Use with appropriate caution alongside Zuo's better-coded corpus.

**Most actionable scaffolding translation for Tyler's context:**
- Meta-cognitive scaffolding → prompt viewer self-assessment ("Can you explain this in your own words?"), explicit "pause and check" moments, metacognitive cues embedded in tutorial structure
- Conceptual scaffolding → knowledge outlines, "here's how the pieces fit together" visual summaries, concept maps, explicit framing of relationships between ideas
- Avoid over-investing in procedural scaffolding (g=0.393) or strategic scaffolding (null) — these yield weak or non-significant returns
