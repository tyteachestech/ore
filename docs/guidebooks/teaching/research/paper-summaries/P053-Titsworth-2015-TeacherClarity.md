# Two Meta-analyses Exploring the Relationship between Teacher Clarity and Student Learning

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Titsworth, S., Mazer, J.P., Goodboy, A.K., Bolkan, S., & Myers, S.A. (2015)
- **Study Type**: Two independent meta-analyses presented jointly (fixed-effects and random-effects approaches)
- **Sample**: Meta-Analysis 1: k = 144 effects, N = 73,281 (includes 92 effects from Fendick 1990 dissertation); Meta-Analysis 2: k = 46 studies, N = 13,501
- **Population**: Primarily college students; secondary school samples included in smaller proportions
- **Published in**: Communication Education, 2015
- **DOI**: 10.1080/03634523.2015.1041998

---

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall effect (Meta-Analysis 1)**: Teacher clarity has a positive average correlation with all student learning outcomes combined: r = .36, var. = .06, k = 144, N = 73,281. 95% CI [.33, .38]. Effect is heterogeneous (chi-square test significant). Clarity accounts for approximately 13% of the variance in student learning outcomes.

- **Finding 2 — Affective vs. cognitive split (Meta-Analysis 1)**: Clarity has a substantially larger effect on affective learning (r = .52, var. = .03, k = 25, N = 8,241; 95% CI [.48, .54]) than on cognitive learning (r = .34, var. = .06, k = 119, N = 65,040; 95% CI [.31, .36]). Neither effect was homogeneous.

- **Finding 3 — Meta-Analysis 2 random-effects results**: Affective learning: r = .53, 95% CI [.48, .58], k = 19, N = 6,889, p < .001. Cognitive learning: r = .46, 95% CI [.40, .51], k = 37, N = 6,612, p < .001. Both effects heterogeneous (affective: Q = 149.21, T2 = .02, I2 = 87.94%; cognitive: Q = 211.08, T2 = .03, I2 = 82.95%).

- **Finding 4 — Key moderator for affective learning**: Study design significantly moderated the clarity-affective learning relationship (QB = 18.84, p < .01). Survey designs: k = 15, r = .57; Experiments: k = 4, r = .32. This was the only significant moderator across all analyses.

- **Finding 5 — No significant moderators for cognitive learning**: Neither cognitive learning measurement type (perceived/self-report: r = .51, k = 10; achievement tests: r = .42, k = 27; QB = 2.69, p = .10), study design (survey: r = .51, k = 13; experiment: r = .42, k = 24; QB = 2.82, p = .09), clarity measurement (perceived: r = .52; observed: r = .41; QB = 3.52, p = .06), nor sample type (secondary: r = .38; college: r = .47; QB = 2.00, p = .16) significantly moderated cognitive learning effects.

- **Finding 6 — Practical significance (BESD)**: Students with high-clarity teachers score above the median on cognitive learning 67% of the time vs. 33% for low-clarity teachers (103% increase). For affective learning: 75.5% vs. 24.5% (208% increase).

- **Finding 7 — Publication bias**: Funnel plots generally symmetrical. Egger's test non-significant for both affective learning (intercept = 1.274, 95% CI [−3.050, 5.598], p = .27) and cognitive learning (intercept = −.065, 95% CI [−1.618, 1.487], p = .47). Rosenthal fail-safe N: 10,416 missing studies needed to nullify affective learning effect; 11,728 for cognitive learning. Publication bias not deemed a threat.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — Information processing**: Clarity helps students attend to, process, store, and retrieve information by reducing cognitive load during encoding.

- **Mechanism — Adaptive instruction**: Clarity is iterative, not one-shot. Teachers calibrate messages through questions, comments, and performance feedback; clarity is a communication process, not a single message property.

- **Mechanism — Affective-first pathway (proposed)**: One explanation for clarity's stronger affective effect: clarity first triggers an emotional/motivational response, which then drives engagement and learning. Zhang and Huang (2008) found that when motivation and affective learning were included in a structural equation model, the direct effect of clarity on cognitive learning became nonsignificant, suggesting full mediation through affect and motivation.

- **Moderator — Study design (significant for affective learning only)**: Survey/correlational studies show substantially higher clarity-affective learning relationships (r = .57) than experiments (r = .32). This gap is likely inflated by shared-method variance in survey designs — students rating both teacher clarity and their own affect using self-report scales.

- **Moderator — Immediacy x clarity interaction (additivity hypothesis, from prior literature)**: High immediacy + high clarity = best learning outcomes; low immediacy + low clarity = worst. No interaction effect — they work independently and additively.

- **Moderator — Test anxiety (Schonwetter et al., 1995)**: Clarity (specifically lecture organization) benefited low- and moderate-anxiety students but not high-anxiety students. Suggests clarity may work differently for students with different orientations toward learning.

- **Non-moderator — Sample type (secondary vs. college)**: No significant differences in the clarity-learning relationship between secondary school and college students.

- **Non-moderator — Culture**: Powell and Harville (1990) found clarity effects consistent across White, Latino, and Asian-American students. Zhang and Zhang (2005) found similar patterns in Chinese classrooms (r = .44 for clarity-affective learning).

## Study Design Details

- **Meta-Analysis 1 approach**: Fixed-effects (Hunter & Schmidt method); r as common metric; reliability corrections applied where data available; effects weighted by sample size
- **Meta-Analysis 2 approach**: Random-effects (Comprehensive Meta-Analysis 2.0); Fisher's z transformation; subgroup analyses for moderators
- **Clarity operationalization types in the dataset**: High-inference student perception scales (TCS, TCSI, Teacher Clarity Measure); experimental manipulation of specific behaviors (vagueness terms, mazes, organizational cues, uncertainty); cluster manipulations (clear vs. unclear teaching conditions)
- **Cognitive learning operationalization types**: Perceived/self-reported learning (Learning Loss Scale, Revised Learning Indicators Scale); performance-based tests (multiple choice, free recall, detail tests, note organization)
- **Key source of additional effects**: Fendick (1990) unpublished dissertation (meta-analysis of 1970–1986 education studies) contributed 92 effects to Meta-Analysis 1
- **Overlap**: The two meta-analyses used overlapping but non-identical inclusion criteria, which accounts for differences in effect sizes

## Conceptual Framework: Teacher Clarity Dimensions

The paper does not present Fendick's (1990) four components directly (those live in the dissertation). However, it synthesizes the preceding research programs into distinct operationalizable dimensions:

**Kennedy et al. (1978) four dimensions** (cross-validated, N = 1,263 students across three countries):
1. **Assesses student learning** — "tries to find out if we don't understand and then repeats things"
2. **Provides time to think** — "gives us a chance to think about what's being taught"
3. **Uses examples** — "works examples and explains them"
4. **Reviews and organizes** — "prepares us for what we will be doing next"

**Ohio State two-factor model** (Bush et al., 1977, N = 2,500+):
- Factor 1: Explaining concepts and directions at understandable pace
- Factor 2: Using examples and illustrations

**Smith and Land low-inference behaviors** (from ~15 experimental studies):
- Vagueness terms (negative): unclear word choices, hedging, imprecision
- Mazes (negative): false starts, redundant words
- Utterances (negative): vocalized pauses ("uh," "ah," "um")
- Bluffing (negative): recovery markers masking lack of knowledge
- Uncertainty terms (negative): signals of teacher lack of assurance

**High-inference measures** (communication studies tradition):
- TCSI (Chesebro & McCroskey, 1998): 10-item, single factor; most widely used in correlational research
- TCS (Powell & Harville, 1990): 15-item scale
- TCS expanded (Sidelinger & McCroskey, 1997): 22-item, oral + written clarity

**Critical distinction — inference level**:
- High-inference: global student impression ("my teacher is clear")
- Low-inference: specific observable behaviors (vagueness term frequency, presence of organizational cues)
- Authors argue for return to low-inference behavioral measurement as more trainable, more precise, and less susceptible to shared-method variance

## Limitations

- **Definitional inconsistency**: No unified definition of teacher clarity across the literature. Different studies operationalize radically different behaviors under the same label, making aggregation inherently noisy.
- **Persistent heterogeneity unexplained**: I2 values of 83–88% indicate the majority of variance is between-studies variation. No proposed moderator fully explains this. Unidentified moderators almost certainly exist.
- **Survey-experiment gap**: The large gap between survey (r = .57) and experimental (r = .32) effects for affective learning suggests significant shared-method variance inflation in survey designs.
- **Cognitive learning measurement problems**: The widely used Learning Loss Scale (Richmond et al., 1987) has poor reliability, does not factor analyze cleanly, shares little variance with performance tests, and conflates affective perception with cognitive measurement. Much of the clarity-cognitive learning literature rests on this questionable measure.
- **Positive publication bias risk**: Despite acceptable Egger's tests, the reliance on published sources may still inflate average effects. Unpublished studies are rarely indexed and seldom cited.
- **Dated experimental base**: Most quality experimental work is from the 1970s–1980s. Current communication research on clarity has been "stagnating" (authors' term). Generalizability to contemporary or online contexts is unknown.
- **Population skew**: Heavy reliance on college student convenience samples. Adult knowledge worker populations, online learners, or professional training contexts not represented.
- **Clarity measurement drift**: High-inference perceptual measures (TCSI) may capture general liking of the instructor as much as actual clarity behaviors, inflating correlations with affective outcomes.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Primarily college students; secondary school samples in a minority of included studies; adult professional training in one study (Faylor et al., 2008, adult learners in training environment)
- **Relevance to Tyler's YouTube tutorial context**: Medium. The effects are well-established in classroom settings (mostly synchronous, mostly higher ed). The underlying mechanisms (information processing, reduced cognitive load, examples, organization) are highly plausible for async video instruction. However, no studies from online or creator contexts are included.
- **Context flags**: K-12 context flag applies to secondary school-sourced effects; MOOC/higher-ed flag applies to the majority of included studies; no creator-economy studies present
- **Red flags**: None for exclusion. The affective learning effects in particular (engagement, motivation, satisfaction) are highly relevant to viewer experience in tutorial videos.

> **MOOC/higher-ed context flag:** The overwhelming majority of studies in both meta-analyses were conducted in college classrooms. The clarity-learning relationship is robust in that setting. Transfer to async YouTube tutorials is mechanistically plausible but empirically unverified — treat effect sizes as upper-bound estimates for Tyler's context.

---

## Conclusions

Teacher clarity has a consistent, moderate-to-strong positive relationship with both cognitive learning (r = .34–.46) and affective learning (r = .52–.53), with the affective effect being reliably larger across both meta-analyses. The only significant moderator found was study design for affective outcomes (survey > experiment), likely reflecting method inflation. Persistent heterogeneity across all subsets indicates that more specific, behaviorally precise clarity research is needed. The authors call for a return to low-inference behavioral measurement over high-inference perceptual scales, arguing the latter tell researchers little beyond "my instructor was clear" and cannot inform practical training.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Framework**: Kennedy et al. (1978) four-dimension model of teacher clarity
  - Components: (1) Assesses student learning, (2) Provides time to think, (3) Uses examples, (4) Reviews and organizes
  - Research alignment: Empirically derived from student reports and cross-validated; direct antecedent to Fendick's (1990) taxonomy

- **Framework**: High-inference vs. low-inference clarity measurement distinction
  - Low-inference: specific observable behaviors (vagueness terms, organizational cues) — countable, trainable, objective
  - Intermediate-inference: dimensions like "organization" or "explanation" — observable but require some interpretation
  - High-inference: global impression ("my teacher is clear") — ambiguous, difficult to train toward, susceptible to method variance
  - Authors' recommendation: researchers and practitioners should work from low-inference behavioral definitions

## Extraction Notes

This paper is highly actionable for the guidebook because:

1. The affective-vs-cognitive asymmetry is immediately useful. For a YouTube creator, "affective learning" maps closely to engagement, motivation to continue watching, and likelihood of return viewing — which are arguably more important than test performance in Tyler's context.

2. The Smith and Land vagueness variables (vagueness terms, mazes, utterances, uncertainty) provide a concrete, low-inference checklist that translates directly to video review practice. A creator could self-audit a recorded tutorial against these specific behaviors.

3. The survey-vs-experiment gap (r = .57 vs. r = .32 for affective outcomes) is a useful calibration note. Perceptual studies may overstate clarity's role because students who like a teacher rate them as clearer. The experimental r = .32 is probably a more conservative, honest baseline.

4. The "affective-first" mechanism (clarity triggers emotional response → motivation → learning) fits the YouTube engagement model extremely well. A viewer who finds a video clear and easy to follow is more likely to feel good about watching, subscribe, and continue learning — even before measurable knowledge gain occurs.

5. One unstated limitation relevant to Tyler: all studies involve some form of feedback loop or iterative interaction (students can ask questions, pause, review notes). Async video removes much of this — which could reduce clarity effects or shift which clarity behaviors matter most (e.g., examples and organization become more critical when there is no opportunity for clarifying questions).
