# Effects of Feedback in a Computer-Based Learning Environment on Students' Learning Outcomes: A Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Van der Kleij, Feskens, & Eggen, 2015
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=40 studies, 70 effect sizes; average study n=106.66 (SD=102.83); total participants ~4,253 estimated; studies published 1968–2012
- **Setting**: Majority university/college (n=32, k=56); some secondary (k=12), primary (k=2)
- **DOI**: 10.3102/0034654314564881
- **Journal**: Review of Educational Research

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — EF superiority overall**: Elaborated feedback produced a mean weighted ES'=0.49 (90% CI [0.36, 0.62], k=53, p=.00), compared to KCR ES'=0.33 (90% CI [0.02, 0.63], k=9, p=.07) and KR ES'=0.05 (90% CI [−0.28, 0.39], k=8, p=.79). Note: EF effects are underestimates because EF was typically contrasted with other feedback types (KCR or KR), not no-feedback baselines.
  - EF vs. no feedback: ES'=0.61 (90% CI [0.31, 0.91], k=9)
  - EF vs. KR: ES'=0.54 (90% CI [0.33, 0.74], k=21)
  - EF vs. KCR: ES'=0.39 (90% CI [0.20, 0.60], k=23)

- **Finding 2 — EF advantage amplifies at higher order learning outcomes**: EF effects on lower order learning outcomes: ES'=0.37 (90% CI [0.21, 0.53], k=31). EF effects on higher order and combined outcomes: ES'=0.67 (90% CI [0.41, 0.78], k=27, p=.00). The additional value of EF over KR or KCR is substantially larger for higher order outcomes than lower order outcomes.

- **Finding 3 — KR is ineffective**: KR mean weighted ES'=0.05 (90% CI [−0.28, 0.39], k=8, p=.79). On lower order outcomes: ES'=0.12 (90% CI [0.00, 0.24], k=7). On higher order outcomes: ES'=−0.01 (90% CI [−0.51, 0.49], k=1). KR is lower than hypothesized minimum of 0.20.

- **Finding 4 — KCR is modest**: KCR overall ES'=0.33 (90% CI [0.02, 0.63], p=.07, k=9). Lower order: ES'=0.31 (90% CI [−0.25, 0.87], k=5). Higher order: ES'=0.38 (90% CI [−0.23, 0.99], k=4). Wide CIs due to small k — results are inconclusive.

- **Finding 5 — Immediate feedback outperforms delayed**: Immediate feedback ES'=0.46 (90% CI [0.33, 0.59], k=58, p=.00); delayed feedback ES'=0.22 (90% CI [−0.05, 0.49], k=12, p=.19). Delayed feedback timing was a significant negative predictor in weighted regression (B=−0.35, 90% CI [−0.65, −0.04], p=.059).

- **Finding 6 — EF feedback level matters**: EF aimed at task level alone: ES'=−0.06 (k=4). EF aimed at task+process levels: ES'=0.50 (k=41). EF at task+regulation: ES'=1.05 (k=4). EF at task+process+regulation: ES'=1.29 (k=1). Higher feedback levels produce larger effects, though k is very small above task+process.

- **Finding 7 — Mathematics subject area shows strikingly large effects**: Mathematics ES'=0.93 (90% CI [0.61, 1.26], k=8, p=.00) — but authors caution this is likely confounded with EF prevalence and should not be generalized. Science: ES'=0.40 (k=29). Social sciences: ES'=0.46 (k=9). Languages: ES'=0.25 (k=24).

- **Finding 8 — No significant timing × outcome level interaction**: Directionality was consistent with the hypothesis (immediate better for lower order, delayed better for higher order) but the interaction was not statistically significant (z=0.82, p=.41). Hypothesis 5 rejected due to insufficient statistical power.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Information richness as active ingredient**: KR merely confirms right/wrong; KCR adds correct answer; EF adds explanation, hints, strategy information, or demonstration of solution. As information richness increases, effect sizes increase consistently across control conditions.

- **EF specificity gradient**: Weighted regression shows that EF in form of explanations without KCR (ES'=0.50) slightly larger than EF as KCR+explanations (ES'=0.35), though difference not significant (p=.248). Subtle guidance may be more effective than highly specific guidance — consistent with Narciss & Huth (2004) finding that not providing KCR leads to deeper processing.

- **Feedback level (Hattie & Timperley framework)**: EF aimed at self level impedes effects (ES'=0.25 with self feedback component). Regulation-level feedback in combination with task+process feedback shows highest effects — but k is too small to generalize.

- **Feedback attention/processing problem**: Students in computer-based environments often ignore or fail to process written feedback (Timmers & Veldkamp, 2011; Van der Kleij et al., 2012). Motivation and perceived need to receive feedback moderate how feedback is processed — this was not directly tested in the meta-analysis.

- **Subject area moderation**: Mathematics showed much larger effects than other subjects — but likely confounded with distribution of feedback types across subjects (EF disproportionately studied in math).

- **Education level moderation**: Primary+high school settings showed lower effects (ES'=0.34) vs. university/college (ES'=0.44), possibly because text-based feedback requires reading abilities not fully developed in younger learners.

## Study Design Details

- **Comparison structure**: Studies compared different feedback types against each other (not purely vs. no-feedback baseline). Control groups received either no feedback, KR, or KCR. This design is informative about relative effectiveness rather than absolute effectiveness.
- **Feedback type**: Item-based, response-based, automatically generated feedback in computer-based assessment or instruction contexts
- **Posttest timing**: Majority of posttests administered immediately or shortly after the formative test (same day or next day) — all effects are short-term
- **Assignment procedure**: Majority of effect sizes from random assignment studies (k=52 of 70)
- **Study quality**: No strict quality threshold imposed; low-quality studies received lower weight due to smaller sample sizes
- **Coding reliability**: Interrater agreement 0.81 for all studies and all effect sizes; 40% of studies double-coded

## Limitations

- **All effects are short-term**: Posttests administered immediately or shortly after the feedback intervention. No follow-up data to assess durability of feedback effects. Long-term effects and true formative potential not assessed.
- **Non-independence of effect sizes**: 70 effects from 40 studies (ratio 70/40 too small for multilevel analysis). Authors ran additional analysis with one ES per study (n=40) — results were "highly similar" — but dependency is a real limitation.
- **Publication bias caveat**: Only published work included (by design); unpublished dissertations excluded to ensure peer review quality. Fail-safe N is relatively modest.
- **Sparse primary/secondary education data**: Only k=2 primary education studies — findings cannot be generalized to school-age learners with confidence.
- **Sparse higher-order outcome data**: Many outcomes are lower-order recall/recognition. Higher-order findings rest on k=27, with wide CIs in subgroups.
- **Mathematics confound**: Strikingly high mathematics effects (ES'=0.93) are likely attributable to the EF prevalence in math studies, not math per se. Authors explicitly caution against interpreting this.
- **EF heterogeneity**: EF covers a very wide range of implementations (hints, explanations, strategy information, metacognitive prompts, worked solutions). The ES'=0.49 summary likely masks substantial variance in what "EF" means.
- **Feedback attention not measured**: Whether students actually read and processed feedback was not measured in most primary studies. This is a significant gap given evidence that students sometimes ignore written feedback in CBAs.
- **No psychometric quality controls**: Item difficulty, posttest reliability (alpha), and number of posttest items were rarely reported — limits ability to weight studies by measurement quality.
- **Interaction test underpowered**: Hypothesis 5 (timing × outcome level interaction) rejected on statistical grounds but direction was as predicted. The cell for delayed feedback + higher order learning contained only two observations.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — primarily university/college students (k=56 of 70 effects); some secondary education (k=12); minimal primary (k=2)
- **Context**: Computer-based assessment and instruction — directly relevant to any digital learning context
- **Relevance to Tyler's use case**: Medium-High. Directly applicable to digital tutorial design (feedback after quizzes, practice questions, check-for-understanding prompts in video). The CBL context is closer to Tyler's async video context than classroom studies. However, all studies involve explicit assessment with scored items — Tyler's context is passive viewing without formal item-based assessment.
- **Context flags**: MOOC/higher-ed context applies to the university majority. No creator-economy or K-12 studies included.
- **Red flags**: None for scope exclusion. Note the assessment framing: findings apply most directly when Tyler incorporates quiz/practice elements, not to passive video watching.

## Conclusions

"The results of this meta-analysis consistently showed that more elaborate feedback led to higher learning outcomes than simple feedback, in particular in regard to higher order learning outcomes. This finding has important implications for designers of educational software and computer-based learning environments." The mean weighted ES' for EF was 0.49, which the authors characterize as moderate-to-large. KR was essentially ineffective (ES'=0.05). The results support the view that information richness — not mere feedback presence — is the active ingredient, with the greatest marginal gain for elaborated feedback appearing at higher order learning outcomes.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Feedback Levels Framework (Hattie & Timperley, 2007)**
  - Components: Self, Task, Process, Regulation
  - Research alignment: Task+process level = dominant in the literature (k=41 of EF studies); Task+regulation and Task+process+regulation produce largest effects but are rarely studied; Self-level feedback impedes learning

- **Feedback Type Taxonomy (Shute, 2008)**
  - Components: KR (knowledge of results) → KCR (knowledge of correct response) → EF (elaborated feedback)
  - Research alignment: KR ineffective (ES'=0.05); KCR modest (ES'=0.33); EF effective (ES'=0.49); confirmed information-richness gradient

## Extraction Notes

This is the third feedback meta-analysis in the registry alongside P001 (Wisniewski) and P009 (Brummer). The registry changelog notes "three-way convergence: information richness is the active ingredient, not feedback presence alone" — this paper provides the strongest computer-specific evidence for that claim.

**Key distinction from other feedback metas**: This paper is specifically scoped to item-based, response-generated, computer-based feedback — making it more directly applicable to digital learning contexts than classroom-feedback metas. The comparison structure (EF vs. KCR vs. KR, not vs. no-feedback) means all effects are relative, and EF's ES'=0.49 is conservatively estimated.

**For chapter writers**: The d=0.49 for EF is the anchor number for computer-based feedback chapters. The finding that EF advantage amplifies for higher order outcomes (ES'=0.37 lower order vs. ES'=0.67 higher order) is the most practically important moderation — it directly argues for explanation-rich feedback over correct-answer feedback when the goal is understanding vs. recall.

**Caution on mathematics effects**: Do not use ES'=0.93 for mathematics as a standalone claim — the paper's own authors warn it is likely a confound.

**Population caveat for Tyler**: All findings assume students respond to an assessment item and receive automated feedback on that response. Tyler's primary format is passive video watching. These findings are most actionable when Tyler incorporates explicit practice elements (quiz cards, pause-and-answer prompts, interactive exercises) into tutorials — not as a general claim about video feedback.
