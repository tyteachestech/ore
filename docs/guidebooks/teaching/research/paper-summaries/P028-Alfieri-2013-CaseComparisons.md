# Learning Through Case Comparisons: A Meta-Analytic Review

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Alfieri, Nokes-Malach, & Schunn, 2013
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=57 experiments, 336 tests; population = mixed (children, adolescents, adults; lab and classroom settings across math, science, and other domains)
- **Journal**: Educational Psychologist, 48(2), 87–113
- **DOI**: 10.1080/00461520.2013.775712
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Overall effect**: Case comparison activities produce a medium positive effect over all other learning conditions. Random effects: d=0.60 (95% CI [0.47, 0.72]) at the experiment level; d=0.50 (95% CI [0.44, 0.56]) at the test level. Fixed effects: d=0.45 (95% CI [0.38, 0.53]) experiments; d=0.37 (95% CI [0.34, 0.41]) tests. Significant heterogeneity: Q(56)=136.74, I^2=59.05%, p<.001 — moderators matter.

- **Objective moderates**: Asking learners to find similarities only (d=0.68, 95% CI [0.60, 0.75]) outperforms asking for both similarities and differences (d=0.28, 95% CI [0.20, 0.36]). Finding only differences produces negative effects (d=−0.19, 95% CI [−0.30, −0.07]). This ordering holds across confounded variables (lag C=0.45, principle C=0.50, publication rank C=0.51).

- **Principle timing is the strongest moderator**: When the principle is given AFTER case comparisons: d=1.18, 95% CI [0.93, 1.44]. Before: d=0.37, 95% CI [0.24, 0.50]. Not provided: d=0.47, 95% CI [0.41, 0.54]. After vs. not-provided: Q(1)=28.18, p<.001; after vs. before: Q(1)=30.78, p<.001; before vs. not-provided: Q(1)=1.84, ns. Principle before does NOT outperform no principle.

- **Content type**: Perceptual content d=0.72 (95% CI [0.49, 0.96]); conceptual d=0.54 (95% CI [0.46, 0.61]); procedural d=0.40 (95% CI [0.31, 0.49]). Perceptual > procedural (Q(1)=6.27, p<.016 adjusted). This moderator is consistent across confounded variables (domain C=0.47, OLS C=0.46, experience C=0.44).

- **Lag**: Immediate testing d=0.57 (95% CI [0.50, 0.64]); same-day d=0.44 (95% CI [0.25, 0.64]); subsequent-day d=0.22 (95% CI [0.11, 0.34]). Immediate > subsequent day (Q(1)=27.21, p<.001). Effect persists but decays.

- **Non-moderators (robust findings)**: Type of instructions (prompted vs. guided), experience level of learner, whether features are provided or learner-generated, age (children vs. adults), and classroom vs. lab setting do NOT significantly moderate outcomes.

- **Comparison to other learning situations (OLS)**: Case comparisons beat control (no study; d=0.69, 95% CI [0.58, 0.80]), nonanalogous (d=0.82, 95% CI [0.55, 1.08]), and single-case (d=0.92, 95% CI [0.57, 1.28]) conditions more strongly than sequential case study (d=0.37, 95% CI [0.31, 0.44]).

- **Publication bias**: Fail-safe N = 2,795 unpublished experiments needed to nullify experiment-level effect; 5,244 at test level. Publication bias is not a plausible explanation for the overall effect.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — effortful search for commonalities**: The core process is learners actively searching for shared features across cases. This effortful search aligns target features, allowing formation of reduced schematic representations stripped of case-specific details. The model proposes 5 steps: (1) instruction to compare, (2) effortful search for commonalities, (3) target features aligned, (4) reduced representation formed, (5) future analogical retrieval.

- **Why similarities-only > both**: Searching for similarities focuses learners on critical relational features. Searching for differences may surface superficial, irrelevant distinctions or increase cognitive load without adding conceptual benefit for single-concept learning. The authors note finding differences may still help when learning to contrast multiple procedures (e.g., Rittle-Johnson & Star, 2007), but most of their sample involved single-concept learning.

- **Why principle-after works so powerfully**: Providing the principle after comparisons lets learners first form tentative inductive conclusions from comparing, then receive the principle as confirmation and reinforcement of that emergent schema. Providing principle before may instead prompt learners to compare cases to the principle rather than to each other, short-circuiting the inductive process. The authors interpret this through Holyoak's (2012) claim that analogical conclusions are tentative and benefit from subsequent confirmation.

- **Why principle-before does not help (vs. nothing)**: No difference found between principle-before and no-principle conditions. The typical STEM instructional model (teach concept, then illustrate with cases) may not leverage comparison's learning benefits.

- **Content type mechanism**: Perceptual comparisons (identifying shared relational structure across visual stimuli) may produce largest effects because minimal cases are used — extraneous surface details are absent, making relational alignment easier and cleaner.

- **Non-moderator: instructions type**: Both prompted ("find similarities") and guided (directed to specific features) produce equivalent effects (d~0.47 vs. 0.52). Guided instructions may compensate for minimal cases; prompts may be sufficient for rich cases.

- **Non-moderator: features provided/generated**: Explicitly labeling features in cases yields equivalent learning to having learners generate them. Constructive generation offers no incremental benefit over explicit provision for this outcome.

- **Lag mechanism**: Temporal proximity between comparison and testing matters — likely via forgetting curve and schema consolidation. Tentative analogical conclusions formed during comparison are more accessible immediately; they decay without further reinforcement (which principle-after may counteract).

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Any learning condition that required simultaneous comparison of two or more analogous cases
- **Comparison**: Sequential case study, single analogous case, nonanalogous case, traditional instruction, or no-study control
- **Units**: Analyzed at both experiment level (k=57) and test level (k=336)
- **Moderator coding reliability**: Average Cohen's kappa = 0.80 across 15 moderators; 25% of studies coded by second author
- **Model**: Random effects (appropriate given diverse methodologies, populations, effect sizes)
- **Publication bias method**: Fail-safe N (conservative; 2,795 / 5,244)
- **Methodological innovation**: Cross-tabulation procedure to disentangle confounded moderators — identified contingency coefficients >.4 and tested whether effects held across all levels

## Limitations

- **Heavy representation of lab studies with minimal cases**: Most studies used simple, brief, minimal case comparisons in controlled settings. Long-duration classroom implementations are underrepresented (only 2 studies with long durations in lab settings).
- **Adolescent age group severely underpowered**: Only 4 experiments; null effect for adolescents should not be interpreted as absence of effect.
- **Sequential conditions dominate OLS comparisons**: 201 of 336 tests compared case comparisons to sequential case study — the most conservative comparison. Overall effects vs. sequential are smaller (d=0.37) than vs. other conditions.
- **Cognitive load not measured**: Authors explicitly flag that no included studies measured learners' subjective cognitive load during comparisons — a key variable in the process model that remains uninvestigated.
- **Content coding complexity**: Content type (conceptual/procedural/perceptual) required inferential judgment by coders; reliability for content was .96 but some classification edge cases were explicitly ambiguous.
- **Domain inconsistency unexplained**: Math domain consistently shows smaller effects. The mechanism is unclear — may involve interactions with rich/minimal cases, experience, or duration, but full explanation requires more targeted studies.
- **No long-form video or async delivery studies**: All studies used live instruction, worked examples, or lab tasks. Transfer to Tyler's async video tutorial context is theoretically sound but empirically untested.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — children (age ≤12), adolescents (13–17), adults (18+); lab and classroom settings; math, science, and other domains
- **Relevance to adult async video learners**: Medium-High. The core effect (simultaneous case comparison > sequential) generalizes across age and setting. Specific implementation variables (objective = similarities, principle after) are directly applicable to tutorial design.
- **Red flags**: No video/async delivery studies in the sample. All comparisons were task-based (learners actively compared); passive viewing of a comparison is not tested. Tyler's context involves him showing the comparison, not having the viewer do the work — mechanism transfer is plausible but requires design work.
- **K-12 context flag**: A substantial portion of included studies used K-12 or undergraduate populations in classroom settings. The finding that age does not moderate outcomes supports generalization to adults, but classroom motivational context differs from voluntary YouTube viewing.

## Conclusions

"Overall our meta-analysis found that case comparisons are generally effective for learning under many different types of implementations, contexts, and measurements. However, the average effect is only medium in size, and there was significant heterogeneity of effects across tasks, contexts, and test measurements, which highlights the importance of investigating which variables moderate the learning outcomes to inform both theory and educational practice."

Four variables reliably moderate effectiveness: (1) the objective of the comparison (find similarities > find both > find differences), (2) the presentation of a principle (after > not provided > before), (3) the content type (perceptual > conceptual > procedural), and (4) the lag between comparison and testing (immediate > same-day > subsequent-day).

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Process Model of Case Comparison** (Alfieri, Nokes-Malach, & Schunn, 2013)
  - Components:
    1. Instruction to compare (prompted or guided)
    2. Effortful search for commonalities
    3. Target features aligned
    4. Reduced representation of cases (schema formation)
    5. Future cases lead to analogical retrieval
  - Research alignment:
    - Step 2 (effortful search) -> objective moderator — similarities-only search most effective
    - Step 3 (alignment) -> feature provision does not affect this step (provided vs. generated equivalent)
    - Step 4 (reduced representation) -> principle-after reinforces and stabilizes the emerging schema
    - Step 5 (retrieval) -> lag moderator — representation is more accessible immediately after formation

## Extraction Notes

**High priority for Tyler's guidebook.** This is the most directly applicable paper in the comparison/explanation science bucket for tutorial design.

**The key actionable finding**: The "before/after" tutorial pattern Tyler already uses intuitively maps onto the highest-performing case comparison condition in this dataset: show two contrasting examples (objective = find similarities), then reveal the principle after. This is not just valid — it is the optimal sequence per this meta-analysis.

**Critical nuance for application**: The d=1.18 "principle after" effect comes from 29 tests in 3 experiments, all from a single paper (Schwartz & Bransford, 1998). The authors explicitly flag this and call for more replication. Do not overclaim this specific number; the directional finding (after > not > before) is robust but the d=1.18 point estimate is narrow-based.

**The "find similarities" finding is counterintuitive but robust**: Most tutorial creators and teachers instinctively show both similarities AND differences. This meta-analysis suggests that framing the comparison as "here's what these two things have in common" outperforms "here's how they're alike and different." However, authors note this applies primarily to single-concept learning — if Tyler is contrasting two competing methods, asking viewers to see differences may still be valuable.

**No inflation flag warranted**: This is a meta-analysis of basic learning science (analogical comparison), not a heavily replicated narrow intervention domain known for inflation.

**Context transfer caveat**: All studies involved learners actively performing comparisons as a task. Tyler's tutorials show comparisons to passive viewers. This is a meaningful gap — the mechanism (effortful search for commonalities) depends on active engagement. Design implication: prompt viewers to do the comparison work before revealing the principle, rather than just displaying the comparison for them to observe.
