# Evaluation of Evidence-Based Practices in Online Learning: A Meta-Analysis and Review of Online Learning Studies

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Means, B., Toyama, Y., Murphy, R., Bakia, M., & Jones, K. (2010/2009). U.S. Department of Education, Office of Planning, Evaluation, and Policy Development, Washington, D.C.
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis and systematic review (government-commissioned report)
- **Sample**: 51 independent effect sizes drawn from 1,000+ screened empirical studies; specific N not available
- **DOI**: None (government report)
- **Available at**: https://www.ed.gov/media/document/evaluation-of-evidence-based-practices-online-learning-meta-analysis-and-review-of-online-learning-studies-revised-september-2010-107159.pdf
- **Citations**: ~3,639 (highly influential)

> **NOTE — Secondary extraction and overlap.** Full text was not acquired for this extraction (freely available from ED.gov). All findings are drawn from: (1) the Consensus abstract, and (2) the LLM1 deep research summary which covers P013 (the journal version) with the same underlying data. This DoE report (P045) and P013 Means et al. (2013) draw from the same meta-analytic dataset — see overlap note in frontmatter.

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Online > face-to-face (on average)**: Students in online learning conditions performed better on average than those in face-to-face instruction.
  - Effect size: approximately d = +0.20 (online vs. F2F)
  - k = 51 independent effect sizes; screening literature from 1996–2008
  - 95% CI: not available from secondary sources

- **Finding 2 — Blended > online > face-to-face**: The difference in student outcomes was **larger** for blended/hybrid conditions (combining online with face-to-face elements) compared to fully online or face-to-face alone.
  - Effect size: approximately d = +0.35 (blended vs. F2F)
  - 95% CI: not available

- **Finding 3 — The blended advantage is confounded**: The larger effect for blended conditions was partially or fully attributable to **additional learning time and instructional resources** received by blended students, not to the blended medium per se. Analysts explicitly noted this confound.

- **Finding 4 — Medium ≠ method**: The report's central conclusion is that the positive effects of online/blended learning should not be attributed to the delivery medium. Instructional design quality, learning time, and the specific activities and resources provided — not whether delivery was online or face-to-face — determined outcomes.

- **Finding 5 — Rigor filtering**: From 1,000+ empirical studies identified, only 51 met criteria for rigorous experimental or quasi-experimental design, adequate controls, and calculable effect sizes. This filtering is a key quality signal — the report is conservative by design.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Online vs. face-to-face**: d ≈ +0.20 (k=51)
  - 95% CI: not available
  - Heterogeneity: not available
- **Blended vs. face-to-face**: d ≈ +0.35
  - 95% CI: not available
  - Confound: additional learning time and resources in blended conditions
- **Online-only vs. blended**: blended larger; exact comparison effect not available

Note: These effect size values appear consistently across both the DoE report (P045) and the journal version (P013). The LLM1 description attributes d=+0.20 and d=+0.35 to P013 (Teachers College Record), which draws from the same data. These should be treated as the same underlying estimates.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism (why online ≥ F2F)**: The report argues the medium is not the mechanism. Online conditions that produced better outcomes typically incorporated: more time on task, higher-quality feedback loops, more flexible review of material, and self-pacing. These are instructional design advantages, not medium advantages.
- **Why blended > pure online**: Blended students often received *more* total instructional resources (both online tools AND face-to-face interaction). When additional resources are controlled for, the blended advantage likely shrinks.
- **Moderators**: Not fully available from secondary sources, but the report identified moderating conditions in narrative review. The Consensus abstract notes that "blended conditions often included additional learning time and instructional elements not received by students in control conditions" as the primary confound variable.

## Study Design Details

- **Source literature**: Systematic search from 1996 through July 2008; 1,000+ empirical studies identified
- **Inclusion criteria**: (1) contrasted online to face-to-face condition, (2) measured student learning outcomes, (3) rigorous research design (experimental or strong quasi-experimental), (4) adequate information to calculate effect size
- **Exclusion**: Studies without comparison group, without learning outcome, or with inadequate design
- **Analysis**: Standard meta-analytic procedures; random-effects assumed
- **Study contexts**: Primarily K-12 and higher education; medical and professional training represented

## Limitations

- Literature ends July 2008 — predates the MOOC era, LMS proliferation, and modern instructional video design
- Most studies from higher education and medical/professional training; K-12 has fewer high-quality studies in the pool
- Only 51 rigorous studies from 1,000+ screened — the inclusion filter is strict, but this also means the 51 studies are not representative of the full online learning landscape
- Blended advantage heavily confounded with additional resources; the "blended learning works" claim without accounting for extra time/resources is misleading
- "Online learning" in 2008 context was substantially different from 2024 YouTube tutorials — text-heavy LMS systems, asynchronous forums, early video lectures; not comparable to modern production-quality instructional video
- No moderator analysis of *which design features* in online conditions drove the advantage — this limits actionability

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — K-12, higher education, medical/professional training
- **Relevance to Tyler's context**: Low-medium — the foundational insight ("design, not medium, drives outcomes") is directly applicable. The specific effect sizes (d=+0.20, +0.35) are dated and from contexts far removed from informal tutorial video.
- **Red flags**: 2008 data ceiling; pre-MOOC, pre-YouTube tutorial era

## Conclusions

Online learning is not inherently superior to face-to-face, nor is it inferior. When adequately designed and resourced, online conditions match or modestly exceed face-to-face outcomes. Blended approaches show larger gains but this is substantially confounded with more total learning time and resources. The report's core claim — that design and time investment drive outcomes, not the delivery medium — is the durable finding with the most practical value.
