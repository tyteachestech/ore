# Those Who Understand: Knowledge Growth in Teaching

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Shulman, L. S. (1986)
- **Study Type**: Conceptual/theoretical — foundational framework paper. No empirical data collected; argument constructed from analysis of historical teacher testing, philosophical reasoning, and the author's Knowledge Growth in Teaching research program.
- **Sample**: N/A — theoretical paper
- **Publication**: *Educational Researcher*, 15(2), 4–14
- **DOI**: 10.3102/0013189X015002004
- **CrossRef citations**: ~9,715 (highest in Teaching Guidebook registry)

> **K-12 context flag:** Shulman's framework was developed in the context of K-12 teacher credentialing and classroom instruction. Direct transfer to adult async video creation is conceptually plausible — the transformation problem is identical — but the original context is formal schooling, not tutorial content creation.

## Key Findings

This is a theoretical framework paper. There are no effect sizes. The contribution is the conceptual architecture — a taxonomy of teacher knowledge and the transformation model.

- **Finding 1 — The Missing Paradigm**: Prior research on teaching focused either on generic pedagogy (management, questioning, feedback) OR on subject matter, but never their intersection. Shulman names this gap the "missing paradigm" — no framework existed for how teachers convert content expertise into teachable form.

- **Finding 2 — Three Categories of Content Knowledge**:
  - **Subject Matter Content Knowledge (SMCK)**: Knowing the subject itself, including both (a) substantive structures — the organizing frameworks, concepts, and principles of a discipline, and (b) syntactic structures — how claims are validated, how truth is established within that discipline. A teacher who only knows "what" without knowing "why" or "how we know" cannot answer student questions beyond the textbook.
  - **Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK)**: The intersection of content and pedagogy — knowing not just the subject but also how to make it comprehensible to a particular audience. Includes: the most useful representations, analogies, illustrations, examples, explanations, and demonstrations; knowledge of what makes a topic easy or hard to learn; knowledge of learner misconceptions and how to address them. This is the category that distinguishes the expert teacher from the expert practitioner.
  - **Curricular Knowledge**: Knowing the full landscape of instructional materials and programs available for a given topic at a given level — the "tools of the trade." Also includes lateral curriculum knowledge (what students are learning in other subjects simultaneously) and vertical curriculum knowledge (what came before and comes after in a sequence).

- **Finding 3 — The Transformation Imperative**: To teach, an expert must transform their knowledge. Shulman describes a pedagogical reasoning process with six stages:
  1. **Comprehension** — understand the content deeply yourself
  2. **Transformation** — convert it into teachable form via: (a) preparation (critical interpretation of materials), (b) representation (finding analogies, metaphors, examples, demonstrations), (c) instructional selection (choosing from a repertoire of teaching approaches), (d) adaptation (tailoring to the specific audience — their prior knowledge, ability, interests, and backgrounds)
  3. **Instruction** — the act of teaching
  4. **Evaluation** — checking comprehension during and after
  5. **Reflection** — reviewing and reconstructing what happened
  6. **New Comprehension** — arriving at deeper understanding through the act of teaching

- **Finding 4 — PCK is Distinct from Subject Expertise**: Deep subject matter knowledge is necessary but not sufficient. The expert practitioner who has never had to explain their knowledge to a novice has not yet developed PCK. The transformation process is a separate cognitive act — it requires knowing your subject from the learner's perspective, not just your own.

- **Finding 5 — Learner Preconceptions as Core PCK**: Knowing what students (or learners) already believe about a topic — including their misconceptions — is a central component of PCK. Without this, a teacher cannot anticipate confusion, correct errors at their root, or build on existing knowledge. Shulman cites research showing that learner misconceptions are often domain-specific and persistent.

- **Finding 6 — Historical Context (The "Manner" vs. "Matter" Split)**: Shulman traces how 19th-century teacher licensing collapsed into purely pedagogical testing (manner of teaching), with no assessment of subject knowledge (matter). He argues this created a profession that treats content as peripheral. PCK is his intervention to reintegrate the two.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Core mechanism — Representation**: Effective teaching requires finding the right representation for a concept for a specific audience. No single representation works for all learners; expert teachers maintain a repertoire. The choice of analogy or example is not decorative — it is the instructional act.

- **Core mechanism — Audience Adaptation**: The same content requires different transformations for different audiences. A learner's prior knowledge, age, cultural background, and motivations all affect which representations will land. PCK is not generic — it is audience-specific.

- **Moderator — Syntactic Knowledge of Subject**: A teacher who knows only the surface facts of a discipline (substantive structure) cannot field unexpected questions, address misconceptions at their root, or help learners understand *why* something is true. Syntactic knowledge — understanding how the discipline validates claims — is a prerequisite for deep PCK.

- **Moderator — Curriculum Awareness**: Teachers who know only their own topic, without knowing what learners already know (vertical curriculum) or what they're learning elsewhere (lateral curriculum), cannot sequence or connect content effectively.

## Study Design Details

- **Type**: Conceptual argument drawing on: (a) the author's multi-year "Knowledge Growth in Teaching" research program at Stanford (observing first-year teachers), (b) historical analysis of 19th-century teacher licensing exams, (c) philosophical analysis of subject matter structure
- **No control condition, no randomization, no measurement instrument**
- **Influence**: This paper launched the PCK research program that now spans thousands of studies across every subject domain

## Limitations

- **No empirical validation in the paper itself**: The taxonomy is reasoned, not measured. Whether PCK as defined here is a reliable, measurable construct was left to subsequent researchers (and remains contested).
- **K-12 classroom origin**: Framework developed from observation of K-12 student teachers and historical classroom contexts. Applicability to self-directed adult learning, async video, or informal expertise-sharing is an extrapolation — a very defensible one, but an extrapolation.
- **Curricular knowledge is the weakest category**: In Shulman's schema, curricular knowledge (knowing the full instructional materials landscape) is coherent for school settings with textbook adoption cycles. For async video creators, this component maps imperfectly.
- **PCK is described, not operationalized**: The paper does not specify how to measure or develop PCK. This was intentional — it is a framework paper — but it means direct application requires translation.
- **Single-author theoretical piece**: No peer review of the framework's internal logic; influence is through citation adoption, not replication.

## Conclusions

Shulman argues that the most important and most neglected category of teacher knowledge is pedagogical content knowledge — the capacity to transform one's own understanding of a subject into forms that are comprehensible, compelling, and appropriate for a specific audience. This requires knowing the subject at two levels (what is true AND how we know it is true), maintaining a repertoire of representations and analogies, understanding learner preconceptions, and selecting instructional approaches adapted to the audience. The paper names a gap in both research and practice — the separation of subject expertise from teaching expertise — and proposes PCK as the bridge. As Shulman states: "Mere content knowledge is likely to be as useless pedagogically as content-free skill."

## Named Frameworks

- **Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK)**
  - Components:
    - Knowledge of representations (analogies, metaphors, examples, illustrations, demonstrations)
    - Knowledge of learner preconceptions and misconceptions
    - Knowledge of what makes topics easy or difficult to learn
    - Ability to adapt representations to specific audiences
  - Research alignment (for this guidebook):
    - Representations -> multimedia principles (Clark & Mayer), worked examples research
    - Preconceptions -> prior knowledge activation, expertise reversal effect
    - Audience adaptation -> learner analysis, expertise reversal effect
    - Repertoire of approaches -> interleaving, varied practice

- **Three Categories of Content Knowledge**
  - Subject Matter Content Knowledge (SMCK) — substantive + syntactic
  - Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK) — the transformation layer
  - Curricular Knowledge — landscape of available materials and sequences

- **Pedagogical Reasoning Cycle** (Comprehension → Transformation → Instruction → Evaluation → Reflection → New Comprehension)
  - Most actionable for Tyler: the Transformation stage, specifically the four sub-acts: preparation, representation, instructional selection, adaptation
