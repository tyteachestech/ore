# Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation from a Self-Determination Theory Perspective

- **Citation**: Ryan, R. M. & Deci, E. L., 2020

- **Sample**: No primary data; reviews hundreds of studies across elementary through postgraduate settings

## Key Findings

This is a framework paper. Effect sizes are cited from other studies, not generated here. The primary value is the definitional and mechanistic architecture of SDT as applied to education.

- **Finding 1 — Motivational taxonomy**: SDT distinguishes five regulatory styles on a continuum from controlled to autonomous:
  1. *External regulation* — compliance with external rewards/punishments
  2. *Introjection* — ego-involvement, avoiding shame/guilt
  3. *Identification* — consciously valuing the activity
  4. *Integration* — activity is congruent with core values and identity
  5. *Intrinsic motivation* — inherent interest and enjoyment
  - Amotivation (lack of any regulation) sits at the far controlled end.
  - Practical implication: the goal is not just "more motivation" but *more autonomous motivation* — identification and integration matter as much as intrinsic.

- **Finding 2 — Three basic psychological needs**: Autonomy, competence, and relatedness are posited as universal. Their satisfaction predicts intrinsic motivation and well-internalized extrinsic motivation; their frustration undermines both.
  - Autonomy: sense of volition and ownership
  - Competence: felt mastery and growth
  - Relatedness: sense of belonging and connection

- **Finding 3 — Teacher autonomy-support**: Specific, empirically-catalogued behaviors support autonomous motivation in students:
  - Listening to students, being responsive to their perspectives
  - Providing meaningful choices and rationales
  - Acknowledging student interests
  - Offering progress-enabling hints rather than controlling directives
  - Avoiding "should" and "have to" language
  - Referenced effect: Reeve et al. (2004) trained teachers; students in trained-teacher classes were more engaged.
  - Meta-analytic effect: Su & Reeve (2011) — 19 intervention studies, d=0.63 for intervention groups vs. controls.

- **Finding 4 — Structure vs. control**: SDT distinguishes between *structure* (clear expectations, consistent rules, informational feedback, scaffolding) and *control* (pressure toward specific behaviors). Both can coexist; highest outcomes associated with high autonomy-support AND high structure. Structure satisfies competence; autonomy-support satisfies autonomy.

- **Finding 5 — Grading undermines intrinsic motivation**: Grolnick & Ryan (1987) found grading on an ecologically valid task was associated with decreased intrinsic motivation and lower conceptual learning. Butler (1987) showed grades provide little competence-relevant feedback, only social comparison. Grades are experienced as controlling signals, not informational ones.
  - Application to Tyler: Feedback that is informational (shows where they stand on mastery, not relative to others) sustains motivation better than evaluative framing.

- **Finding 6 — Performance goals vs. mastery goals**: Performance goals (outperform others) tend to produce controlled motivation. Mastery goals (improve own competence) more aligned with autonomous motivation and well-being. Performance-avoidance goals are most detrimental.

- **Finding 7 — Intrinsic motivation declines across school years**: Multiple longitudinal studies; associated with decreasing need satisfaction. Gnambs & Hanfstingl (2016) meta-analysis confirmed this. Schools are generally not creating need-supportive conditions.

- **Finding 8 — Cross-cultural universality**: SDT's basic need claims are supported across collectivist and individualist cultures (Nigeria, India, Japan, Korea, China, Russia, U.S., Finland, Belgium). The *salience* and *expression* of needs varies culturally, but their functional importance does not. Autonomy in particular is not Western-specific.

- **Finding 9 — Teacher needs mirror student needs**: Teachers' own psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) must be satisfied for them to be need-supportive of students. Controlling administrative environments reduce teacher autonomy, which in turn increases teacher controlling behavior toward students. This cascading effect is empirically documented.

- **Finding 10 — Technology and SDT**: Games and digital environments can satisfy all three needs (SDT-aligned game design). This is flagged as a future research direction. Ryan & Rigby (2019) applied this to educational technology.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

This paper does not generate primary effect sizes. It cites the following from other work:

- **Autonomy-support interventions** (teacher training): d=0.63, Su & Reeve (2011) meta-analysis, k=19 studies, intervention vs. control teachers
- **Intrinsic motivation → engagement → GPA**: Froiland & Worrell (2016) — intrinsic motivation predicted engagement, which predicted GPA; consistent across African American and Latino students
- **Choice → intrinsic motivation**: Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008) meta-analysis — positive effect of choice on intrinsic motivation (effect size not quoted in text, described as positive)
- **Performance grading → motivation**: Grolnick & Ryan (1987) — negative effect on intrinsic motivation and conceptual learning (no d quoted)
- **Intrinsic motivation → school achievement**: Taylor et al. (2014) meta-analysis — "significant role" (effect size not quoted in text)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Need satisfaction → autonomous motivation → outcomes**: The central causal chain. Need satisfaction is the proximate mechanism; autonomous motivation mediates downstream outcomes (engagement, learning, well-being).

- **Functional significance of events**: Every classroom event (feedback, reward, choice, instruction style) is processed for its *informational* vs. *controlling* significance. Informational events (competence-relevant, volitional) sustain motivation. Controlling events undermine it. This is the cognitive appraisal mechanism.

- **Internalization process**: Extrinsic motivations can be internalized along the continuum from introjection → identification → integration. The degree of internalization predicts outcome quality. Internalization is facilitated by autonomy-support, rationale-provision, and relatedness.

- **Moderator — autonomy-support AND structure**: Best outcomes when both are high simultaneously. Structure alone (controlling delivery) does not produce the same gains. Soenens & Vansteenkiste (2010) — structure's effects are influenced by how it's delivered.

- **Moderator — perceived locus of causality**: Whether the person experiences causality as internal (autonomous) or external (controlled) mediates whether motivation is high-quality or not.

- **Moderator — cultural context**: Emic variations in how needs are expressed; etic universality in functional importance. Person-centered analyses (rather than variable-centered) increasingly recommended to capture cultural nuance.

## Limitations

- **Author bias**: Paper authored by the theory's creators (Ryan and Deci). No independent review of the evidence base. Claims presented selectively.
- **No effect size synthesis**: The paper summarizes findings narratively; the reader cannot assess publication bias, heterogeneity, or mean effect sizes from this paper alone.
- **School-context emphasis**: Nearly all cited research involves formal educational institutions — grading systems, mandatory attendance, teacher-student hierarchies. Tyler's context (voluntary YouTube tutorial viewing) has different structural dynamics.
- **No null findings discussed**: The paper does not address studies where SDT-based interventions failed or produced null results.
- **Conceptual conflation risk**: The taxonomy (Fig. 1) is useful, but the continuum assumption (that motivation moves along a linear autonomy dimension) has been contested empirically — some studies show these are not simply ordered.

## Conclusions

"SDT specifically maintains that for teachers to actively support students' needs, they themselves must experience need supports." The authors argue that the gap between what SDT research demonstrates and current educational policy (grades, HST, performance goals) is a "knowledge versus policy gap we should aspire to close." The paper's core prescriptive position: environments that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness produce higher-quality motivation, learning, and well-being across cultures, ages, and educational levels — and current dominant policies work against this.

## Named Frameworks

- **Framework**: Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
  - Components:
    - Three Basic Psychological Needs: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness
    - Motivational taxonomy: Amotivation → External → Introjection → Identification → Integration → Intrinsic
    - Relative autonomy index: summary score of autonomous vs. controlled motives
    - Mini-theories within SDT: Cognitive Evaluation Theory (intrinsic motivation), Organismic Integration Theory (internalization), Basic Psychological Needs Theory, Causality Orientations Theory, Goal Contents Theory, Relationships Motivation Theory
  - Research alignment:
    - Autonomy need → viewer sense of choice and ownership in learning
    - Competence need → calibrated challenge, mastery-oriented feedback, visible progress
    - Relatedness need → parasocial connection, creator warmth and responsiveness
    - Identification/Integration → helping learners connect content to their own values and goals (not just "here's the info")
    - Introjection → what Tyler must avoid: shame-based or ego-threat framing

This is a framework paper and should be treated as such — no primary effect sizes to extract. Its value for the guidebook is:

1. **Definitional clarity**: The taxonomy (Fig. 1) is the single best visual for distinguishing motivation types. Worth referencing in the chapter on learner motivation.

2. **Practical behavior list**: The autonomy-supportive teaching behaviors (Section 3.1, 3.2) are directly actionable for tutorial design — listening, providing rationale, offering meaningful choice, avoiding controlling language.

3. **Foundation for P012, P034, P037**: Per registry notes, this paper provides the theoretical scaffolding for Howard (P012), Wang (P034), and Rosli (P037). Read those papers' findings through this framework.

4. **Tyler-specific translation opportunities**:
   - *Choice* in tutorial design: giving viewers decision points, branching explanations, "if you already know X, skip to..."
   - *Rationale provision*: explaining why a concept matters before teaching it (the "curiosity gap" opening in Tyler's template maps onto this)
   - *Informational vs. controlling feedback*: how Tyler frames viewer skill-building in CTAs and community responses
   - *Competence scaffolding*: calibrating difficulty, making mastery feel achievable but not trivial

5. **Watch for inflation**: The d=0.63 for teacher autonomy-support interventions is from a non-preregistered meta-analysis of 19 studies in school settings. Do not carry this effect size forward as-is to Tyler's context. Treat as directional signal only.
