# From Recorded to AI-Generated Instructional Videos: A Comparison of Learning Performance and Experience

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Xu, T., Liu, Y., Jin, Y., Qu, Y., Bai, J., Zhang, W., & Zhou, Y., 2024
- **Study Type**: Experimental (RCT — random assignment to two conditions)
- **Sample**: n=76, undergraduate and graduate students from four universities in western China; aged 17-27 (M=22.11, SD=2.47); 32 males, 44 females
- **Conditions**: AI-generated instructional video (AIIV, n=38) vs. traditional recorded video (RV, n=38)
- **Content domain**: English vocabulary learning (8 words per video; GRE/TOEFL/IELTS vocabulary)
- **Video duration**: AIIV = 7 min 46 s; RV = 7 min 58 s
- **DOI**: 10.1111/bjet.13530

> **MOOC context flag:** Participants are Chinese university students engaged in controlled English vocabulary learning — closer to Tyler's async video context than K-12, but the content type (second language acquisition, vocabulary) is a narrow domain. Standardization advantages of AI speech may not transfer to Mac/AI tutorial content where accent and prosody matter less.

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Retention (PRIMARY)**: AIIV group scored significantly higher on the retention posttest (M=7.50, SD=0.86) compared to RV group (M=6.53, SD=2.00), U=543.5, z=-2.075, p=0.038, r=-0.24. Effect is small-to-medium.
  - The authors attribute this to AI TTS providing standardized pronunciation and accent — consistent delivery may have aided word recall.

- **Finding 2 — Transfer**: No significant difference in transfer scores between AIIV (M=18.11, SD=3.49) and RV (M=16.87, SD=4.36), t(74)=1.364, p=0.177, Cohen's d=0.31. The numerical trend favored AIIV but did not reach significance.

- **Finding 3 — Overall Learning Performance**: No significant difference in overall performance (AIIV: M=25.60, SD=4.07; RV: M=23.39, SD=6.08), U=597.5, z=-1.298, p=0.194, r=-0.15.

- **Finding 4 — Cognitive Load (LOWER for AIIV, non-significant)**: Mental load: AIIV M=2.40 vs. RV M=2.78, t(74)=-1.85, p=0.068, d=-0.42. Mental effort: AIIV M=2.19 vs. RV M=2.49, t(74)=-1.46, p=0.149, d=-0.33. Both sub-dimensions trended lower for AIIV but neither reached significance. This was INCONSISTENT with H2 (which predicted higher cognitive load for AIIV).

- **Finding 5 — Social Presence (SIGNIFICANTLY LOWER for AIIV)**: AIIV M=2.89, SD=1.07 vs. RV M=3.66, SD=0.97, t(74)=-3.28, p=0.002, Cohen's d=-0.75. This is the single significant subjective difference — real human instructors generated substantially more social presence.

- **Finding 6 — Uncanny Valley NOT triggered**: Humanness score for AI teacher: M=2.82 (SD=1.31) — rated low humanness. Attractiveness: M=3.24 (SD=1.04) — rated moderately to highly attractive. The authors interpret this combination (low humanness + high attractiveness) as evidence that the uncanny valley threshold was not crossed.

- **Finding 7 — Emotions, Motivation, Trust, Parasocial Interaction, Satisfaction: No significant differences**, but all directionally lower for AIIV:
  - Emotions: AIIV M=5.53 vs. RV M=5.54, p=0.980, d=-0.0057 (essentially identical)
  - Motivation: AIIV M=3.00 vs. RV M=3.34, p=0.119, d=-0.36
  - Trust: AIIV M=5.58 vs. RV M=5.58, p=1.000, d=0.00 (identical)
  - Parasocial interaction: AIIV M=2.55 vs. RV M=2.82, p=0.416, d=-0.19
  - Satisfaction: AIIV M=3.05 vs. RV M=3.51, p=0.094, d=-0.39

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Retention**: r=-0.24 (U=543.5, z=-2.075, p=0.038) — SIGNIFICANT, favors AIIV
- **Transfer**: d=0.31 (t(74)=1.364, p=0.177) — non-significant, numerically favors AIIV
- **Overall performance**: r=-0.15 (U=597.5, z=-1.298, p=0.194) — non-significant
- **Mental load**: d=-0.42 (t(74)=-1.85, p=0.068) — trending, favors AIIV (lower load)
- **Mental effort**: d=-0.33 (t(74)=-1.46, p=0.149) — non-significant, favors AIIV
- **Emotions**: d=-0.0057 (t(74)=-0.03, p=0.980) — null
- **Motivation**: d=-0.36 (t(74)=-1.58, p=0.119) — non-significant, favors RV
- **Social presence**: d=-0.75 (t(74)=-3.28, p=0.002) — SIGNIFICANT, favors RV

- **Satisfaction**: d=-0.39 (t(74)=-1.70, p=0.094) — non-significant, favors RV

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Standardized delivery advantage**: AI TTS provides consistent pronunciation, accent, and pacing. For vocabulary learning specifically, this may improve retention by reducing acoustic variability. Authors note this advantage may not generalize to disciplines requiring logical reasoning (science, engineering).

- **Social presence — cognitive load tradeoff**: The heightened social presence of real human instructors came at the cost of higher cognitive load. Authors frame this as "real human videos are not the absolute ideal teaching resource" — the human's social warmth introduces processing overhead that the AI avatar eliminates.

- **Content type moderator (discussed, not tested)**: Authors explicitly flag that their findings are specific to second language vocabulary acquisition. Science and engineering content requires logical reasoning scripts that AI may not generate as effectively. The standardization benefit is domain-specific.

- **Novelty effect (untested)**: Authors acknowledge the short-term nature of the study may have inflated AIIV retention scores via novelty. Not examined.

- **No moderator analyses conducted** within the sample — single study, no subgroup breakdowns.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: AI-generated video pipeline: (1) GPT generates lecture script from slide OCR, (2) SADtalker + Microsoft Edge TTS generates lip-synced talking-head avatar, (3) manual compositing with slides. Produces a B3-type instructional video (narrator at fixed position overlapping slide background).
- **Control**: Same human instructor recorded with conventional methods; same slide design.
- **Content**: 8 English vocabulary words (GRE/TOEFL/IELTS level), selected via pilot familiarity test (M familiarity=1.73/7)
- **Follow-up period**: Immediate only — posttests administered immediately after single-session video viewing (~25 minutes total experiment time)
- **Attrition**: None reported — n=76 completed
- **Blinding**: Participants were told which condition they were in (necessary); raters not mentioned
- **Power analysis**: Conducted via G*Power 3.1; d=0.8 minimum required 42 participants; n=76 exceeds this

## Limitations

- **Single domain (EFL vocabulary)**: All results are from English word learning. Transfer to conceptual or procedural content is untested and authors explicitly flag this concern.
- **Short-term only**: Immediate posttest; no delayed retention test. Novelty effect on AIIV retention cannot be ruled out.
- **AIIV components not isolated**: Image, voice, and lecture text were all generated simultaneously — impossible to attribute effects to any single component. Future work will separate these.
- **Self-report dominance**: All subjective measures are self-reported; no behavioral engagement data (eye-tracking, EEG). Dynamic attention allocation is unknown.
- **Chinese university student sample**: All participants from western China mainland; English is a foreign language for all. Accent consistency advantage of TTS is especially salient here — may not replicate for native English speakers.
- **Small n within each cell**: n=38 per group. While powered for large effects, small-to-medium effects in secondary outcomes may be underpowered.
- **Single video per condition**: One AIIV and one RV. No generalization across instructors, topics, or video styles.
- **Pipeline not fully automated**: Manual compositing step required; not yet a push-button solution.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (undergraduate and graduate), Chinese, EFL learners
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Low — this is a controlled lab study of vocabulary acquisition, not behavior change
- **Relevance to Tyler's context**: Medium. The core question (AI avatar vs. human presenter in instructional video) is directly relevant. The specific domain (EFL vocabulary) limits direct claim transfer. The social presence and cognitive load findings are the most portable.
- **Red flags**: None (non-clinical, non-addiction population). Flag: EFL/second-language context means TTS standardization benefit is heightened relative to native-language tutorial content.

## Conclusions

AI-generated instructional videos (using GPT scripts, TTS, and lip synthesis) can facilitate learning as effectively as traditional recorded videos, with the AIIV condition producing significantly higher retention scores. However, AI videos generate substantially less social presence (d=-0.75) than real human instructors, while other subjective dimensions (satisfaction, motivation, trust, emotions) show no significant differences but trend directionally lower. The uncanny valley effect was not observed — the AI teacher was rated attractive but low in humanness, suggesting the current generation of AI avatars occupies a comfortable position below the uncanny valley threshold. The tradeoff between social warmth (favoring real humans) and cognitive efficiency (favoring AI) is the central theoretical contribution.

## Named Frameworks

- **Equivalence Principle** (Horovitz & Mayer, 2021)
  - Components: Virtual instructors can serve the same role as human instructors in video-based learning; people respond to emotional tone of computerized agents as they do to humans
  - Research alignment: Partially supported — learning outcomes equivalent, but social presence is not equivalent

- **Social Presence Theory** (Martha & Santoso, 2019; Borup et al., 2012)
  - Components: Degree to which individuals feel existence, attention, and recognition by others in social context
  - Research alignment: RV > AIIV on social presence (d=-0.75, p=0.002); tradeoff with cognitive load

- **Uncanny Valley** (Mori et al., 2012)
  - Components: Humanoid entities that are highly realistic but imperfect trigger negative responses (decreased trust, negative emotion)
  - Research alignment: Not triggered in this study — low humanness + moderate attractiveness combination avoided the effect

## Extraction Notes

This paper is directly relevant to Tyler's interest in AI avatar tools for tutorial creation. The headline finding — AI-generated videos match or exceed retention from recorded human-presenter videos — is creator-favorable. But the social presence deficit (d=-0.75) is the most robust finding in the paper and should not be buried.

Key practical signal for the guidebook: the human instructor's social warmth appears to impose a real cognitive cost. AI avatars may be a legitimate option for content where standardization and low cognitive overhead matter more than parasocial connection — but the paper's domain (EFL vocabulary) makes this harder to generalize to Tyler's Mac/AI tutorial format, where explaining reasoning (not just vocabulary) is central.

Cross-reference note: Compare with P049 Gu (animated agent approximately equal to human for learning, lower attentional cost). These two studies converge on similar conclusions from different methodological angles. Key distinction — Xu et al. includes a fully generated lecture text (not just visual/voice), making it the more complete test of AI-pipeline video.

The "standardized pronunciation" explanation for the retention advantage is almost certainly domain-specific to EFL and should not be cited as a general principle for tutorial creators.
