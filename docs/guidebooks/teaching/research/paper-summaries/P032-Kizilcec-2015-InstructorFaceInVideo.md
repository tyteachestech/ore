# The Instructor's Face in Video Instruction: Evidence From Two Large-Scale Field Studies

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Kizilcec, Bailenson, & Gomez, 2015
- **Study Type**: Two longitudinal field studies — Study 1: observational with self-selection; Study 2: randomized controlled field experiment
- **Sample**:
  - Study 1: N=2,951 (behavioral analysis), 3,494 (self-report), Coursera sociology MOOC, 10 weeks; 48% female, 52% male, mean age 37.6
  - Study 2: N=12,468 watched at least one lecture; Coursera sociology MOOC, 10 weeks; 51% female, 49% male, 63% aged 25-44, >75% bachelor's or master's degree
  - Combined N referenced: ~15,419 unique learners across both studies
- **Platform**: Coursera MOOC (Stanford-hosted sociology course)
- **DOI**: 10.1037/edu0000013
- **Note**: Article has been corrected (minor typographic error in Tables 2 and 3; "value" inadvertently included after "p" — no substantive changes)

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Preference majority but substantial minority avoids face (Study 1)**: 57% of learners chose face videos, 35% without face, 8% both. When shown without face first, 35% chose face and 44% chose no face — order effects were significant (chi-square significant, p<.001). Most commonly reported reason for avoiding face: distraction ("the instructor's face only added extraneous cognitive load"; "since most of what was described was on the slides, I did not find it much value").

- **Finding 2 — Self-selected face viewers report better subjective experience (Study 1)**: Learners who chose face-on videos reported higher perceived learning, affect, and lower effort than those who chose face-off. Effect sizes are small but statistically significant.
  - Perceived learning with vs. without face: 3.52 (SD=0.79) vs. 3.46 (SD=0.82); effect size 1.7% (95% CI [0.8%, 2.7%]), z=3.6, p<.001
  - Affect: 5.30 (SD=1.00) vs. 5.15 (SD=1.07); effect size 2.8% (95% CI [2.0%, 3.7%]), z=6.6, p<.001
  - Effort: 2.85 (SD=0.94) vs. 2.93 (SD=0.93); effect size -2.9% (95% CI [-1.5%, -4.3%]), z=-4.0, p<.001
  - Data from 21,576 responses from 3,494 learners. **Caution: self-selection bias — these are not causal estimates.**

- **Finding 3 — Strategic vs. constant face: higher cognitive load and social presence, no learning benefit (Study 2 RCT)**: The strategic presentation (face shown only when pedagogically indicated — when spoken text is independent of slide content) induced 2.5% higher cognitive load and 2.2% higher social presence than constant face, with no difference in learning outcomes or attrition at the group level.
  - Cognitive load: strategic 0.47 (SD=0.20) vs. constant 0.46 (SD=0.19); bootstrapped z=2.4, p=.016; effect size 2.5% (95% CI [0.5%, 4.6%]); n=10,810 responses from 2,672 learners
  - Social presence: strategic 0.59 (SD=0.18) vs. constant 0.57 (SD=0.17); z=2.1, p=.037; effect size 2.2% (95% CI [0.1%, 4.2%]); n=5,965 responses from 2,169 learners
  - Course grade: 8.6 (SD=26.0) strategic vs. 8.9 (SD=26.1) constant; d=0.01, n.s.
  - Assessment taking: 10.7% (660/5473) strategic vs. 11.4% (720/5615) constant; odds ratio=1.06, n.s.

- **Finding 4 — No main effect on attrition (Study 2)**: No significant difference in attrition between constant and strategic conditions at the group level (z=0.16, p=.85 at Week 1; z=1.60, p=.11 at Week 2; z=0.77, p=.44 at Week 3).

- **Finding 5 — Learning preference moderates cognitive load (Study 2)**: Verbal-preference learners experienced 20% higher cognitive load with the strategic face than the constant face. Visual-preference learners experienced 13% lower cognitive load with the strategic face. Interaction effect significant: F(3, 487) = 3.0, p=.030.

- **Finding 6 — Learning preference moderates attrition (Study 2)**: Verbal-preference learners in the strategic condition were 46% more likely to drop out than verbal-preference learners in the constant condition (Cox regression z=1.93, p=.05). Visual-preference learners in the strategic condition were 77% more likely to drop out than verbal-preference learners in the constant condition (z=3.49, p<.001) — the largest attrition difference observed.

- **Finding 7 — Social presence grows over time but equally across conditions**: Social presence increased 9.3% between first and last week of the course (z=30.00, p<.001, 95% CI [2.3, 16.4]). Strategic condition showed higher social presence in specific weeks (Weeks 3, 4, 7, p<.05) but no consistent overall advantage.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Study 1 (observational, self-selection — not causal):**
- Perceived learning (with vs. without face): effect = 1.7% (95% CI [0.8%, 2.7%]), z=3.6, p<.001, n=21,576 responses/3,494 learners
- Affect: 2.8% (95% CI [2.0%, 3.7%]), z=6.6, p<.001
- Effort: -2.9% (95% CI [-1.5%, -4.3%]), z=-4.0, p<.001
- Preference distribution: chi-square (2, N=2,231) = 1,892, p<.001

**Study 2 (RCT, N=12,468):**
- Course grade: d=0.01, n.s.
- Assessment taking: OR=1.06, n.s.
- Cognitive load (strategic vs. constant): 2.5% (95% CI [0.5%, 4.6%]), z=2.4, p=.016
- Social presence: 2.2% (95% CI [0.1%, 4.2%]), z=2.1, p=.037

**Moderator analyses (Study 2, learning preference subgroup, N=1,206):**
- Cognitive load x learning preference interaction: F(3, 487)=3.0, p=.030
  - Verbal/constant: 0.43 (SD=0.20)
  - Verbal/strategic: 0.49 (SD=0.19) — 20% higher
  - Visual/constant: 0.46 (SD=0.19)
  - Visual/strategic: 0.43 (SD=0.19) — 13% lower
- Course grade x learning preference: F(3, 487)=2.2, n.s.
- Assessment taking x learning preference: chi-square(3)=2.4, n.s.
- Social presence x learning preference: F(3, 812)=0.6, n.s.
- Attrition (verbal, strategic vs. constant): z=1.93, p=.05, proportional hazard n.s.
- Attrition (visual, strategic vs. constant): z=1.57, p=.12, n.s. (reversed direction)
- Attrition (verbal vs. visual, constant): z=2.59, p=.01 — verbal 35% more likely to persist
- Attrition (verbal/constant vs. visual/strategic): z=3.49, p<.001 — 77% difference

**Heterogeneity note**: Cognitive load varied strongly with video length (r=.56, t(36)=4.1, p<.001), not just presentation style.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Image principle (Mayer, 2001)**: The paper is framed as a test of this principle — that adding a picture of the instructor to multimedia instruction does not improve learning because social cue benefits are offset by increased extraneous cognitive processing. Results are consistent with this principle: no learning benefit found in the RCT.

- **Cognitive aid / signaling mechanism**: The strategic face was designed as a cognitive attention-directing cue — face appears when the spoken text is independent of the slide, signaling the learner to attend to the instructor. This backfired: the intermittent appearance increased the face's salience, inducing more social presence AND more cognitive load than constant presence.

- **Social presence mechanism**: Instructor face induces parasocial interaction and learned social responses (politeness, attention). Strategic condition still induced more social presence than constant, contrary to prediction — suggesting the intermittent disappearance heightened the face's salience rather than reducing it.

- **Learning preference as moderator**: Verbal-preference learners benefited from constant face (lower cognitive load, lower attrition). Visual-preference learners showed opposite pattern for cognitive load but not for learning outcomes or attrition (not significant). Authors suggest visual-preference learners may rely less on nonverbal cues and treat the strategic face as a distracting visual stimulus.

- **Self-selection confound (Study 1)**: Learners who chose face videos were, on average, subjectively better off — but this reflects selection bias, not causation. Preferences partly determine experience ratings.

## Study Design Details

- **Study 1 (observational)**:
  - Course offered both face and no-face versions; learners chose freely after Week 1 exposure to both
  - All learners saw both versions in Week 1 (alternating) before choosing
  - Video-watching behavior tracked via database timestamps (minimum 3 min viewed = counted)
  - Self-report: affect, effort, perceived learning (Weeks 2-8); optional survey with preference and rationale questions (10% response rate)
  - Linear model with fixed effect per video and learner-clustered bootstrap (1,000 replications)
  - Cohen's d not reported — model uses normalized relative change
  - Order effects present in the two reversed weeks — acknowledged as limitation

- **Study 2 (RCT)**:
  - Random assignment at enrollment via user ID to constant face (n=6,335) or strategic face (n=6,133) conditions
  - 57 lecture videos created in two versions; strategic version individually reviewed and edited by one researcher using a predefined decision rule (Figure 2)
  - Face present 33-67% of the time in strategic condition
  - Attrition defined by start/stop times (5th/95th percentile of watching times per lecture)
  - 89% of learners scored a zero grade (did not take assessments) — grade analysis excludes them
  - Social presence: 5-item scale (Cronbach's alpha=.72), 17% response rate
  - Cognitive load: adapted NASA-TLX 2-item subset, 21% response rate
  - Learning preference: single item from Learning Styles Index, 11% response rate (N=1,206 subgroup)

- **Follow-up**: 10 weeks
- **Attrition**: High by design (MOOC context); 89% took no assessments; the 51-77% who started in the first 3 weeks are the analysis base for attrition

## Limitations

- **MOOC context** — highly educated adult learners (75%+ bachelor's/master's degree), voluntary enrollment, no institutional stakes. Not directly generalizable to lower-stakes async tutorial content like YouTube (though closer to Tyler's context than K-12).
- **Single instructor, single subject** — all videos were sociology lectures with a single presentation format (text slides + narration, occasional graphics). Findings may not transfer to instructors who write and annotate in real time (computer science, math).
- **Differential attrition in RCT** — random assignment created comparable groups at the start, but differential dropout over 10 weeks could introduce bias in later self-report comparisons. Authors acknowledge this and restrict some analyses to the first half of the course.
- **Strategic condition coded by a single researcher** — subjectivity in applying the decision rule; limited intercoder reliability data.
- **Self-report outcomes** — cognitive load and social presence are subjective measures subject to expectation and demand effects.
- **Learning preference measured with a single item** — the Learning Styles Index item used has known psychometric challenges; authors acknowledge this limitation.
- **No no-face condition in Study 2** — the experiment compared constant vs. strategic; the condition with no face at all (which ~35% of learners preferred in Study 1) was not tested experimentally.
- **Order effects in Study 1** — two weeks with reversed presentation order showed substantially different preference distributions, suggesting framing effects.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Adult MOOC learners — highly educated (75%+ college degree), globally diverse, voluntary enrollment, mean age 37.6
- **Relevance to Tyler's context**: Medium-High. MOOC learners watching asynchronous video lectures is structurally similar to YouTube tutorial viewers, though commitment and institutional framing differ. Self-motivated adult learners overlap substantially.
- **Context flag**: MOOC/higher-ed context — closer to Tyler's context than K-12, but note differences in learner commitment (MOOC enrollees often expect formal course experience) and subject matter (sociology lectures vs. Mac/AI tutorials with heavy screen-share and live interaction).
- **Red flags**: None (no clinical population, no hard addiction domain)

## Conclusions

The paper tests the "image principle" — that showing an instructor's face in video does not improve learning — in two large-scale field studies. Study 1 finds that although the majority of learners prefer the face, a substantial minority (35%) actively avoid it to reduce distraction. Study 2 (RCT, N=12,468) confirms: constant face vs. strategically presented face produces no significant difference in learning outcomes or attrition at the group level. The strategic presentation increased cognitive load and social presence without improving outcomes — worse for verbal-preference learners in particular. The authors conclude that "a one-size-fits-all strategy for the presentation of the instructor's face was found to adversely affect some learners by inducing higher cognitive load and attrition, depending on their learning preference."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Image Principle (Mayer, 2001)**
  - Components: Adding an image of an instructor/agent to multimedia instruction does not promote learning; social cue benefits are offset by extraneous visual processing costs
  - Research alignment: Supported by Study 2 RCT (no learning difference); partially complicated by Study 1 self-selection data showing subjective preference for the face
- **Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (Mayer, 2005)**
  - Components: Dual channels, limited capacity, active processing; extraneous load hinders germane processing
  - Research alignment: Strategic face raised cognitive load (not reduced it as predicted), suggesting the intermittent face increased salience rather than reducing cognitive burden
- **Cognitive Aid / Signaling Principle**
  - Components: Instructor face as attention-directing cue to signal when verbal content is independent of slide
  - Research alignment: Did not function as predicted — backfired for verbal-preference learners

## Extraction Notes

This is a high-value paper for Tyler's "should I be on camera?" question. Key actionable signals:

1. **The face does not hurt or help learning on average** — the null learning result from a 12,468-person RCT is unusually strong evidence. Tyler should not expect showing his face to meaningfully increase or decrease how much viewers learn.

2. **The face affects subjective experience** — viewers who see the face report better affect and lower effort. This matters for engagement and return-viewer behavior even if learning outcomes are flat.

3. **35% of viewers find the face distracting** — this is the central tension. In a tutorial context (vs. sociology lecture), the slide/screen content is likely even more cognitively demanding, which may shift this ratio. Worth flagging when comparing with P003 (Beege meta) and P023 (Alemdag).

4. **Strategic showing backfired** — the attempt to "show face only when helpful" increased cognitive load vs. constant face. This is counterintuitive and practically important: don't try to cleverly manage when your face appears; pick a lane.

5. **Individual differences matter most** — the strongest effect in the paper is the verbal/visual preference interaction with attrition. Learners with verbal preference persisted better with constant face. Since YouTube audiences self-select and can stop at any time, this suggests that consistent face presence may reduce drop-off for a meaningful subset of viewers.

6. **MOOC context flag required** — both studies use Coursera sociology MOOCs with text slides. Tyler's Mac/AI tutorials are heavy screen-share with live software interaction. The cognitive load dynamics may differ substantially. Treat effect direction with confidence but magnitude with caution.
