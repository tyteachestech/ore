# Multimedia Design for Learning: An Overview of Reviews With Meta-Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Noetel, M., Griffith, S., Delaney, O., Harris, N. R., Sanders, T., Parker, P., del Pozo Cruz, B., & Lonsdale, C. (2022)
- **Study Type**: Meta-meta-analysis (overview of systematic reviews) — highest tier of evidence synthesis
- **Sample**: 11 largest reviews pooling 808 effect sizes from 66,553 participants (primary analysis); 674 effect sizes dual-extracted from 23 quantitative reviews; 29 reviews included total
- **Coverage**: 12 multimedia design principles, each supported by at least one meta-analysis
- **Journal**: Review of Educational Research
- **DOI**: 10.3102/00346543211052329
- **Note**: Prepublication version reviewed. Published as 2022; submitted 2021.

---

## Key Findings

- **Overall effect of multimedia design principles on learning**: The 11 largest reviews pooled 808 effect sizes from 66,553 participants. Average effect on learning: g=0.38 (95% CI [0.27, 0.49], k=808). Design principle explained essentially all variance in learning outcomes (R²=1.00, p<0.001) — meaning the specific principle used matters enormously; the average hides large within-domain variation.

- **Retention vs. transfer equivalence**: Effects were nearly identical for retention (g=0.42, 95% CI [0.32, 0.52]) and transfer (g=0.43, 95% CI [0.31, 0.54]). Outcome type did not moderate results (p=0.51). Good multimedia design improves both recall and application.

- **Cognitive load reduced**: Overall small improvement from design principles (g=0.22, 95% CI [0.04, 0.40], k=68).

- **Pacing moderator (robust, p=0.02)**: Design principles were more important in system-paced media like lectures/video (g=0.41, 95% CI [0.33, 0.49]) than self-paced media like websites (g=0.27, 95% CI [0.19, 0.35]). Key implication: for YouTube video (system-paced), design choices matter more than for interactive web content.

- **Element interactivity moderator (very robust, p<0.001)**: Design principles were far more important for complex content (g=0.70, 95% CI [0.59, 0.81]) than simple content (g=0.20, 95% CI [0.02, 0.39]). Teaching difficult concepts: design quality is most critical.

- **Prior knowledge did NOT moderate effects (p=0.14)**: Expertise-reversal hypothesis not confirmed. High and low prior-knowledge learners benefited similarly on average. Individual reviews showed inconsistent directions. Education level, subject domain, and presentation format also did not consistently moderate results.

- **Effect size inflation caveat**: Previous non-systematic reviews (e.g., Mayer, 2008) reported d>1.0 for multimedia principles. Meta-analytic estimates here are substantially more conservative (g=0.15–0.78 for principles with adequate evidence). Authors explicitly note this is more consistent with effect sizes observed across psychology and education more broadly.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values) — By Principle

### Captioning Second-Language Video
- Comprehension: g=0.99 (95% CI [0.60, 1.38], k=15; Montero Perez et al., 2013)
- Vocabulary: g=0.87 (95% CI [0.58, 1.15], k=10; Montero Perez et al., 2013)
- **Context restriction**: L2 (second-language) learning only. This is not a general "add captions to your video" principle. For native-language tutorials, verbal redundancy data (below) is the relevant comparison.

### Spatial Contiguity (labels close to what they label, not in a legend)
- Overall: g=0.74 (95% CI [0.67, 0.82], k=46; Ginns, 2006)
- Spatial contiguity specifically: g=0.63 (95% CI [0.55, 0.71], k=58; Schroeder & Cenkci, 2018)

### Temporal Contiguity (related info presented simultaneously, not sequentially)
- g=0.78 (95% CI [0.64, 0.92], k=13; Ginns, 2006)
- Example: one bullet point revealed as you speak to it, not all bullets visible at once

### Signaling (arrows, highlights, color, laser pointer, zoom)
- Learning: g=0.43 (95% CI [0.35, 0.50], k=209; Schneider et al., 2018) — largest k of any principle
- Cognitive load reduction: g=0.25 (95% CI [0.04, 0.45], k=27; Schneider et al., 2018)
- Learning-relevant fixations: g=0.39 (95% CI [0.09, 0.68], k=14; Schneider et al., 2018)
- Motivation: g=0.13 (95% CI [0.04, 0.22], k=13; Schneider et al., 2018)
- Alpizar 2020 replication: d=0.38 overall; d=0.63 specifically from multimedia; d=0.47 for low prior-knowledge learners
- Richter 2016: r=0.17 overall; low prior knowledge r=0.19; high prior knowledge r=-0.08

### Modality Effect (spoken narration + images vs. on-screen text + images)
- Uncorrected: g=0.38 (95% CI [0.33, 0.43], k=86; Reinwein, 2012)
- Publication-bias corrected: g=0.20 (95% CI [0.15, 0.25], k=86; Reinwein, 2012)
- Cognitive load: g=0.62 (95% CI [0.27, 0.96], k=2; Ginns, 2005) — note very small k
- **Recommended value for chapter use**: g=0.20 (corrected). The uncorrected g=0.38 is likely inflated.

### Pleasant Colors and Anthropomorphics (Emotional Design)
- Learning: g=0.35 (95% CI [0.29, 0.42], k=45; Brom et al., 2018)
- Intrinsic motivation: g=0.25 (95% CI [0.17, 0.34], k=23; Brom et al., 2018)
- Perceived difficulty reduction: g=0.21 (95% CI [0.08, 0.33], k=14; Brom et al., 2018)

### Segmentation (breaking content into discrete chunks)
- Overall learning: g=0.34 (95% CI [0.30, 0.38], k=123; Rey et al., 2019)
- Instructor-controlled segmenting: g=0.41 (95% CI [0.32, 0.50], k=32) — stronger than learner-controlled
- Learner-controlled segmenting: g=0.20 (95% CI [0.11, 0.28], k=32)
- Cognitive load reduction: g=0.23 (95% CI [0.13, 0.33], k=20; Rey et al., 2019)
- Retention: g=0.32; Transfer: g=0.36
- Time-on-task increase with segmenting: g=0.92 (95% CI [0.82, 1.02], k=19) — segmented content takes longer but produces better learning

### Personalization (conversational vs. formal language)
- Learning: g=0.33 (95% CI [0.23, 0.44], k=55; Ginns et al., 2013)
- Cognitive load reduction: g=0.62 (95% CI [0.13, 1.11], k=4; Ginns et al., 2013) — very small k, extremely wide CI

### Removing Seductive Details (Coherence Principle)
- Overall learning: g=0.33 (95% CI [0.18, 0.48], k=68; Sundararajan & Adesope, 2020)
- Persistent on-screen seductive details removed: g=0.43 (95% CI [0.29, 0.57], k=47)
- Transient seductive details removed: g=0.12 (95% CI [-0.33, 0.57], k=18) — not significantly different from zero

### Animation (Dynamic vs. Static Graphics)
- Overall: g=0.23 (95% CI [0.12, 0.33]; Berney & Bétrancourt, 2016; k=61 studies)
- Meaningful/representational animation: g=0.40 (95% CI [0.34, 0.46], k=59; Höffler & Leutner, 2007)
- Decorational animation: g=-0.05 (95% CI [-0.17, 0.07], k=17) — null effect; animations must communicate meaning

### Pedagogical Agents (On-screen character or artificial teacher)
- Overall: g=0.19 (95% CI [0.12, 0.27], k=43; Schroeder et al., 2013)
- Agents that gesture/signal to important content: g=0.28 (95% CI [0.01, 0.54], k=7; Davis, 2018)
- 3D agents: g=0.11 (95% CI [-0.06, 0.27], k=21) — not significant
- 2D agents: g=0.38 (95% CI [0.16, 0.60], k=11)
- Learner preference for signaling agents over non-signaling: g=0.44 (95% CI [0.08, 0.81], k=8)

### Verbal Redundancy (same content in both audio and on-screen text)
- Overall: g=0.15 (95% CI [0.08, 0.22], k=57; Adesope & Nesbit, 2012)
- Adding text to audio (text on screen alongside narration): g=0.29 (95% CI [0.20, 0.39], k=34) — positive
- Adding audio to text (narration over a reading passage): g=-0.04 (95% CI [-0.14, 0.06], k=23) — null
- Cognitive load for verbal redundancy: g=0.13 (95% CI [-0.05, 0.31], k=10) — marginal

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Dual-channel processing**: Core mechanism from CTML (Mayer). Visual channel (images) and auditory channel (speech) can be loaded simultaneously without interfering. Best use: spoken narration + meaningful visuals. Problem case: on-screen text + images forces visual channel to do two jobs at once.

- **Extraneous load reduction**: Principles like signaling, removing seductive details, and contiguity work by eliminating cognitive effort spent on non-learning tasks (finding connections between distant elements, filtering irrelevant material). This frees capacity for actual learning.

- **Intrinsic load management**: Segmentation, personalization, and self-pacing work by making the task itself more manageable — allowing consolidation between chunks before moving forward.

- **Germane load promotion**: Anthropomorphics and emotional design may work by increasing engagement, converting freed cognitive capacity into deeper (germane) processing rather than disengagement.

- **Pacing moderator (robust, p=0.02)**: System-paced (video, lectures) > self-paced (websites) for design principle impact. YouTube is system-paced — this is the category where design choices matter most.

- **Element interactivity moderator (very robust, p<0.001)**: Complex content benefits far more from good design (g=0.70 vs. g=0.20). High element interactivity = many interacting concepts that must be mentally connected. Tutorials on complex software workflows fall in this category.

- **Prior knowledge: NOT a reliable moderator (p=0.14)**: Expertise-reversal hypothesis not confirmed at this level. Individual reviews showed inconsistent directions (some found larger effects for novices, others for experts). Net result: cannot reliably predict that design principles will help novices more than experts.

- **Education level, subject domain, presentation format**: None were consistent moderators. The principles appear to generalize across these boundaries.

## Study Design Details

- **Design**: Overview of systematic reviews (meta-meta-analysis)
- **Inclusion**: 29 reviews comparing multimedia WITH vs. WITHOUT a specific design feature; outcomes = learning or cognitive load
- **Exclusion**: Reviews comparing multimedia vs. no multimedia; reviews reporting only health, satisfaction, or engagement outcomes
- **Search**: ERIC, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science; January 2020 + December 2020 update
- **Independence**: Most comprehensive review selected per design principle to avoid double-counting primary studies across reviews
- **Quality assessment**: 7-criteria tool applied to 29 reviews. Key finding: only 1 of 29 reviews formally assessed primary study quality (Adesope & Nesbit, 2012). Duplicate screening rare (2/29). Most assessed publication bias but used suboptimal methods (Trim-and-Fill, Egger's).

---

## Limitations

- **No primary study quality assessment in most included reviews**: Only 1/29 reviews formally assessed quality of underlying studies. Authors explicitly flag "garbage in, garbage out" risk — bias in the 1,189+ underlying primary studies propagates into these estimates.

- **Cannot ensure analytical consistency across reviews**: Different meta-analytic decisions (moderator coding, publication bias approach, outcome operationalization) across 29 reviews could create systematic distortions. Authors could not fully control for this.

- **Publication bias assessment methods were inconsistent and often underpowered**: The modality effect drops from g=0.38 to g=0.20 when corrected for publication bias — suggesting other principles may be similarly inflated. Most reviews used Trim-and-Fill or Egger's rather than selection models.

- **Mostly laboratory and formal educational settings**: Generalizability to informal YouTube tutorial contexts (voluntary learners, no assessment, ambient distraction) is assumed but not established. All primary studies involve students in assessment-oriented settings.

- **Engagement, satisfaction, and viewer retention excluded as outcomes**: By design — reviews reporting only these outcomes were excluded. This paper cannot directly inform YouTube viewer retention, click-through, or audience growth. Design principles are validated for learning outcomes only.

- **Worked examples, retrieval practice, interleaving not covered**: No systematic review meeting inclusion criteria existed for these principles at time of search. The paper does not speak to them — absence from this paper is not evidence of absence of effect.

- **No prospective registration for most included reviews**: Selective reporting within reviews possible.

- **K-12, university, and adult professional learning pooled**: Education level did not statistically moderate effects, but underlying contexts differ substantially from YouTube informal learning.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — primarily higher-education (university students), some K-12, some adult professional learning; no creator-economy/YouTube studies
- **Relevance to Tyler's async video tutorials**: High. Video is explicitly system-paced — the category where design principles showed strongest effects (g=0.41 vs. g=0.27 for self-paced). Most principles directly map to tutorial design decisions.
- **Context flags**: Research comes from formal educational settings, not YouTube. The cognitive architecture principles derive from working memory limits, not institutional context, which supports generalizability — but informal viewer behavior may differ.
- **Red flags for exclusion**: None.

> **MOOC/Higher-Ed context flag:** The majority of underlying primary studies come from formal educational settings (K-12 through university). Tyler's context (informal YouTube, voluntary learners, self-selected audience) differs in learner commitment and institutional framing. However, the principles are grounded in cognitive architecture (working memory limits, dual-channel processing) rather than institutional context — this supports cautious generalization. The strongest context-transfer argument: video is system-paced in both settings, and that is where design principles showed their largest effects.

## Conclusions

"We found robust support for many multimedia design principles informed by the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning and Cognitive Load Theory. Specifically, we found that interventions that leveraged our two channels of information processing (visual and auditory) improved learning (e.g., captioning second-language video; modality effect). We also found benefits for strategies that reduced extraneous cognitive load (e.g., contiguity, signaling, segmentation, personalisation, removing seductive details). Many such interventions have robust support for their causal models, with randomised experiments showing improvements in both learning and hypothesised mediators (e.g., cognitive load, eye movements). While the quality of the reviews was inconsistent, the consistency of the results and strength of the effects provide strong support for these models of multimedia learning. Results show good multimedia design can have a transformative effect on a range of educational outcomes. As a result, mastering these design principles is a critical competency for all educators."

## Named Frameworks

- **Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)** — Mayer (2001, 2009)
  - Core assumption: Dual-channel (visual + auditory), limited capacity, active processing
  - Principles covered in this paper: contiguity, signaling, modality, segmenting, personalization, coherence (seductive details), verbal redundancy, pedagogical agents, animation, captioning
  - Research alignment: Principles reducing extraneous load (contiguity, signaling, coherence) and leveraging dual-channel (modality) show strongest effects

- **Cognitive Load Theory (CLT)** — Sweller (1988)
  - Three loads: intrinsic (task complexity), extraneous (poor design), germane (schema construction)
  - Research alignment: Element interactivity (proxy for intrinsic load) moderates design principle effects; extraneous load reduction is the mechanism for most significant principles

**Quality note**: This is the highest-level evidence synthesis available for multimedia design principles. The effect sizes here are more credible than values from individual meta-analyses, and substantially more credible than the d>1.0 figures reported in Mayer's non-systematic reviews.

**Key design insight for Tyler**: The pacing moderator (system-paced g=0.41 vs. self-paced g=0.27) directly validates that design principles matter more for video than for interactive content.
