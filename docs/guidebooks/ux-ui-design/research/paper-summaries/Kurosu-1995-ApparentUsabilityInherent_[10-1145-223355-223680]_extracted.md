# Apparent Usability vs. Inherent Usability

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Kurosu, M. & Kashimura, K. (1995). CHI '95 Conference Companion.
- **Study Type**: Experimental (correlational design)
- **Sample**: n=252 raters; 26 interface stimuli generated by n=26 designers/engineers/secretaries
- **DOI**: 10.1145/223355.223680
- **PMID**: Not available

## Key Findings

- **Aesthetic-usability correlation**: Ratings of apparent usability (perceived ease of use) correlated with beauty ratings at r=0.589 across 26 ATM/cash dispenser screen layouts. This is the primary founding empirical result of what became known as the "aesthetic-usability effect."

- **Inherent usability does NOT predict apparent usability**: All seven operationalized usability strategies showed weak correlations with apparent usability ratings. The strongest was "familiarity of key arrangement" (r=0.730, but this is a nominal/contingency coefficient -- see notes), while spatial and operational strategies ranged from r=-0.306 to r=0.137.

- **Users substitute aesthetics for function**: When asked to rate how easy an interface looks to use, participants' responses tracked beauty far more closely than any objective design feature intended to improve actual usability.

- **Cross-group replication**: Two independent student groups (design school, n=156; university psychology, n=96) showed high agreement -- inter-rater r=0.679 for apparent usability, r=0.783 for beauty -- allowing data merge.

- **Dissociation exists**: Fig. 2 in the paper illustrates four quadrant examples: high usability + high beauty, high usability + low beauty, low usability + low beauty, low usability + high beauty. The quadrant of low usability + high beauty (aesthetically appealing but functionally poor) demonstrates the divergence is real, not artifactual.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Apparent usability ~ Beauty**: r=0.589, N=252
- **Apparent usability ~ Inherent usability strategies** (all reported as correlation or contingency coefficients):
  - Glance sequence (main display position): r=0.000
  - Familiarity of key arrangement: C=0.730 (contingency coefficient, nominal scale -- not directly comparable to r)
  - Grouping (number of key groups): r=0.075
  - Operation sequence-1 (thousands key arrangement): C=0.113 (nominal)
  - Hand dominance (key block placement): r=-0.127
  - Operation sequence-2 (Yen key placement): r=-0.306
  - Safety strategy (cancel key distance): r=0.137

Note: No p-values or confidence intervals were reported. Effect sizes are raw correlation coefficients only. This is a 1995 conference short paper (2 pages); full statistical reporting was not standard.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Proposed mechanism**: Users cannot directly observe inherent usability before use. Aesthetics are immediately visible. The visual appearance of an interface becomes a proxy cue for ease of use -- users infer function from form.
- **Moderators**: Not formally tested. The study used a single domain (ATM/cash dispenser layout), single culture (Japanese), and student raters. No moderator analyses conducted.
- **Designer vs. non-designer distinction**: Stimuli were generated by a mixed group (GUI designers, industrial designers, engineers, secretaries) but raters were students only. No breakdown of rater type.

## Study Design Details

- **Stimulus generation**: 26 participants arranged identical graphical elements (numeric keys, display areas, special keys) into ATM screen layouts. Hard copies used as stimuli.
- **Evaluation task**: 252 participants rated each layout on two 10-point scales: (1) how easy it looks to use (apparent usability), (2) how beautiful it looks.
- **Inherent usability operationalization**: Seven strategies derived from interviews with interface designers (glance sequence, familiarity, grouping, operation sequence, hand dominance, safety) -- each measured as distance in cm or nominal key arrangement type.
- **Control/Comparison**: No control condition; correlational design comparing aesthetic ratings to inherent usability measures.
- **Follow-up**: None -- single-session rating task.
- **Blinding**: Not reported. Raters saw static screen layouts; no interaction with actual systems.

## Limitations

- **No actual usability testing**: "Inherent usability" was operationalized via designer heuristics (distance measurements, key arrangement conventions), not measured through task performance. The paper never validates whether these strategies actually improve performance.
- **Single domain**: ATM cash dispenser screens only. Generalizability to GUIs, web interfaces, or app design is inferential.
- **Single culture**: Japanese sample (Hitachi, Tokyo). Cultural aesthetic preferences may not generalize.
- **Student raters**: 252 raters were students, not representative users of ATMs or the broader public.
- **Short paper format**: 2-page CHI companion paper. No confidence intervals, no p-values, limited methodological detail.
- **Familiarity confound**: The "familiarity" strategy (telephone vs. calculator key layout) showed the highest inherent usability coefficient (C=0.730), but this may itself be aesthetic/learned preference rather than a clean inherent usability measure.
- **No causal claim possible**: Correlational design; cannot determine whether beauty causes perceived usability or a common underlying factor (e.g., orderliness, balance) drives both.
- **26 stimuli is a small N for a correlational study**: Statistical power for r=0.589 with k=26 is modest.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Students (design school + university psychology), Japanese
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: N/A -- this is a design perception study, not behavior change
- **Relevance to UX/UI guidebook**: High. This is the founding empirical paper for the aesthetic-usability effect -- essential context for chapters on visual design, first impressions, and interface aesthetics.
- **Red flags**: None for this use case. Student population is appropriate for perception rating tasks.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that apparent usability is more strongly correlated with aesthetic judgments (r=0.589) than with any measure of inherent usability. They recommend that "interface designers should strive not only to improve the inherent usability but also to brush up the apparent usability or the aesthetic aspect of the interface." This is the first published empirical demonstration of what later researchers named the aesthetic-usability effect.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Apparent Usability vs. Inherent Usability distinction**
  - Apparent usability: How easy an interface looks to use (pre-use judgment)
  - Inherent usability: Actual ease of use based on functional design properties
  - Key insight: These are dissociable -- aesthetic appearance is the primary driver of apparent usability, not functional design quality

## Extraction Notes

This is a 2-page CHI companion short paper -- not a full research article. The statistical reporting is minimal by modern standards (no CIs, no p-values, no power analysis). However, the core finding (r=0.589 between beauty and apparent usability) is clear and has been extensively replicated and built upon, most notably by Tractinsky et al. (2000) who found even stronger effects (r=0.93) in a direct replication with Israeli users.

For the guidebook, this paper's value is historical/foundational: it established the phenomenon. Tractinsky (2000) is the stronger empirical anchor. Use this paper to credit the origin of the construct, then rely on Tractinsky and subsequent meta-analyses for effect magnitude claims.

One interpretive caution: the paper conflates "aesthetics drive perceived usability" with "aesthetics matter for design." The practical implication for a builder is not that function doesn't matter -- it's that a functionally excellent interface that looks ugly will be perceived as hard to use before anyone tries it, suppressing adoption.
