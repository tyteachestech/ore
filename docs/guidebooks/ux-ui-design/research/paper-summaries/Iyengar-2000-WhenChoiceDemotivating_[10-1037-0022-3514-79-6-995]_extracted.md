# When Choice is Demotivating: Can One Desire Too Much of a Good Thing?

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Iyengar, S.S. & Lepper, M.R. (2000)
- **Study Type**: Multi-experiment (2 naturalistic field experiments + 1 laboratory experiment)
- **Sample**: Study 1: N≈502 grocery shoppers observed (249 stopped); Study 2: N=193 university students; Study 3: N=134 university students
- **Total across studies**: ~879 participants observed/enrolled
- **DOI**: 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995
- **Journal**: Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Vol. 79, No. 6, pp. 995-1006
- **Context**: The foundational "jam study" establishing choice overload as an empirical phenomenon across field and lab settings

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Jam study (Study 1), Purchase behavior**: Consumers exposed to 6 jam flavors were ~10x more likely to purchase than those exposed to 24 flavors.
  - Limited choice (6 jams): 31/104 = 29.9% purchased
  - Extensive choice (24 jams): 4/145 = 2.8% purchased
  - Chi-square: χ²(1, N=249) = 32.34, p < .0001
  - Note: No effect size (Cohen's d / OR) was reported; effect is expressed as raw proportions.

- **Finding 2 — Jam study (Study 1), Initial attraction**: More consumers initially stopped at the extensive-choice booth.
  - Extensive (24 jams): 145/242 = 60% stopped
  - Limited (6 jams): 104/260 = 40% stopped
  - Chi-square: χ²(1, N=502) = 19.89, p < .001
  - This is the "appeal-vs-action" dissociation: more choice attracts more attention but converts less.

- **Finding 3 — Essay study (Study 2), Completion rate**: Students given 6 topics completed the optional assignment more often than those given 30 topics.
  - Limited choice (6 topics): 52/70 = 74.3% completed
  - Extensive choice (30 topics): 74/123 = 60.2% completed
  - Chi-square: χ²(1, N=193) = 3.93, p < .05

- **Finding 4 — Essay study (Study 2), Essay quality**: Essays written by limited-choice students were rated higher on both content and form.
  - Content: Limited M=8.13 (SD=0.95) vs. Extensive M=7.79 (SD=0.91), F(1,124)=4.18, p < .05
  - Form: Limited M=8.04 (SD=1.33) vs. Extensive M=7.59 (SD=1.02), F(1,124)=4.64, p < .03
  - Overall grade composite: Limited M=8.09 (SD=1.05) vs. Extensive M=7.69 (SD=0.82), F(1,124)=5.65, p < .02
  - Essays were graded blind by two raters unaware of condition (inter-rater r=.70 for content, r=.89 for form)

- **Finding 5 — Chocolate study (Study 3), Post-sampling satisfaction**: Limited-choice participants reported higher satisfaction after tasting their chosen chocolate.
  - Limited choice: M=6.28 (SD=0.54)
  - Extensive choice: M=5.46 (SD=0.82)
  - No-choice control: M=4.92 (SD=0.98)
  - F(1,122) = 28.02, p < .0001
  - Tukey comparisons: limited > extensive > no-choice (all pairwise differences significant)

- **Finding 6 — Chocolate study (Study 3), Purchase behavior**: Limited-choice participants were 4x more likely to choose chocolates as compensation.
  - Limited choice: 48% chose chocolates over $5 cash
  - Extensive choice: 12% chose chocolates over $5 cash
  - No-choice control: 10% chose chocolates over $5 cash
  - Chi-square: χ²(2, N=134) = 21.84, p < .0001
  - Extensive choice and no-choice did not differ from each other on this measure.

- **Finding 7 — Chocolate study (Study 3), Simultaneous enjoyment and frustration**: Extensive-choice participants reported enjoying the decision MORE but also finding it more difficult and frustrating.
  - Enjoyment: Extensive M=6.02 (SD=0.75) vs. Limited M=4.72 (SD=1.36), F(1,132)=47.01, p < .0001
  - Difficulty: Extensive M=4.45 (SD=1.79) vs. Limited M=3.30 (SD=1.49), F(1,132)=16.38, p < .0001
  - Frustration: Extensive M=3.10 (SD=1.77) vs. Limited M=2.24 (SD=1.72), F(1,123)=7.61, p < .007
  - Note: Enjoyment and difficulty were uncorrelated (r=.11, ns); difficulty and frustration were correlated (r=.36, p<.0001)

- **Finding 8 — Satisficing vs. optimizing hypothesis (Study 3, NOT supported)**: No differences by condition in satisficing vs. optimizing goals, anticipated satisfaction, felt informatedness, or default-option selection.
  - Satisficing confidence: F(1,94)=0.15, ns
  - Optimizing confidence: F(1,94)=0.09, ns
  - Anticipated satisfaction: F(1,132)=0.61, ns
  - Informatedness: F(1,132)=0.14, ns
  - Default-option tendency: F(1,132)=0.24, ns

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

No standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d, OR, r) are reported. All effects are expressed as proportions, means, F-statistics, and chi-square values. The authors did not calculate standardized effect sizes.

**Study 1 (Jam):**
- Purchase rate: 29.9% vs. 2.8% (limited vs. extensive), χ²(1, N=249)=32.34, p<.0001
- Booth approach rate: 60% vs. 40% (extensive vs. limited), χ²(1, N=502)=19.89, p<.001
- Number of jams tasted: M=1.50 vs. M=1.38 (extensive vs. limited), F(1,245)=0.83, ns

**Study 2 (Essays):**
- Completion: 74% vs. 60% (limited vs. extensive), χ²(1, N=193)=3.93, p<.05
- Content quality: M=8.13 vs. M=7.79, F(1,124)=4.18, p<.05
- Form quality: M=8.04 vs. M=7.59, F(1,124)=4.64, p<.03
- Overall grade: M=8.09 vs. M=7.69, F(1,124)=5.65, p<.02

**Study 3 (Chocolates):**
- Enjoyment of process: M=6.02 vs. M=4.72, F(1,132)=47.01, p<.0001
- Difficulty: M=4.45 vs. M=3.30, F(1,132)=16.38, p<.0001
- Frustration: M=3.10 vs. M=2.24, F(1,123)=7.61, p<.007
- Post-sampling satisfaction: M=6.28 vs. M=5.46 vs. M=4.92 (limited, extensive, no-choice), F(1,122)=28.02, p<.0001
- Purchase/compensation: 48% vs. 12% vs. 10% (limited, extensive, no-choice), χ²(2, N=134)=21.84, p<.0001
- Choosing time: M=8.91s (SD=6.02) vs. M=24.36s (SD=12.99), F(1,131)=77.02, p<.0001
- Perception of choice quantity: M=3.61 vs. M=4.88 on "too few to too many" scale, F(1,132)=43.68, p<.0001

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Proposed mechanism (supported): Increased responsibility → frustration → disengagement**: Extensive choices create enhanced feelings of responsibility for finding the optimal option. This heightens frustration during choice-making and later regret after choosing, even though initial enjoyment is also elevated. Authors suggest extensive-choice participants disengage from post-choice psychological rationalization processes (Gilovich & Medvec, 1995), leaving them unable to enhance their satisfaction with the chosen option.

- **Proposed mechanism (not supported): Satisficing vs. optimizing shift**: Authors initially hypothesized that extensive-choice would push people toward satisficing (accepting "good enough") while limited-choice would induce optimizing. Study 3 found no differences — both conditions showed satisficing patterns equally.

- **Moderator 1 — Prior preferences / preference-matching contexts**: Authors explicitly note that choice overload likely does NOT occur when consumers enter with strong pre-existing preferences. In preference-matching contexts (e.g., looking for a specific item on a menu), more options should help. Studies were designed to minimize this by using unfamiliar/exotic products and prescreening for non-habitual consumers.

- **Moderator 2 — Expertise**: People with domain expertise effectively narrow the subjective choice set even when objective options are large. The "paradox" is that the only people truly comfortable with extensive choice are those who experience many options as few.

- **Moderator 3 — Stakes/objective correctness**: Authors predict choice overload effects are amplified when choices involve objective "right/wrong" answers (e.g., medical decisions, financial instruments) vs. purely subjective preferences. Present studies used subjective preference tasks specifically to provide a conservative test.

- **Moderator 4 — Self-generated vs. externally imposed choices**: Speculative — authors note this was not tested. People may create excessive options for themselves that they then struggle with.

- **Moderator 5 — Choosing for self vs. others**: Speculative — authors cite evidence that decision aversion is stronger when choosing for others. Extensive-choice overload may be amplified in that context.

## Study Design Details

**Study 1 (Jam Field Experiment):**
- **Setting**: Draeger's Supermarket, Menlo Park, CA — an upscale grocery known for extraordinary selection (~300 jam varieties)
- **Manipulation**: Tasting booth displayed either 6 or 24 exotic Wilkin & Sons jam flavors; displays rotated hourly, counterbalanced across 2 Saturdays
- **Dependent measures**: (1) Proportion of passersby who stopped; (2) jam purchase within 1 week via coupon redemption
- **Control**: Solicitation friendliness verified equivalent across conditions (M=4.5, r=.90 inter-rater agreement); no gender differences by condition
- **Key design decision**: Common flavors (strawberry, raspberry) excluded to reduce strong prior preferences; limited set (6) selected to be neither most nor least preferred via pre-survey of 30 Stanford undergrads
- **Limitation on design**: Consumers could self-select into conditions based on which booth they passed, though booth hours were rotated

**Study 2 (Essay Field Experiment):**
- **Setting**: Stanford University introductory social psychology class, N=193 students (70 limited, 123 extensive)
- **Manipulation**: Extra-credit opportunity with either 6 or 30 essay topics on assigned movie
- **Yoked design**: Every 6-topic assignment represented a subset of the 30 topics; 5 versions of the 6-topic list counterbalanced across all 30 items
- **Grading**: Two blind graduate student raters on 10-point content + form scales; inter-rater r=.70 (content), r=.89 (form)
- **Assignment to condition**: Section-level (not individual), so different Ns (70 vs. 123)
- **Attrition**: 4 students dropped the class and were excluded

**Study 3 (Chocolate Laboratory Experiment):**
- **Setting**: Columbia University, N=134 (33 limited, 34 extensive, 67 no-choice control)
- **Stimuli**: Godiva chocolates (6 vs. 30 flavors), labeled with official names
- **No-choice control**: Yoked design — same chocolates chosen by choice participants were offered to no-choice participants from a box of 8 identical chocolates
- **Dependent measures**: Decision-making questionnaire (before tasting), satisfaction questionnaire (after tasting), and behavioral compensation choice ($5 cash vs. $5 Godiva box, administered by a second experimenter unaware of condition)
- **Prescreening**: Only participants who liked chocolate and consumed Godiva "never" or "occasionally" were recruited (~92% of approached)
- **Experimenter bias control**: 40 sessions videotaped and rated by external blind raters; friendliness did not vary by condition (r=.69, F(2,36)=.01, ns)
- **All sessions randomized**: Participants assigned at random to conditions

## Limitations

- **No standardized effect sizes reported**: All comparisons use raw proportions, means, and F/chi-square statistics. Cohen's d or odds ratios are not calculated, making cross-study comparison and meta-analytic integration imprecise. (The Scheibehenne et al. 2010 meta-analysis later coded this paper's effect sizes retroactively.)

- **Narrow operationalization**: "Limited" = exactly 6 options in all studies; "extensive" = 24 or 30. The threshold for overload onset is not tested — this paper cannot tell us if the effect appears at 8, 10, or 15 options.

- **Non-representative samples**: Study 1 uses upscale grocery shoppers in Menlo Park (affluent, primarily middle-aged Caucasian women); Studies 2 and 3 use Stanford/Columbia undergrads. Generalizability to other demographics is untested.

- **Self-selection confound in Study 1**: Shoppers chose which booth to approach based on the visible display. The extensive-choice booth may have attracted different types of consumers (e.g., novelty seekers) than the limited-choice booth. Study 2 addressed this by using section-level assignment.

- **Single product categories per study**: Each study uses one product type. Generalizability to other domains (services, software options, menu design) is inferred but not tested.

- **No mechanism directly confirmed**: Study 3 ruled out the satisficing/optimizing explanation but the "heightened responsibility → regret" mechanism was supported only indirectly via affect measures, not through mediation analysis.

- **No follow-up measures**: Satisfaction was assessed immediately post-sampling. Longer-term regret or post-purchase evaluation were not tracked.

- **Demand characteristics in Study 3**: Participants received chocolates as part of a compensation decision framed as a "marketing research study." This framing may have inflated or deflated chocolate appeal across conditions.

- **Conflict of interest**: None disclosed.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — grocery shoppers (general adult, Study 1), introductory psychology students at Stanford (Study 2), Columbia undergrads (Study 3)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: High — the choice overload phenomenon directly informs how to design option sets in apps, onboarding flows, and feature menus to avoid demotivating users
- **Red flags**: None; no clinical population, no compulsive behavior, tasks involve subjective preferences (jam, chocolates, essay topics) — low-stakes analogs to digital product contexts
- **Note on ecological validity**: The jam study is particularly high in external validity as a field experiment with real purchase decisions. The lab chocolate study sacrifices some ecological validity but gains experimental control. Essay study sits in between.

## Conclusions

"The three studies described in this report demonstrate for the first time the possibility that, although having more choices might appear desirable, it may sometimes have detrimental consequences for human motivation." The core paradox: extensive choice is initially more attractive but subsequently undermines motivation to act and reduces satisfaction with the choice made. "Having unlimited options, then, can lead people to be more dissatisfied with the choices they make." Authors explicitly note the effect may be amplified in high-stakes domains (medical, financial) where there are perceived "objectively correct" answers. The boundary condition is clear: in preference-matching contexts (when people already know what they want), more choice should still help.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Choice Overload Hypothesis**
  - Components: (1) Extensive choice is initially more appealing than limited choice, (2) but extensive choice subsequently reduces motivation to choose, satisfaction with chosen option, and actual purchasing/action behavior, (3) while simultaneously increasing enjoyment AND frustration of the decision process
  - Theoretical basis: Anticipated regret, heightened responsibility, cognitive burden of large choice sets
  - What was NOT supported: The satisficing/optimizing distinction as a mediating mechanism

## Quotable Passages

> "Nearly 30% (31) of the consumers in the limited-choice condition subsequently purchased a jar of Wilkin & Sons jam; in contrast, only 3% (4) of the consumers in the extensive-choice condition did so." (Study 1 Results, p. 998)

> "Participants in the extensive-choice condition reported experiencing the decision-making process as being simultaneously more enjoyable, more difficult, and more frustrating. Later, after actually sampling their chocolates, extensive-choice participants reported being more dissatisfied and having more regret about the choices they had made than did limited-choice participants." (General Discussion, p. 1003)

> "The ultimate paradox might be that the only circumstance in which choosers are truly comfortable with extensive choices is when, because of the chooser's previous experience, these choices are perceived as limited in number." (General Discussion, p. 1005)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Contexts where choosers do NOT have strong pre-established preferences; subjective preference tasks; contexts where options are comparable in attractiveness and difficulty; consumers with low domain expertise; cases where the "correct" choice is ambiguous or purely personal
- **Does NOT apply to**: Preference-matching contexts (when the shopper already knows what they want); expert choosers who effectively compress large option sets; contexts with strong stakes where choosing fewer options would mean missing critical alternatives (e.g., treatment options, legal scenarios)
- **Unknown/untested**: The precise threshold at which overload begins (is it 7? 10? 12?); effects in digital/screen-based interfaces; effects with non-hedonic products; long-term choice regret; whether choice overload generalizes to non-Western cultures (Iyengar later found cultural variation in a 2002 Science paper, not represented here); whether self-generated extensive choices produce the same effect as experimenter-imposed ones

## Extraction Notes

This is the foundational paper for choice overload — historically influential but methodologically limited by modern standards. The effect sizes are large in Study 1 (30% vs. 3% purchase rate is a 10x difference) but no standardized effect sizes are reported, which contributed to replication uncertainty and subsequent meta-analytic ambiguity.

The Scheibehenne et al. (2010) meta-analysis (also in the ux-design paper inventory) directly re-examined this paper's findings alongside other choice overload studies and found no reliable mean effect across studies (d=0.02), though with enormous variance. The Iyengar & Lepper (2000) Study 1 jam result is often cited as the iconic finding, but it is a single field experiment with N=249 at the tasting booth, no pre-registration, and self-selection into conditions partially controlled only by hourly rotation.

The "inflation_flag: choice-architecture" applies because this domain shows substantial publication bias and file-drawer effects in subsequent meta-analyses. The 30% vs. 3% purchase rate difference, while dramatic, reflects real grocery behavior and has practical significance for product designers regardless of the exact effect magnitude. Use with appropriate caveats about generalizability and the possibility of null results in replication attempts.

The three-way comparison in Study 3 (limited > extensive > no-choice on satisfaction; limited >> extensive ≈ no-choice on purchasing behavior) is particularly useful for guidebook use: it shows that extensive choice collapses to essentially the same behavioral outcome as having no choice at all, while satisfaction ordering differs. This asymmetry has theoretical importance.
