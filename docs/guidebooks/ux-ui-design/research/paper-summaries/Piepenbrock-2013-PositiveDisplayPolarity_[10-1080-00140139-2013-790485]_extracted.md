# Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund, & Buchner, 2013
- **Study Type**: Experimental (between-subjects, 2x2 design)
- **Sample**: n=169 total (84 younger adults age 18-33, 85 older adults age 60-85); normal or corrected-to-normal vision; clinical cataract and major ocular pathology excluded
- **Published in**: Ergonomics, Vol. 56, No. 7, pp. 1116-1124
- **DOI**: 10.1080/00140139.2013.790485

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Visual acuity, younger adults**: Positive polarity produced substantially better visual acuity than negative polarity.
  - Effect size: d=2.17, p<0.01
  - Task: Landolt C optotypes on 24-inch TFT-LCD; between-subjects manipulation

- **Finding 2 — Visual acuity, older adults**: Positive polarity also produced better visual acuity for older adults, though the advantage was smaller.
  - Effect size: d=0.58, p=0.01
  - The age x polarity interaction was significant (F(1,163)=19.80, p<0.01, eta2=0.11), confirming the smaller-but-present advantage

- **Finding 3 — Visual acuity overall polarity effect**: Main effect of polarity was large across the full sample.
  - F(1,163)=69.31, p<0.01, eta2=0.30

- **Finding 4 — Proofreading performance**: Positive polarity produced better error detection in a proofreading task for both age groups.
  - Main effect of polarity: F(1,165)=9.92, p<0.01, eta2=0.06
  - Main effect of age (younger better than older): F(1,165)=38.95, p<0.01, eta2=0.19
  - Interaction between polarity and age: NOT significant (F(1,165)=0.60, p=0.44, eta2<0.01) — the polarity advantage did not differ by age group in proofreading

- **Finding 5 — Reading rate**: No effect of polarity on words read per text — the accuracy gains were not purchased by slower reading.
  - Main effect of polarity: F(1,165)=0.51, p=0.48, eta2<0.01
  - No age effect, no interaction

- **Finding 6 — Subjective well-being and physical discomfort**: No significant differences between polarity conditions on mood, eyestrain, or headache. One marginal interaction (muscle strain/back pain x age x polarity) was treated as a chance finding.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Visual acuity, younger adults, polarity effect**: d=2.17
- **Visual acuity, older adults, polarity effect**: d=0.58
- **Visual acuity, main effect of polarity (full sample)**: F(1,163)=69.31, p<0.01, eta2=0.30
- **Visual acuity, main effect of age**: F(1,163)=42.91, p<0.01, eta2=0.21
- **Visual acuity, age x polarity interaction**: F(1,163)=19.80, p<0.01, eta2=0.11
- **Proofreading, main effect of polarity**: F(1,165)=9.92, p<0.01, eta2=0.06
- **Proofreading, main effect of age**: F(1,165)=38.95, p<0.01, eta2=0.19
- **Proofreading, age x polarity interaction**: F(1,165)=0.60, p=0.44, eta2<0.01 (not significant)
- **Reading rate**: all F's non-significant, eta2<0.01

Note: No 95% CIs reported; F and d statistics only.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Primary mechanism — luminance**: Positive polarity displays are brighter (white background ~350 cd/m2 vs. black background ~1 cd/m2). Higher luminance causes pupillary constriction, which reduces spherical aberrations and increases depth of field, improving retinal image quality. This mechanism is supported by a prior study (Buchner, Mayr, & Brandt 2009) showing that the polarity advantage disappears when overall luminance is equated between positive and negative conditions.

- **Moderator — age**: The positive polarity advantage on visual acuity was smaller for older adults (d=0.58) than younger adults (d=2.17). Authors attribute this partial attenuation to intraocular light scatter (degraded lens and vitreous humour) causing mild glare in bright displays. However, the advantage was NOT attenuated for proofreading — suggesting that real-world reading tasks (which involve cognition above the sensory threshold) are less sensitive to this glare effect than pure acuity threshold tests.

- **Moderator — pathological vision impairment (not tested here, excluded)**: Adults with clinically significant cataract, AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, or uveitis were excluded. Prior literature suggests these populations may prefer negative polarity. This study's conclusions apply only to older adults with normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Between-subjects polarity assignment. Each participant saw all stimuli in one polarity only (positive or negative). This avoids the performance-effort trade-off that confounds within-subject polarity designs.
- **Displays**: 24-inch Apple iMac TFT-LCD; white luminance 350 cd/m2, black luminance 1 cd/m2; Michelson contrast ~1.00 for both conditions
- **Visual acuity task**: Landolt C optotypes (FrACT software); chin rest at 184 cm; 30 trials per participant; forced-choice 8-orientation response
- **Proofreading task**: 28 texts of 250 words each (9th/10th grade level); 10 pt Helvetica; 14 deliberate errors per text; 50 sec per text; chin rest at 50 cm
- **Blinding**: Not relevant — polarity is visually obvious
- **Ambient illumination**: Deliberately matched to display conditions (positive: 16 lx at eye; negative: 0 lx at eye at acuity distance; 117 lx vs. 3 lx at reading distance)
- **Cognitive screening**: All participants passed dementia/MCI screening (Kalbe et al. 2004)
- **Power**: Designed to detect large effect (f=0.40) at alpha=0.05, beta=0.05 — minimum 84 per age group; achieved 84-85 per group

## Limitations

- **No CIs reported**: Only F statistics, eta2, and d values reported. Cannot estimate precision of effect sizes.
- **Extreme stimulus contrast**: White at 350 cd/m2 vs. black at 1 cd/m2 is higher contrast than typical real-world interfaces. Results may not translate directly to softer light mode implementations (e.g., off-white backgrounds, gray text).
- **Polarity only — no font, size, or spacing factors**: Study isolates polarity but does not address how it interacts with typography choices.
- **No color**: All stimuli were pure black-and-white. Most interfaces use color; dark mode UI often uses dark-gray backgrounds, not pure black.
- **German-language texts**: Proofreading used German materials; language familiarity effects not assessed.
- **Clinical pathology excluded**: Findings explicitly do not apply to adults with significant eye disease, including untreated cataract. For accessibility design (WCAG), this is a notable gap.
- **Screen technology**: TFT-LCD only. OLED dark mode presents different luminance dynamics (true blacks). Mechanism may differ on OLED.
- **Ecological validity of proofreading task**: 50 seconds per 250-word text in a dark room with no ambient computing context is not typical of how people actually read on devices.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult — both younger (18-33) and older (60-85); healthy community sample; Germany
- **Relevance to app design for non-designer building Apple-ecosystem apps**: High — directly addresses which display polarity default produces better legibility, which is foundational for any text-heavy app UI
- **Red flags**: None — this is exactly the population and question relevant to this guidebook. Note the clinical exclusions, which means findings do not extend to users with serious visual impairments.

## Conclusions

"A positive polarity advantage was observed for both younger and older adults. Consequently, we recommend to present dark characters on light background in digital displays independent of the target audience's age." The authors further note the advantage is primarily driven by display luminance and pupillary response, not by familiarity. For non-pathological older adults, brighter positive polarity displays more than compensate for age-related retinal illuminance decline.

## Extraction Notes

This is a clean, well-powered experimental study with a straightforward finding: light mode (dark-on-light) produces better visual acuity and proofreading accuracy than dark mode for both age groups. The between-subjects design is a genuine methodological strength — it addresses the main confound that caused null findings in earlier within-subjects studies.

The effect sizes are striking: d=2.17 for visual acuity in younger adults is very large. This likely reflects the extreme black/white stimulus conditions, and app designers should interpret this as directional rather than calibrated for typical UI contexts. The proofreading effect (eta2=0.06) is more modest and arguably more ecologically relevant.

The key nuance for the guidebook: the mechanism is luminance, not polarity per se. A light-mode app with a very dark, low-contrast implementation could underperform a thoughtfully-designed dark-mode app with higher overall luminance. The practical recommendation is: default to light mode, keep background luminance high relative to text, and offer dark mode as a preference — not the default.

The OLED caveat is worth surfacing in the guidebook: on modern iPhones with OLED screens, dark mode achieves true black pixels (zero luminance), which is a materially different physical situation than the CRT/LCD dark mode this study was designed to address.
