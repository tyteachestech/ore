# The Influence of Design Aesthetics in Usability Testing

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Sonderegger, A. & Sauer, J., 2010. Applied Ergonomics, 41, 403-410.
- **Study Type**: Experimental (between-subjects, 2x2 mixed design)
- **Sample**: n=60, adolescents aged 13-16 (M=14.2 yrs), 52% female; Swiss secondary school pupils; self-reported experienced mobile phone users (M=8.7 uses/day)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.apergo.2009.09.002
- **PMID**: unknown

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 -- Aesthetics improves objective task performance**: Participants using the attractive phone prototype completed tasks significantly faster than those using the unattractive prototype.
  - Task completion time: M=147.7s (SD=58.0) appealing vs. M=198.5s (SD=83.6) unappealing
  - F=8.9, df=1,57, p<.01 (no effect size reported as d or eta; F-statistic only)
  - This is a ~25.6% reduction in time for the attractive condition

- **Finding 2 -- Aesthetics improves interaction efficiency**: Users of the attractive prototype reached goals in fewer clicks relative to the optimal path.
  - Interaction efficiency: 59% (SD=17) appealing vs. 46% (SD=19) unappealing
  - F=8.8, df=1,57, p<.01

- **Finding 3 -- Aesthetics reduces errors**: Users of the attractive prototype made significantly fewer navigation errors.
  - Errors per trial: 2.0 (SD=2.0) appealing vs. 4.8 (SD=5.0) unappealing
  - F=12.0, df=1,57, p<.001
  - All three performance measures (time, efficiency, errors) favored the attractive prototype

- **Finding 4 -- Aesthetics inflates perceived usability (PSSUQ)**: Attractive prototype rated higher on all PSSUQ subscales, despite functionally identical products.
  - Overall PSSUQ: M=6.13 (SD=0.48) appealing vs. M=5.19 (SD=0.91) unappealing; F=20.8, df=1,57, p<.001
  - System usefulness: 6.29 vs. 5.32, F=13.6, p<.001
  - Information quality: 6.27 vs. 5.66, F=7.2, p<.01
  - Interface quality: 5.30 vs. 3.60, F=14.5, p<.001

- **Finding 5 -- Aesthetics effect on perceived usability persists after use**: The usability halo does not erode with hands-on experience. Ratings on the 1-item usability scale were stable before and after the task (no significant interaction, F<1). If anything, the attractiveness gap widened after use (attitude polarization effect).

- **Finding 6 -- Gender did not moderate aesthetics effects**: No significant gender differences on perceived usability, attractiveness, task time, or interaction efficiency. One significant gender effect on errors (females more errors with unappealing prototype) but authors note this was small and difficult to interpret.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

Note: The paper reports F-statistics only; Cohen's d / eta-squared values are not provided. Raw means and SDs allow approximate calculation.

- **Task completion time**: F(1,57)=8.9, p<.01 — Appealing: M=147.7s (SD=58.0); Unappealing: M=198.5s (SD=83.6)
- **Interaction efficiency**: F(1,57)=8.8, p<.01 — Appealing: 59% (SD=17); Unappealing: 46% (SD=19)
- **Errors per trial**: F(1,57)=12.0, p<.001 — Appealing: 2.0 (SD=2.0); Unappealing: 4.8 (SD=5.0)
- **Perceived attractiveness (manipulation check)**: F(1,58)=39.8, p<.001 — Appealing: M=5.3; Unappealing: M=3.15 (1-7 scale)
- **Perceived usability (1-item scale)**: F(1,57)=9.8, p<.01 — Appealing: M=6.14; Unappealing: M=5.32
- **PSSUQ overall**: F(1,57)=20.8, p<.001 — Appealing: 6.13 (SD=0.48); Unappealing: 5.19 (SD=0.91)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism -- Halo effect**: Aesthetics creates a "what is beautiful is good" spillover where attractiveness positively colors judgments of all other product attributes including usability. Initial attitude formed within ~50ms (Lindgaard et al., 2006) then biased assimilation of subsequent experience reinforces it (attitude polarization).
- **Mechanism -- Increased motivation (this study's result)**: Attractive product increased performance, interpreted as aesthetic appeal putting users "in flow" or at ease, enhancing focus and motivation to complete tasks efficiently.
- **Mechanism -- Prolongation of joyful experience (contrasting prior work)**: In leisure contexts (Sauer & Sonderegger, 2009), attractive product *slowed* users as they lingered on the experience. Direction of performance effect may depend on usage context.
- **Moderator -- Usage context (work vs. leisure)**: Authors hypothesize that work/performance-oriented contexts elicit "increased motivation" from aesthetics, while leisure contexts elicit "prolonged enjoyment." Present study conducted in school setting (work-like), prior conflicting study in leisure setting. Not directly tested within this study.
- **Moderator -- Gender**: Not a meaningful moderator (all F<1 for key outcomes). One marginal effect on errors not interpreted as meaningful.
- **Stable halo after use**: Experience with the product did not correct the aesthetics-driven usability perception gap. This is important: the halo is not merely pre-use speculation -- it persists through actual interaction.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Two functionally identical computer-simulated mobile phones (PowerPoint on Tablet PC), one with appealing visual design, one unappealing. Designs manipulated via colour, texture, symmetry, clarity. Interaction triggers were exactly the same size for both.
- **Control/Comparison**: Unappealing prototype (same functionality, different visual design)
- **Tasks**: 2 tasks -- sending a text message (min 9 clicks) and phone number suppression in settings (min 16 clicks)
- **Follow-up period**: Single session, ~20 minutes. Pre/post measures within session only.
- **Attrition**: Not reported (n=60 appears complete)
- **Manipulation check**: Successful -- attractive prototype rated 8.1/10 vs. unappealing at 2.0/10 in pilot; in main study 5.3 vs. 3.15 on 1-7 scale (F=39.8, p<.001)
- **Objective task difficulty equivalence check**: Average time-per-click was identical between conditions (M=3.0 vs. 3.1, F<1), confirming the two prototypes were functionally equivalent.

## Limitations

- **Adolescent-only sample**: All participants were 13-16 year olds. Authors explicitly caution against generalizing to adults. Performance direction (increased motivation) may differ in adults or leisure contexts.
- **No adult comparison group**: Cannot isolate whether performance findings are age-specific or context-specific without direct adult comparison.
- **Short single-session exposure**: Effects measured after brief one-time use only. Unknown whether halo and performance effects persist or decay with repeated use.
- **Familiarity confound**: Attractive prototype may have looked more like a commercially available phone, potentially boosting perceived usability via familiarity rather than pure aesthetics. Authors controlled partially (excluded Sony Ericsson W800i owners) but cannot fully rule out.
- **No Cohen's d or eta-squared reported**: Effect size magnitude cannot be directly compared to other studies without calculation from means and SDs.
- **Small n per condition**: n=30 per group limits power for subgroup analyses.
- **Simulated product, not real app**: Computer simulation in PowerPoint; external validity to real-world interactive apps (iOS, macOS) is uncertain.
- **Single product category**: Mobile phone. Generalization to desktop software, web apps, or native macOS/iOS apps requires caution.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Adolescent students (13-16 yrs), Switzerland
- **Relevance to non-designer building Apple-ecosystem apps**: Medium. Core findings (aesthetics -> perceived usability; aesthetics -> performance) are consistent with adult studies and cross-cultural replications. The performance direction finding is context-dependent and adolescent-specific -- less directly applicable. The perceived usability halo effect is the most relevant and most robustly replicated finding.
- **Red flags**: Adolescent-only sample; single brief session; leisure vs. work context moderates direction of performance effect

## Conclusions

"The results showed that participants using the highly appealing phone rated their appliance as being more usable than participants operating the unappealing model. Furthermore, the visual appearance of the phone had a positive effect on performance, leading to reduced task completion times for the attractive model." The usability halo from aesthetics did not diminish after actual hands-on use -- if anything it widened. The direction of the performance effect (faster vs. slower) appears to depend on whether the context is work-oriented or leisure-oriented.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Halo Effect**: A salient positive characteristic (visual attractiveness) biases perception of other attributes (usability, information quality, system usefulness). Applied to UI: beautiful design makes users feel the interface is more functional, clearer, and easier to use, even when functionality is identical.
- **"Increased motivation" vs. "Prolongation of joyful experience"**: Two competing hypotheses for how aesthetics affects objective performance. Work context -> increased motivation -> faster. Leisure context -> prolonged enjoyment -> slower. Both are plausible and context-dependent.

## Extraction Notes

This is a useful supporting paper for the "aesthetics affects real outcomes, not just perception" claim. The key caution is the adolescent sample and single-session design. However, the perceived usability halo effect findings are tightly consistent with the broader literature (Tractinsky 2000, Ben-Bassat 2006, Brady & Phillips 2003, Sauer & Sonderegger 2009). For the guidebook, the most citable findings are: (1) beautiful design inflates perceived usability scores even when functionality is identical, and (2) this halo does not erode after use -- users don't "correct" their aesthetics-driven perceptions once they interact with the product.

The performance direction finding (attractive = faster here) is interesting but should be cited with the caveat that prior work with similar methodology found the opposite in leisure contexts. The mechanism discussion is genuinely useful for explaining *why* aesthetics matters beyond superficial preference.

No Cohen's d is reported. Approximate effect sizes can be estimated from the table data if needed for comparison with other studies.
