# The Information Capacity of the Human Motor System in Controlling the Amplitude of Movement

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Fitts, P.M. (1954). Journal of Experimental Psychology, 47(6), 381-391.
- **Study Type**: Experimental (three within-subjects laboratory experiments)
- **Sample**: n=52 total across three experiments (Exp I: n=16 college men; Exp II: n=16 college men, different from Exp I; Exp III: n=20 college students, 10 men/10 women)
- **DOI**: 10.1037/h0055392
- **PMID**: Unknown — pre-PubMed publication

## Key Findings

- **Core Law**: Movement time (MT) increases as a linear function of task difficulty (Id), where difficulty is defined as Id = log2(2A/W) bits/response. This relationship held across all three experiments and all task types, establishing what is now universally called Fitts's Law.

- **Performance Rate Stability**: The information output rate (Ip = Id/t) of the human motor system was approximately constant across a wide central range of amplitude and tolerance conditions:
  - Tapping (light stylus): 10.3–11.5 bits/sec over best 8 of 16 conditions (range = 1.2 bits)
  - Disc transfer: 9.0–10.4 bits/sec over best 8 of 16 conditions (range = 1.3 bits)
  - Pin transfer: stable within 1.0 bit/sec over best 10 of 20 conditions

- **Physical Work Is Not the Limiting Factor**: Changing stylus weight from 1 oz to 1 lb had minimal effect on performance rate. Pearson r = .97 between Ip values for the two stylus conditions across all 16 tapping conditions. Fitts interprets this as evidence that central organizing time (not muscular capacity) sets the performance ceiling.

- **Optimal Amplitude Range**: Movement amplitudes of 4–8 inches consistently produced the best performance rates across all tasks. Both very small (1-2 in.) and implicitly very large amplitudes showed reduced efficiency.

- **Error Rate Was Low and Task-Appropriate**: Average error rates were 1.2% (light stylus) and 1.3% (heavy stylus) in the tapping task — confirming subjects successfully traded speed for accuracy as task difficulty increased. Maximum error rate was 4.1% at the hardest condition (W = 0.25 in., A = 16 in.).

- **Practice Effects Were Minimal**: Performance improved only 3–5% from first to second block within a session, indicating results reflected stable motor capacity rather than learning artifacts.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

This is a foundational measurement paper, not an intervention study. Traditional effect sizes (Cohen's d, etc.) are not applicable. The key quantitative results are:

- **Performance rate range, tapping (1-oz stylus)**: 5.56–10.75 bits/sec across 16 conditions; 10.08–11.54 bits/sec for best 8 conditions
- **Performance rate range, tapping (1-lb stylus)**: 5.49–10.81 bits/sec across 16 conditions
- **Correlation between stylus conditions**: r = .97 (n=16 conditions)
- **Performance rate range, disc transfer**: 7.47–10.38 bits/sec across 16 conditions
- **Performance rate range, pin transfer**: 8.92–12.57 bits/sec across 20 conditions; stable within 1.0 bit/sec over best 10 conditions
- **Index of difficulty range tested**: 1–10 bits/response (disc transfer); up to ~12 bits/response (pin transfer)
- **Error rate**: 1.2–1.3% average; peak 4.1% at hardest condition

No CIs reported (1954 norms). No significance testing for the primary thesis — the paper argues from the consistency of the Ip measure across conditions.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Proposed Mechanism — Central Organizing Time**: Fitts argues the constant information rate reflects a fixed capacity of central neural mechanisms for monitoring motor output and organizing successive movements. This is not limited by muscular strength or endurance (stylus weight manipulation). He explicitly invokes Fenn's (1938) concept that maximum tapping rate is limited by the speed at which the CNS can alternate excitation and inhibition.

- **Information Theory Framework**: Draws on Shannon & Weaver (1949) and Miller (1953). Motor variability (noise) limits how many distinct response categories can be reliably produced — the ratio of amplitude to tolerance defines how many categories are required.

- **Moderator — Movement Type**: Performance ceiling varied slightly by task type (~10 bits/sec for tapping, ~10-12 for pin transfer), suggesting different limb/muscle groups have somewhat different channel capacities. Finger movements may have higher capacity than arm movements.

- **Moderator — Amplitude Extremes**: Very small amplitudes (1–2 in.) and (implicitly) very large ones showed reduced Ip. The law holds over a central optimum range, not at extremes.

- **Moderator — Stylus Weight (Physical Load)**: Minimal moderating effect on performance rate (r = .97); slightly shifted the optimum toward smaller amplitudes with the heavier stylus.

- **Direction Asymmetry**: Subjects were more accurate on flexor than extensor movements. With the light stylus, over/undershoots were equal; with the heavy stylus, undershoots were twice as frequent.

## Study Design Details

- **Experiment I — Reciprocal Tapping**: 16 subjects tapped two metal plates alternately with a stylus. 4 target widths (W = 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0 in.) × 4 amplitudes (A = 2, 4, 8, 16 in.) = 16 conditions. Two stylus weights (1 oz, 1 lb) tested on separate days. 15-sec trials, 55-sec rest. Within-subjects, counterbalanced order.
- **Experiment II — Disc Transfer**: 16 different subjects transferred plastic washers between pins. 4 tolerance levels (hole-pin gap = 1/16, 1/8, 1/4, 1/2 in.) × 4 amplitudes (A = 4, 8, 16, 32 in.) = 16 conditions. No errors permitted.
- **Experiment III — Pin Transfer**: 20 different subjects (mixed sex) transferred pins between holes. 4 pin sizes × 5 amplitudes (A = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 in.) = 20 conditions. Repeated across 3 days; analysis uses combined days 2+3.
- **Control/Comparison**: Within-subjects across conditions; no separate control group — the paper tests whether Ip is constant (the invariant itself is the finding).
- **Follow-up**: Same-session and multi-day replications only.
- **Blinding**: Not applicable for this task type.

## Limitations

- **Narrow population**: All subjects were Ohio State University college students (18–22 age range implied). No demographic diversity. Predominantly male (Exp I and II were all-male).
- **Artificial laboratory tasks**: Tapping metal plates, transferring washers and pins are not naturalistic interactions. Generalization to touchscreens, mice, and GUI targets requires subsequent validation (which has been extensively provided in later literature, but is not in this paper).
- **No confidence intervals or significance tests**: By 1954 norms, the argument is from consistency of the Ip metric. Modern reviewers would expect formal statistical tests of linearity.
- **One-dimensional movement only**: All experiments involved linear amplitude. The law's extension to 2D pointing (as in GUIs and touchscreens) was established by later researchers.
- **Fixed task type within experiments**: Each experiment used a single motor task type. Cross-task generalization within this paper is suggestive but not formally tested.
- **Optimum range not formally defined**: The "central range" where Ip is stable is identified by inspection, not by a formal boundary criterion.
- **Funding**: US Air Force (Contract AF33(038)-10528). Military origin — motivated by control interface design. No conflict of interest in the modern sense, but context is human-machine interfaces for aircraft, not consumer software.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult — college-age participants performing arm/hand motor tasks
- **Relevance to UX/UI design**: Very High — Fitts's Law is the single most-cited quantitative principle in HCI and interaction design. Directly governs touch target sizing, button placement, menu design, cursor movement, and gesture design.
- **Red flags**: None. This is foundational empirical work with no clinical or pathological framing.
- **Note on generalizability**: The specific tasks (stylus tapping, disc/pin transfer) are analog. Subsequent research by Card, Moran & Newell (1980s), MacKenzie (1992), and many others has confirmed the law applies to mouse pointing, touchscreen tapping, and cursor movement with high fidelity. This paper should be cited as the origin of the law; practical numbers for digital targets come from later applied work.

## Conclusions

Fitts concludes that "the performance capacity of the human motor system plus its associated visual and proprioceptive feedback mechanisms, when measured in information units, is relatively constant over a considerable range of task conditions." He frames the finding as evidence that a fixed capacity for central neural organization — not muscular limitation — determines motor speed-accuracy tradeoff. The practical implication (not stated in 1954 terms but now universal in HCI): making targets larger or closer reduces movement time in a predictable, logarithmic way, and this relationship is robust across very different types of movement tasks.

## Named Frameworks

- **Fitts's Law (Index of Difficulty)**
  - Formula: Id = log2(2A/W) bits/response
  - Performance Index: Ip = Id/t bits/sec
  - Key insight: MT is proportional to Id; human motor system has a roughly constant Ip (~10-12 bits/sec for arm movements)
  - HCI form (Shannon formulation, later standard): MT = a + b * log2(A/W + 1)
  - Research alignment: Demonstrated empirically across 3 tasks, multiple amplitudes, and two loads

## Extraction Notes

This is the origin paper for Fitts's Law — one of the most empirically robust and practically consequential findings in all of HCI. Every major GUI, touch interface, and cursor interaction model traces to this 1954 paper.

**For the guidebook:** The paper establishes the principle but predates digital interfaces entirely. Chapter writers should cite Fitts (1954) as the origin, then draw on MacKenzie (1992) or Card et al. (1983) for the formulation that became standard in HCI (the Shannon-adjusted formula MT = a + b * log2(A/W + 1)), and on applied studies for concrete recommendations (e.g., Apple's 44pt minimum touch target, which derives from Fitts's Law applied to finger mechanics).

**Practical translation for non-designer readers:** Fitts's Law says the time to tap, click, or point at a target depends on two things only — how far away it is and how big it is. Bigger targets and closer placement always reduce errors and movement time. This is why primary actions sit at screen edges/corners (infinite width in one direction via Fitts), why menus expand toward the cursor, and why mobile tap targets need to be larger than visual elements suggest.

**Evidence grade rationale:** "Foundational" — this is the source paper for a principle with 70 years of replication and direct application in every HCI platform design guideline (Apple HIG, Material Design, etc.).
