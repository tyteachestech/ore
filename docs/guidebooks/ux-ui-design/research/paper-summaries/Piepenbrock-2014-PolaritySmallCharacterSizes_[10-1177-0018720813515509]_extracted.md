# Smaller Pupil Size and Better Proofreading Performance with Positive than with Negative Polarity Displays

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Piepenbrock, Mayr & Buchner, 2014
- **Study Type**: Experimental (within-subjects crossover)
- **Sample**: n=35, young healthy adults (20-30 years, M=24.3), native German speakers, normal or corrected-to-normal vision; 8 additional participants excluded due to poor pupil tracking
- **DOI**: 10.1177/0018720813515509
- **PMID**: unknown

> **Important file note:** The DOI in the filename and the registry description reference "Positive display polarity is particularly advantageous for small character sizes" (Human Factors, 2014). However, the converted PDF contains the companion paper "Smaller pupil size and better proofreading performance with positive than with negative polarity displays." These are two separate papers by the same authors. The small character sizes paper is cited as "in press" within this document (line 854). The extracted data below reflects what is actually in this file. The registry entry for P18 should be verified against the correct PDF.

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 -- Proofreading accuracy**: Significantly better with positive polarity (dark text on light background) than negative polarity (light text on dark background).
  - Effect size: dz = 0.77, t(34) = 4.54, p < .01
  - Sample: n=35

- **Finding 2 -- Reading rate**: Participants read significantly more words per 50-second text in positive polarity condition.
  - Effect size: dz = 0.68, t(34) = 4.04, p < .01
  - Sample: n=35

- **Finding 3 -- Pupil diameter**: Substantially smaller in positive polarity condition. Mean pupil: 2.09mm (positive) vs. 3.65mm (negative). This is the largest observed effect.
  - Effect size: dz = 2.96, t(34) = -17.49, p < .01
  - Ambient illumination at eye: 118.4 lx (positive) vs. 2.7 lx (negative) -- a 44x difference driven by screen luminance alone.

- **Finding 4 -- Pupil size predicts performance**: In a linear mixed-effects model, smaller pupil size significantly predicted better proofreading accuracy (b = -0.24, SE = 0.04, t = -6.04, p < .01) and higher reading rate (b = -2.12, SE = 0.41, t = -5.12, p < .01).

- **Finding 5 -- Subjective experiences**: Post-task questionnaire showed consistent preference for positive polarity.
  - 82% preferred positive polarity overall
  - 62% reported more difficulty focusing on individual words with negative polarity
  - 55% reported more difficulty following lines of text with negative polarity
  - 77% reported more blur with negative polarity
  - 78% reported more glare with positive polarity (negative finding -- glare higher with light mode)

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Pupil diameter**: dz = 2.96 (t(34) = -17.49, p<.01), n=35; positive: 2.09mm, negative: 3.65mm
- **Proofreading accuracy (hits - false alarms)**: dz = 0.77 (t(34) = 4.54, p<.01), n=35
- **Reading rate (words/50s)**: dz = 0.68 (t(34) = 4.04, p<.01), n=35
- **Pupil -> accuracy (mixed model)**: b = -0.24, SE = 0.04, t = -6.04, p<.01
- **Pupil -> reading rate (mixed model)**: b = -2.12, SE = 0.41, t = -5.12, p<.01
- **Prior literature reference** (Buchner & Baumgartner, 2007): sample effect sizes of η2 = 0.23, 0.06, 0.10 across three experiments; mean η2 = 0.13, equivalent to f = 0.39 (large by Cohen's conventions)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Display luminance hypothesis (primary mechanism)**: Positive polarity displays emit far more light, causing pupil constriction (smaller pupil = sharper retinal image via reduced spherical aberrations and increased depth of field). This tolerates accommodative lag better for near reading tasks.
  - Supporting: Advantage disappears when luminance is equalized between conditions (Buchner et al., 2009 cited in text)
  - Supporting: Pupil size was indeed smaller with positive polarity, consistent with prediction
  - Caution: Study measured but did not experimentally manipulate pupil size, so causal directionality cannot be fully established

- **Alternative mechanisms not ruled out**:
  - Familiarity: Dark-on-light is ubiquitous in print; cognitive processes may be tuned to it
  - Visual system asymmetry: Retina and V1 devote more resources to processing dark-on-light (Ratliff et al., 2010; Yeh et al., 2009)

- **Glare moderator**: Glare was reported higher with positive polarity by 78% of participants -- a potential drawback of light mode, particularly relevant in high-ambient-light environments.

- **No moderators explicitly tested** in this study (within-subject design, single polarity variable).

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: 36 texts of 250 words each, presented in blocks alternating positive/negative polarity. 6 blocks of each.
- **Font**: 10pt Helvetica (x-height = 0.22 degrees visual angle) -- notably small, closely resembling caption/footnote sizes
- **Display**: 24-inch TFT-LCD, max contrast (white: 328.8 cd/m2, black: 0.6 cd/m2)
- **Control/Comparison**: Within-subject crossover -- each participant read both polarities
- **Task**: Proofreading for spelling and grammar errors (accuracy = hits - false alarms); reading rate also measured
- **Ambient conditions**: Dark room, 0.1 lx baseline; 118.4 lx (positive condition) vs. 2.7 lx (negative condition) at eye
- **Viewing distance**: 50 cm (chin rest)
- **Eye tracking**: SMI Eye Tracking Glasses, binocular, 30 Hz

## Limitations

- **Pupil size not manipulated**: Correlation between pupil size and performance established, but causal mechanism not experimentally confirmed
- **Low ambient illumination**: Room was dark (0.1 lx background); real-world office environments target 500 lx. Prior research suggests ambient level does not affect the polarity advantage, but the extreme setup reduces ecological validity for bright-light use cases (sunlight, outdoor environments)
- **Young adults only**: n=35, ages 20-30. Results may differ for older adults (accommodation differences) or vision-impaired users
- **Small character size only**: 10pt Helvetica -- findings most directly applicable to small text. The companion paper (small character sizes paper, same DOI) specifically explores how the polarity effect scales with character size
- **Single font/typeface**: Only Helvetica tested; generalizability to other fonts or rendering environments not assessed
- **Lab conditions**: Dark room may have exaggerated pupil differences vs. normal use

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Young adult university students (German), normal vision
- **Relevance to UX/UI design (non-designer building Apple apps)**: High -- directly about screen text legibility, uses Apple hardware (iMac TFT-LCD), and findings translate directly to light/dark mode decisions for small text (captions, footnotes, labels)
- **Red flags**: None; general population, no clinical elements

## Conclusions

Positive polarity (dark text on light background) significantly outperforms negative polarity on proofreading accuracy (dz=0.77) and reading rate (dz=0.68) with small character sizes (10pt). The proposed mechanism is that bright positive polarity displays cause pupil constriction (mean 2.09mm vs. 3.65mm, dz=2.96), producing a sharper retinal image. The authors conclude: "the data also emphasises the recommendation to present text in positive polarity." The glare finding (78% reported more glare with positive polarity) is worth noting as a counterpoint for specific use contexts.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Display Luminance Hypothesis**: Positive polarity advantage in reading is caused by higher display luminance -> pupil constriction -> reduced spherical aberration -> sharper retinal image -> better perception of small details. Supported but not causally confirmed in this study.
