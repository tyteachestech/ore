## 1. Aesthetic-Usability Effect

### P01 — Kurosu & Kashimura (1995) — CORE
- **Authors:** Masaaki Kurosu, Kaori Kashimura
- **Year:** 1995
- **Title:** Apparent usability vs. inherent usability: Experimental analysis on the determinants of the apparent usability
- **Journal/Source:** CHI '95 Conference Companion on Human Factors in Computing Systems, pp. 292-293
- **DOI:** 10.1145/223355.223680
- **Citations:** ~217
- **Key Finding:** Users' judgments of usability are strongly influenced by aesthetics (r = .589 correlation between apparent usability and beauty). The first paper to empirically demonstrate the aesthetic-usability effect.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Foundational justification for why visual polish matters — directly supports the guidebook's premise that a non-designer must learn aesthetics to build apps that feel usable.

### P02 — Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar (2000) — CORE
- **Authors:** Noam Tractinsky, Adi Shoval-Katz, Dror Ikar
- **Year:** 2000
- **Title:** What is beautiful is usable
- **Journal/Source:** Interacting with Computers, 13(2), 127-145
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00031-X
- **Citations:** ~1,393
- **Key Finding:** Replicated Kurosu & Kashimura's findings cross-culturally (Israel). Aesthetics affected post-use perceptions of both aesthetics AND usability, whereas actual usability had no such effect. The aesthetic "halo effect" persists even after real use.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The most cited paper on the aesthetic-usability effect. Essential for the chapter on why design quality matters — beautiful interfaces are perceived as more usable regardless of actual usability.

### P03 — Sonderegger & Sauer (2010) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Andreas Sonderegger, Juergen Sauer
- **Year:** 2010
- **Title:** The influence of design aesthetics in usability testing: Effects on user performance and perceived usability
- **Journal/Source:** Applied Ergonomics, 41(3), 403-410
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.apergo.2009.09.002
- **Citations:** ~458
- **Key Finding:** Participants using a highly appealing phone rated it as more usable AND completed tasks faster than those using an unappealing but functionally identical model. Aesthetics affects not just perception but actual performance.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Extends aesthetic-usability effect beyond perception to actual task performance — strengthens the case that visual design directly improves user outcomes.

### P04 — Tuch, Roth & Hornbaek (2012) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Alexandre N. Tuch, Sandra P. Roth, Kasper Hornbaek
- **Year:** 2012
- **Title:** Is beautiful really usable? Toward understanding the relation between usability, aesthetics, and affect in HCI
- **Journal/Source:** Computers in Human Behavior, 28(5), 1596-1607
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.chb.2012.03.024
- **Citations:** ~200+
- **Key Finding:** The "what is beautiful is usable" notion can reverse under strong usability manipulation — when usability problems are severe, beauty can't compensate. Shows boundary conditions of the aesthetic-usability effect.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Important nuance: beauty helps, but doesn't excuse broken usability. Prevents the reader from over-indexing on aesthetics at the expense of function.

### P05 — Lavie & Tractinsky (2004) — CORE
- **Authors:** Talia Lavie, Noam Tractinsky
- **Year:** 2004
- **Title:** Assessing dimensions of perceived visual aesthetics of web sites
- **Journal/Source:** International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 60(3), 269-298
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2003.09.002
- **Citations:** ~800+
- **Key Finding:** Identified two distinct dimensions of interface aesthetics: "classical" (clean, clear, symmetrical, organized) and "expressive" (original, fascinating, creative). Perceived usability correlates more strongly with classical aesthetics.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Gives the reader a concrete framework: classical aesthetics (the "clean and organized" pole) is what most directly improves perceived usability — highly actionable for a beginner learning to design app UIs.

## 2. First Impressions & Visual Appeal

### P06 — Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006) — CORE
- **Authors:** Gitte Lindgaard, Gary Fernandes, Cathy Dudek, Judith Brown
- **Year:** 2006
- **Title:** Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!
- **Journal/Source:** Behaviour & Information Technology, 25(2), 115-126
- **DOI:** 10.1080/01449290500330448
- **Citations:** ~1,000+
- **Key Finding:** Users form reliable aesthetic judgments of visual appeal within 50 milliseconds of exposure. These snap judgments are highly consistent across repeated viewings and correlate with longer exposure assessments.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The strongest empirical evidence for why first impressions matter in app design. Users judge your app's quality before they've read a single word — visual design is not optional.

## 3. Usability Heuristics & Error Prevention

### P07 — Nielsen & Molich (1990) — CORE
- **Authors:** Jakob Nielsen, Rolf Molich
- **Year:** 1990
- **Title:** Heuristic evaluation of user interfaces
- **Journal/Source:** Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '90), pp. 249-256
- **DOI:** 10.1145/97243.97281
- **Citations:** ~3,000+
- **Key Finding:** Introduced heuristic evaluation as an inspection method. A small set of evaluators using general heuristics can identify a large proportion of usability problems in an interface.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The origin of the 10 usability heuristics — the most widely used framework for evaluating interface quality. Essential reference for the error prevention, consistency, and overall usability chapters.

### P08 — Nielsen (1994) — CORE
- **Authors:** Jakob Nielsen
- **Year:** 1994
- **Title:** Enhancing the explanatory power of usability heuristics
- **Journal/Source:** Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '94), pp. 152-158
- **DOI:** 10.1145/191666.191729
- **Citations:** ~2,500+
- **Key Finding:** Refined the original heuristics into the canonical 10: visibility of system status, match between system and real world, user control and freedom, consistency and standards, error prevention, recognition rather than recall, flexibility and efficiency, aesthetic and minimalist design, help users recover from errors, help and documentation.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The definitive version of the 10 heuristics. Every chapter in the guidebook implicitly references at least one of these. The "pre-ship design checklist" (Appendix G) should map directly to these.

## 4. Fitts's Law & Touch Targets

### P09 — Fitts (1954) — CORE
- **Authors:** Paul M. Fitts
- **Year:** 1954
- **Title:** The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement
- **Journal/Source:** Journal of Experimental Psychology, 47(6), 381-391
- **DOI:** 10.1037/h0055392
- **Citations:** ~10,000+
- **Key Finding:** Movement time to a target is a logarithmic function of the distance to the target divided by the target's width (MT = a + b * log2(2A/W)). Larger, closer targets are faster to acquire.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The foundational law for all interaction design involving pointing/tapping. Directly explains why Apple's Human Interface Guidelines specify minimum touch target sizes, why buttons should be appropriately large, and why important actions go at screen edges.

### P10 — Parhi, Karlson & Bederson (2006) — CORE
- **Authors:** Pekka Parhi, Amy K. Karlson, Benjamin B. Bederson
- **Year:** 2006
- **Title:** Target size study for one-handed thumb use on small touchscreen devices
- **Journal/Source:** MobileHCI '06: Proceedings of the 8th Conference on Human-Computer Interaction with Mobile Devices and Services, pp. 203-210
- **DOI:** 10.1145/1152215.1152260
- **Citations:** ~400+
- **Key Finding:** Minimum target size of 9.2mm for discrete tapping tasks and 7.6mm for serial tasks is required for one-handed thumb operation without degrading performance. Below these thresholds, error rates increase significantly.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Directly actionable for iOS app development. Apple's 44pt minimum tap target comes from research like this. Essential for the touch interaction / mobile usability chapter.

## 5. Hick's Law & Choice Overload

### P11 — Proctor & Schneider (2018) — CORE
- **Authors:** Robert W. Proctor, Darryl W. Schneider
- **Year:** 2018
- **Title:** Hick's law for choice reaction time: A review
- **Journal/Source:** Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 71(6), 1281-1299
- **DOI:** 10.1080/17470218.2017.1322622
- **Citations:** ~92
- **Key Finding:** Comprehensive review of Hick's 1952 law (choice reaction time is a logarithmic function of the number of alternatives). Covers boundary conditions, challenges, and contemporary applications. Confirms it as a fundamental law of interface design.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** The best modern review of Hick's law. Directly supports chapters on navigation simplification, menu design, and progressive disclosure — fewer choices = faster decisions.

### P12 — Iyengar & Lepper (2000) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Sheena S. Iyengar, Mark R. Lepper
- **Year:** 2000
- **Title:** When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?
- **Journal/Source:** Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995-1006
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995
- **Citations:** ~6,000+
- **Key Finding:** People presented with 6 choices were 10x more likely to purchase than those presented with 24 choices. Excessive choice leads to decision paralysis, lower satisfaction, and avoidance behavior.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** The "jam study" — most famous empirical demonstration of choice overload. Directly supports UI simplification, progressive disclosure, and the principle that fewer options in menus/settings leads to better user outcomes.

## 6. Cognitive Load & Working Memory

### P13 — Miller (1956) — CORE
- **Authors:** George A. Miller
- **Year:** 1956
- **Title:** The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information
- **Journal/Source:** Psychological Review, 63(2), 81-97
- **DOI:** 10.1037/h0043158
- **Citations:** ~30,000+
- **Key Finding:** Human working memory has a capacity of approximately 7 +/- 2 chunks of information. Performance degrades when this limit is exceeded. "Chunking" — grouping items into meaningful units — extends effective capacity.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Foundation for understanding cognitive load in UI design. Explains why navigation items, form fields, and option groups should be chunked into manageable groups. Underpins Hick's law, progressive disclosure, and information architecture decisions.

## 7. Typography & Readability

### P14 — Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013/2016) — CORE
- **Authors:** Luz Rello, Ricardo Baeza-Yates
- **Year:** 2013 (conference) / 2016 (journal)
- **Title:** Good fonts for dyslexia (2013) / The effect of font type on screen readability by people with dyslexia (2016)
- **Journal/Source:** ASSETS '13, pp. 14-21 (2013) / ACM Transactions on Accessible Computing, 8(4), Article 15 (2016)
- **DOI:** 10.1145/2513383.2513447 (2013) / 10.1145/2897736 (2016)
- **Citations:** ~300+ combined
- **Key Finding:** Eye-tracking study with 97 subjects (48 with dyslexia) testing 12 fonts. Sans-serif, monospaced, and roman fonts significantly improve reading performance. Helvetica, Courier, Arial, and Verdana are optimal. Italic fonts impair readability.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Directly actionable typography guidance for app design. Supports using San Francisco (Apple's system font, a sans-serif) and avoiding italic body text. Applicable to both macOS/iOS native apps and Raycast extensions.

### P15 — Shaikh & Chaparro (2005) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** A. Dawn Shaikh, Barbara S. Chaparro
- **Year:** 2005
- **Title:** The effects of line length on reading performance of online news articles
- **Journal/Source:** Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting, 49(5), 701-705
- **DOI:** 10.1177/154193120504900514
- **Citations:** ~100+
- **Key Finding:** 95 characters per line (cpl) produced fastest reading speed. Users preferred both short (35 cpl) and long (95 cpl) line lengths. The commonly recommended 50-75 cpl range balances speed, comprehension, and comfort.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Directly informs typography and layout decisions — optimal line length for app text areas, settings screens, and content views. The 50-75 cpl guideline maps well to typical app content widths.

## 8. White Space & Layout

### P16 — Chaparro, Shaikh & Baker (2005) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Barbara S. Chaparro, A. Dawn Shaikh, J. Ryan Baker
- **Year:** 2005
- **Title:** Reading online text with a poor layout: Is performance worse?
- **Journal/Source:** Usability News, 7(1)
- **DOI:** N/A (practitioner publication)
- **Citations:** ~50+ (widely referenced in practitioner literature)
- **Key Finding:** Optimized white space led to slower reading but HIGHER comprehension than poor white space usage. Enhanced layouts were preferred, regarded as less tiring, and received higher satisfaction scores — even though raw reading speed didn't improve.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Demonstrates that white space trades speed for comprehension and comfort — a worthwhile trade for app interfaces where users need to understand content, not just scan it. Supports generous padding/margins in UI layouts.

## 9. Dark Mode & Display Polarity

### P17 — Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner (2013) — CORE
- **Authors:** Cosima Piepenbrock, Susanne Mayr, Iris Mund, Axel Buchner
- **Year:** 2013
- **Title:** Positive display polarity is advantageous for both younger and older adults
- **Journal/Source:** Ergonomics, 56(7), 1116-1124
- **DOI:** 10.1080/00140139.2013.790485
- **Citations:** ~150+
- **Key Finding:** Dark text on light background (positive polarity) produces better visual acuity and proofreading performance than light text on dark background (negative polarity), for both younger and older adults. The advantage stems from higher luminance enabling better detail perception.
- **Designation:** Core
- **Relevance:** Evidence-based guidance for dark mode vs. light mode defaults. Supports Apple's approach of light mode as default with dark mode as user preference. Critical for the dark/light mode chapter — light mode is objectively better for reading accuracy.

### P18 — Piepenbrock, Mayr, Buchner (2014) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Cosima Piepenbrock, Susanne Mayr, Axel Buchner
- **Year:** 2014
- **Title:** Positive display polarity is particularly advantageous for small character sizes
- **Journal/Source:** Human Factors, 56(7), 1227-1240
- **DOI:** 10.1177/0018720813515509
- **Citations:** ~100+
- **Key Finding:** The positive polarity advantage is amplified at small character sizes. For larger text, the difference diminishes. This means dark mode is especially problematic for fine print, captions, and footnotes.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Nuances the dark mode guidance: if your app uses small text (captions, secondary labels, footnotes), light mode provides a bigger legibility advantage. Directly relevant to iOS Dynamic Type considerations.

## 10. Gestalt Principles

### P19 — Peterson & Berryhill (2013) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Dwight J. Peterson, Marian E. Berryhill
- **Year:** 2013
- **Title:** The Gestalt principle of similarity benefits visual working memory
- **Journal/Source:** Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 20(6), 1282-1289
- **DOI:** 10.3758/s13423-013-0460-x
- **Citations:** ~120
- **Key Finding:** Gestalt grouping by similarity improves visual working memory performance — but only when similar items are also spatially proximal. Similarity alone is insufficient; proximity must co-occur for the benefit.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Empirical support for combining proximity and similarity in UI grouping. When designing lists, toolbars, or settings screens, similar items should be both visually alike AND physically close together.

### P20 — Wong (2010) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Bang Wong
- **Year:** 2010
- **Title:** Points of View: Gestalt principles (Part 1)
- **Journal/Source:** Nature Methods, 7(11), 863
- **DOI:** 10.1038/nmeth1110-863
- **Citations:** ~32
- **Key Finding:** Clear, accessible overview of Gestalt principles (similarity, proximity, connection, enclosure) applied to visual information design. Enclosure is the most powerful grouping principle, overriding similarity, proximity, and even connection.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Excellent pedagogical source for explaining Gestalt principles to a non-designer. The hierarchy (enclosure > connection > proximity > similarity) is directly actionable for UI layout decisions.

## 11. Animation & Motion Design

### P21 — Chevalier et al. (2016) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Fanny Chevalier, Nathalie Henry Riche, Catherine Plaisant, Amira Chalbi, Christophe Hurter
- **Year:** 2016
- **Title:** Animations 25 years later: New roles and opportunities
- **Journal/Source:** AVI '16: Proceedings of the International Working Conference on Advanced Visual Interfaces, pp. 280-287
- **DOI:** 10.1145/2909132.2909255
- **Citations:** ~100+
- **Key Finding:** Comprehensive review categorizing animation roles in interfaces: transitions (orienting users during view changes), feedback (confirming actions), instruction (teaching interaction patterns), and decoration (delight). Established a taxonomy of animation purposes.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Framework for deciding when and why to use animation in app design — not just for delight, but for orientation, feedback, and teaching. Directly applicable to SwiftUI transitions and Raycast extension animations.

## 12. Form Design

### P22 — Bargas-Avila, Brenzikofer, Tuch, Roth & Blattler (2011) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Javier A. Bargas-Avila, Oliver Brenzikofer, Alexandre N. Tuch, Sandra P. Roth, Simon Blattler
- **Year:** 2011
- **Title:** Simple but crucial user interfaces in the world wide web: Introducing 20 guidelines for usable web form design
- **Journal/Source:** In: Bentley, F. & Lunsford, R. (eds.) User Interfaces. InTech.
- **DOI:** 10.5772/15710
- **Citations:** ~100+
- **Key Finding:** Synthesized research into 20 evidence-based form design guidelines covering layout, labels, input fields, error handling, and submission. Top-aligned labels are fastest; inline validation reduces errors; error messages should be polite and specific.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Directly actionable for any app with forms (login, settings, data entry). The 20 guidelines can be distilled into the "Form Design" section of the guidebook. Applicable to iOS forms, macOS preferences, and Raycast form views.

## 13. Information Architecture & Navigation

### P23 — Rosenfeld, Morville & Arango (2015) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Louis Rosenfeld, Peter Morville, Jorge Arango
- **Year:** 2015 (4th edition)
- **Title:** Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond
- **Journal/Source:** O'Reilly Media (book). ISBN: 978-1491911686
- **DOI:** N/A (book)
- **Citations:** Canonical reference text, thousands of citations across editions
- **Key Finding:** Established the foundational framework of information architecture: organization systems, labeling systems, navigation systems, and search systems, all operating within the intersection of users, content, and context.
- **Designation:** Supplementary (book, not paper)
- **Relevance:** The definitive reference for IA. Directly relevant to how the reader should structure navigation, organize settings, and label features in their apps. The "users + content + context" triad is the most practical IA framework available.

## 14. Mobile Usability & Touch Interaction

### P24 — Henze, Rukzio & Boll (2011) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Niels Henze, Enrico Rukzio, Susanne Boll
- **Year:** 2011
- **Title:** 100,000,000 taps: Analysis and improvement of touch performance in the large
- **Journal/Source:** MobileHCI '11: Proceedings of the 13th International Conference on Human-Computer Interaction with Mobile Devices and Services, pp. 133-142
- **DOI:** 10.1145/2037373.2037395
- **Citations:** ~200+
- **Key Finding:** Analyzed 100 million touch events from a deployed game. Users systematically tap below and to the right of intended targets. Touch accuracy varies by screen position — corners and edges are less accurate. Larger targets dramatically reduce error rates.
- **Designation:** Supplementary
- **Relevance:** Massive real-world dataset validating Fitts's law findings in mobile context. The systematic offset finding is directly relevant to iOS app design — interactive elements should account for touch imprecision, especially in corners.

## 15. Progressive Disclosure

### P25 — Tidwell (2010) — SUPPLEMENTARY
- **Authors:** Jenifer Tidwell
- **Year:** 2010 (2nd edition)
- **Title:** Designing Interfaces: Patterns for Effective Interaction Design
- **Journal/Source:** O'Reilly Media (book). ISBN: 978-1449379704
- **DOI:** N/A (book)
- **Citations:** Canonical pattern reference
- **Key Finding:** Documented progressive disclosure as a core interaction pattern: initially show only essential features/information, revealing complexity on demand. Reduces cognitive load for beginners while preserving power-user access.
- **Designation:** Supplementary (book, not paper)
- **Relevance:** Progressive disclosure is the primary strategy for managing complexity in apps — directly relevant to Raycast extensions (command palette with drill-down), iOS settings (basic view with "Advanced" sections), and macOS preferences.

## Coverage Gaps to Consider

These topics have thinner empirical paper coverage and may benefit from additional searching:
1. **Color theory applied to interfaces** — Research is fragmented; no single seminal paper. May need practitioner sources (e.g., Itten's color theory adapted to UI).
2. **Error prevention & recovery** — Well covered by Nielsen's heuristics (P07/P08) but no standalone landmark paper specific to error messaging in UI.
3. **Information architecture** — More practitioner literature than empirical papers. Rosenfeld & Morville (P23) is the canonical reference but is a book.
4. **Progressive disclosure** — Similarly more practitioner consensus than empirical. Nielsen's 2006 NN/g article is widely cited but not a peer-reviewed paper.
5. **Animation/motion** — Emerging area with less classical empirical work. P21 is the best available review.
