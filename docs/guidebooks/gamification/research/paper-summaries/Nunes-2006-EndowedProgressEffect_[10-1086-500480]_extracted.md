# The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Nunes, J. C. & Drèze, X., 2006
- **Study Type**: Multi-experiment (1 field experiment + 2 lab/scenario studies)
- **Sample**: Study 1: n=300 car wash customers (field); Study 2: n=146 undergraduate business students; Study 3: n=240 liquor store shoppers
- **DOI**: 10.1086/500480
- **PMID**: Not available

---

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Completion Rate (Study 1, field)**: Customers endowed with 2-of-10 stamps (20% artificial progress) on a loyalty card completed the program at 34% versus 19% for controls who received a standard 8-stamp card. Both groups required the same 8 real purchases.
  - Effect size: χ²(1) = 8.1, p < .01
  - Sample: n=300 (150 per condition); 80 cards redeemed total

- **Finding 2 — Inter-purchase Time (Study 1, field)**: Customers endowed with artificial progress returned an average of 2.9 fewer days between car washes compared to controls.
  - Effect size: F(1, 636) = 5.2, p < .05 (main effect of condition)
  - Goal gradient within condition: time between visits decreased by 0.5 days per additional purchase, F(1, 636) = 18.38, p < .01
  - Hazard rate model (stronger): condition χ²(1) = 9.97, p < .01; level (proximity) χ²(1) = 27.85, p < .01
  - Condition × level interaction: not significant in either analysis (F < 1 / χ² < 1)

- **Finding 3 — Perceived Progress vs. Sunk Cost (Study 2)**: Overall program impression increased as endowment increased from 0 to 2 to 5 credits (M₀ = 4.74, M₂ = 5.55, M₅ = 6.26), F(2, 143) = 11.91, p < .01. Crucially, the dollar value of the endowment ($12.50 vs. $30) had NO effect, F(1, 72) = 1.25, p = .27. This rules out sunk-cost/waste-aversion as the mechanism.
  - Effect size: No Cohen's d reported; F-values only

- **Finding 4 — Relative Progress Drives Effect (Study 2)**: Participants endowed with 5/15 progress (1/3 complete) rated programs higher than those endowed with 2/12 (1/6 complete), despite identical remaining effort (10 purchases in both cases).
  - Effect size: F(1, 72) = 3.79, p = .05
  - M₁/₃ = 6.26 vs. M₁/₆ = 5.55

- **Finding 5 — Points Medium Amplifies Effect (Study 3)**: Main effect of medium (points vs. purchases): F(1, 232) = 61.27, p < .01. Points-denominated endowments produced significantly higher program commitment than purchase-denominated ones.

- **Finding 6 — Reason for Endowment Amplifies Effect (Study 3)**: Main effect of endowment condition: F(3, 232) = 111.00, p < .01. Condition × medium interaction: F(3, 232) = 7.40, p < .01. When endowment was in purchases, no-reason condition did NOT differ from control (M = 4.24 vs. 4.06, p = .25). When endowment was in points, no-reason condition DID significantly exceed control (M = 4.79 vs. 4.11, p < .01).

- **Finding 7 — Specious Reason = Realistic Reason (Study 3)**: An arbitrary reason ("because you are here today") worked identically to a history-based reason ("because of your purchase record") for both points (M = 6.31 vs. 6.19, p = .48) and purchases (M = 5.33 vs. 5.20, p = .42). Any reason suffices.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Study 1 (Field Experiment — Car Wash):**
- Completion rate: 34% (endowed) vs. 19% (control); χ²(1) = 8.1, p < .01; n=300
- Inter-purchase time difference: 2.9 days less for endowed group; F(1, 636) = 5.2, p < .05
- Goal gradient slope: −0.5 days/purchase; F(1, 636) = 18.38, p < .01
- Hazard rate, condition: χ²(1) = 9.97, p < .01
- Hazard rate, level: χ²(1) = 27.85, p < .01
- Condition × level interaction: F < 1 (n.s.); χ² < 1 (n.s.)

**Study 2 (Scenario — Restaurant Loyalty Program):**
- Overall program impression, omnibus: F(2, 143) = 11.91, p < .01; n=146
- 0 vs. 2 credits: M = 4.74 vs. 5.55, p = .01
- 0 vs. 5 credits: M = 4.74 vs. 6.26, p < .01
- Endowment value ($12.50 vs. $30) effect: F(1, 72) = 1.25, p = .27 (null — key mechanism test)
- Relative progress (1/3 vs. 1/6 complete): F(1, 72) = 3.79, p = .05; M = 6.26 vs. 5.55

**Study 3 (Scenario — Wine Store Loyalty Program):**
- Endowment condition main effect: F(3, 232) = 111.00, p < .01; n=240 (n=30/cell)
- Medium main effect (points vs. purchases): F(1, 232) = 61.27, p < .01
- Condition × medium interaction: F(3, 232) = 7.40, p < .01
- Cell means (see full table under Moderator section)
- Scale: 7-point composite (attractiveness + likelihood to join + likelihood to complete)

Note: No Cohen's d or eta-squared reported across any study. Effects are directional and test-statistic only.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Primary mechanism — Perceived task incompleteness**: Framing a goal as "already begun and incomplete" (vs. "not yet begun") invokes Zeigarnik-type motivation to finish uncompleted actions. This is distinct from sunk-cost or waste-aversion. Study 2 directly isolates this by holding actual effort constant and varying only the fraction-complete perception.

- **Primary mechanism — Goal gradient compounding**: As people perceive proximity to a goal, effort accelerates. Artificial head-start creates earlier proximity, which then compounds naturally. Study 1 shows the gradient (−0.5 days/purchase) operates across both conditions, but endowed customers start higher on the gradient.

- **Moderator 1 — Progress currency (points vs. purchases)**: Points create a "medium maximization" illusion (Hsee et al. 2003) where the effort-medium return appears large. This means points-denominated endowments work WITHOUT requiring a reason, whereas purchase-denominated endowments require a reason to be effective.
  - Evidence: No-reason/points = 4.79 (sig above control 4.11, p < .01); No-reason/purchases = 4.24 (n.s. vs. control 4.06, p = .25)

- **Moderator 2 — Reason for endowment (none / specious / realistic)**: Providing any reason — even an arbitrary one — activates the effect when tracking in purchases. A reason appears to neutralize consumer skepticism (Persuasion Knowledge Model). Realistic vs. specious reasons do NOT differ significantly.
  - Evidence: Specious vs. realistic in points: p = .48; in purchases: p = .42

- **Null moderator — Absolute endowment value**: Dollar magnitude of the head-start ($12.50 vs. $30) has no effect on program appeal (F(1,72) = 1.25, p = .27). This rules out waste-aversion / sunk-cost explanations.

- **Null moderator — Condition × gradient interaction**: Goal gradient effect is parallel across conditions (non-significant interaction in both ANOVA and hazard rate model), suggesting the endowment shifts the intercept of effort rather than the slope.

## Study Design Details

**Study 1 (Field):**
- **Intervention**: 150 loyalty cards with 2 stamps pre-affixed ("endowed progress" condition); 150 standard 8-stamp cards (control). Both required 8 real purchases.
- **Control**: 8-of-8 card, no stamps, 0% starting progress
- **Follow-up**: 9 months (April–September 2004); no redemptions after September 17
- **Outcome**: Redemption rate + inter-purchase days
- **Setting**: Professional car wash, major metropolitan area; customers recruited on 2nd and 3rd Saturday in April 2004

**Study 2 (Scenario, lab):**
- **Design**: 2 (progress: 1/3 or 1/6 complete) × 2 (endowment value: $12.50 or $30) between-subjects, plus control. Full factorial + baseline.
- **Instrument**: Paper-and-pencil, hypothetical restaurant loyalty program. 9-point scales (likelihood to register, attractiveness, subjective likelihood of success). Cronbach's α = .80.
- **Population**: Undergraduate business students at West Coast university; participated for course credit

**Study 3 (Scenario, intercept):**
- **Design**: 2 (medium: points vs. purchases) × 4 (endowment: none / no reason / specious reason / realistic reason) between-subjects. n=30/cell.
- **Instrument**: 7-point scales (attractiveness, likelihood to join, likelihood to complete). Cronbach's α = .85.
- **Population**: Liquor store shoppers (prior customers only); n=240

## Limitations

- **Study 1 observation bias**: Behavioral data only collected from the 80 who completed the full program. Non-completers' effort trajectory is unobserved. The claimed effect on inter-purchase time is computed only among completers, not all 300 cardholders — this may introduce survivorship bias.
- **Studies 2 & 3 are hypothetical**: No real money, no real behavior. Self-reported likelihood of joining and completing is not the same as actual enrollment or completion.
- **Single endowment focus**: All studies examine a one-time head-start. Real programs may offer recurring endowments; the paper does not test whether the effect persists, decays, or reverses over multiple reward cycles.
- **Ceiling effects not tested**: The paper notes endowing 90 of 100 stamps is implausible; the optimal endowment size is unknown. No dose-response data.
- **Student sample in Study 2**: Undergraduates at a single West Coast business school; limited generalizability.
- **No standardized effect sizes**: No Cohen's d, partial eta-squared, or odds ratios reported. Magnitude of effects is difficult to compare across studies or to external benchmarks.
- **Possible framing confound**: Study 2 compares programs that require 10, 12, or 15 total purchases. Participants may have viewed fewer-purchase programs as inherently more attractive independent of the endowment, which the authors acknowledge but argue makes the result "conservative."
- **No clinical or compulsive populations**: The paper focuses entirely on reward-program behavior in typical consumers; no clinical boundary conditions are established.

---

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult consumers (car wash customers, liquor store shoppers); one study uses undergraduates
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: High — the mechanism (artificial head-start on a progress tracker) directly maps to habit-tracking app design (streak starters, "you're already X% there" onboarding)
- **Red flags**: None. No clinical populations, no compulsive behavior contexts. Commercial loyalty program framing is not directly transferable to health behavior without moderator testing, but the core effect is robust in normative consumer samples.

---

## Conclusions

The endowed progress effect demonstrates that providing artificial advancement toward a goal — moving the goal post back while simultaneously pre-filling progress — increases both task completion rates (34% vs. 19% in the field) and inter-purchase frequency. The mechanism is perceived relative progress (feeling further along) rather than loss aversion or sunk-cost reluctance. The effect is amplified when progress is tracked in points rather than purchases, and when any reason — even an arbitrary one — is given for the endowment. The authors conclude: "the endowed progress works best when a reason, even a seemingly arbitrary one, is offered for the endowment, and the effect is more profound when effort is recorded in an alternative currency or medium."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Goal Gradient Effect** (Hull 1932)
  - Components: Effort increases as proximity to goal decreases
  - Research alignment: Confirmed in Study 1 (−0.5 days/purchase, F = 18.38, p < .01)

- **Zeigarnik Effect** (Zeigarnik 1927)
  - Components: Uncompleted tasks create tension motivating completion
  - Research alignment: Proposed as primary mechanism for endowed progress; not directly operationalized but supported by the perceived-completion manipulation in Study 2

- **Medium Maximization** (Hsee et al. 2003)
  - Components: Intervening currency (points) creates an illusion of advantage, drawing attention to effort-medium ratio rather than effort-outcome ratio
  - Research alignment: Directly tested as moderator in Study 3; points condition significantly outperforms purchases condition (F(1,232) = 61.27, p < .01)

- **Persuasion Knowledge Model** (Friestad & Wright 1994)
  - Components: Consumers recognize persuasion tactics and become skeptical
  - Research alignment: Proposed mechanism explaining why reason for endowment matters; supported by Study 3 finding that purchases-based endowment without a reason fails to outperform control

## Quotable Passages

> "By converting a task requiring eight steps into a task requiring 10 steps, but with two steps already complete, the task is reframed as one that has been undertaken and incomplete rather than not yet begun. This increases the likelihood of task completion and decreases completion time." (Abstract)

> "We do not find any statistically significant difference, implying the dollar amount of the endowment, whether $12.50 or $30, had no discernible effect on overall impression... This suggests that reluctance to waste, self justification, and other explanations of a sunk cost effect based on the absolute size of the investment made are not likely to be the underlying cause of the endowed progress effect." (Study 2 Results, p. 15)

> "An entirely arbitrary reason was shown to work just as well as a reason based on purchase history for both points and purchases... It appears that providing a reason, any reason, can aid the effectiveness of an artificial endowment." (Study 3 Results, p. 19)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: General adult consumers in loyalty/frequency program contexts; effect demonstrated in field (car wash) and scenario-based settings (restaurant, wine store)
- **Applies to**: Situations where the task goal is discrete and extrinsic reward is contingent on full completion (task goals, not learning or performance goals)
- **Does NOT apply to**: Goals where partial completion still earns proportional reward (the effect depends on an all-or-nothing completion structure)
- **Does NOT apply to** (tentative): Endowments that are implausibly large relative to the total (e.g., 90 of 100 stamps); the authors note these would undermine credibility, but no threshold was empirically tested
- **Does NOT apply to**: Repeat reward cycles — the study only examines a single reward period. Whether the effect decays or inverts with repeated artificial endowments is unknown
- **Unknown/untested**: Optimal endowment percentage (20% was tested in Study 1; 1/3 and 1/6 in Study 2; 1/3 in Study 3 — no systematic dose-response across the full range)
- **Unknown/untested**: Long-term behavioral impact vs. enrollment/intention measures — Studies 2 and 3 measure self-reported likelihood, not actual behavior
- **Unknown/untested**: Whether the effect transfers to non-commercial self-improvement contexts (fitness, learning, habit change) or clinical populations
- **Unknown/untested**: Cultural boundary conditions (all studies conducted in the U.S.)

## Extraction Notes

This is a foundational paper for progress mechanics design. The key insight for NotionRPG is the **artificial head-start** manipulation — giving users "bonus XP" or "starting streak credit" upon onboarding does not require the user to earn it; the framing of incompleteness does the motivational work, not the economic value of what was given. Three direct design implications:

1. Start users at, e.g., "2 of 10" rather than "0 of 8" when task requirements are identical — the 20% artificial progress in the field experiment nearly doubled completion rate (19% → 34%).
2. Use points (XP, coins) rather than raw purchase/action counts when possible — points produce the effect even without explaining the endowment.
3. If using purchase/action tracking, always provide a reason for any head-start bonus, even a weak one ("welcome bonus" is sufficient).

Caution: the completion rate figures (34% vs. 19%) are striking but are based on a single field study with no preregistration, no standardized effect size, and survivorship bias in the time-between-visits analysis. Studies 2 and 3 are hypothetical. The effect is well-evidenced as a behavioral intention signal; its magnitude in real-world self-improvement apps needs independent replication.
