# Gamification of Task Performance with Leaderboards: A Goal Setting Experiment

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Landers, Bauer, & Callan, 2017 (published in Computers in Human Behavior)
- **Study Type**: Randomized experiment (single-factor between-subjects)
- **Sample**: n=240 (after exclusions from n=339), undergraduate students, southeastern US university; mean age 22.76 (SD=7.32); 74.6% female
- **Task**: 12-minute online brainstorming task (listing uses for a knife); performance = count of unique uses generated
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.008
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Leaderboard equals difficult/impossible goals**: The leaderboard condition produced mean performance (M=37.52, SD=19.45) statistically equivalent to both the difficult goal condition (M=36.56, SD=14.35) and the impossible goal condition (M=41.55, SD=16.34). No significant difference between leaderboard and difficult (B=-4.21, p=.155) or impossible (B=1.93, p=.529).

- **Finding 2 — Leaderboard beats do-your-best and easy**: The leaderboard significantly outperformed do-your-best (M=23.84, SD=12.88; B=-16.67, p<.001, sr2=.083) and easy goal conditions (M=22.10, SD=9.22; B=-18.29, p<.001, sr2=.093). Overall ANOVA: F(4,235)=16.62, p<.001, partial eta2=.220.

- **Finding 3 — Implicit goal adoption without instruction**: Participants in the leaderboard condition were never told what score to aim for, yet their aggregate output clustered around the difficult-to-impossible range. This confirms the implicit goal-setting mechanism: the presence of visible scores induces spontaneous self-regulation toward top-ranked targets.

- **Finding 4 — Goal commitment predicts performance across all conditions**: Goal commitment (5-item scale, alpha=.80) was a significant positive predictor of performance in Step 1 of regression: B=7.89, p<.001, sr2=.107. In the full model, goal commitment in the leaderboard condition became B=14.87 (p<.001).

- **Finding 5 — Goal commitment moderates leaderboard effect**: The interaction between leaderboard condition and goal commitment was significant vs. the easy goal condition (B=-11.39, p=.024, sr2=.015) and approached significance vs. do-your-best (B=-8.74, p=.087, sr2=.008). The goal commitment-performance slope was steepest in the leaderboard condition, consistent with goal-setting theory prediction for difficult goals.

- **Finding 6 — Leaderboard condition had lowest mean goal commitment**: Despite highest performance, leaderboard participants had the lowest mean goal commitment (M=3.33, SD=0.59) compared to all goal conditions (range 3.60-3.75). This suggests the mechanism is not just engagement -- those who do commit to leaderboard goals show disproportionately high performance.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Overall condition effect on performance**: partial eta2=.220, F(4,235)=16.62, p<.001
- **Leaderboard vs. do-your-best**: B=-16.67, beta=-.41, sr2=.083, p<.001
- **Leaderboard vs. easy goal**: B=-18.29, beta=-.42, sr2=.093, p<.001
- **Leaderboard vs. difficult goal**: B=-4.21, beta=-.12, sr2=.006, p=.155 (ns)
- **Leaderboard vs. impossible goal**: B=1.93, beta=.05, sr2=.001, p=.529 (ns)
- **Goal commitment main effect (Model 1)**: B=7.89, beta=.33, sr2=.107, p<.001
- **Goal commitment in leaderboard condition (Model 2)**: B=14.87, beta=.63, p<.001
- **Interaction: leaderboard x goal commitment vs. easy**: B=-11.39, beta=-.21, sr2=.015, p=.024
- **Interaction: leaderboard x goal commitment vs. DYB**: B=-8.74, beta=-.15, sr2=.008, p=.087 (trend)
- **R2 Model 1**: .327; **DR2 Model 2**: .019
- **Correlation: goal commitment x performance**: r=.29, p<.001 (from Table 2)

Note: No Cohen's d values directly reported for leaderboard vs. goal conditions. Effect sizes expressed as semi-partial r2 and standardized betas throughout.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — Implicit goal induction**: Leaderboards work by presenting a ranked set of visible reference scores. Without explicit instruction, users spontaneously select a target from those scores and engage in self-regulation (effort adjustment) to reduce the gap between current performance and target. The top or near-top score tends to be adopted, mirroring the effect of an explicitly assigned difficult goal.

- **Mechanism — Self-regulation loop**: Participants received real-time performance feedback (live count of unique uses generated), enabling continuous comparison to the leaderboard. This closed-loop feedback-goal comparison is the proposed causal pathway.

- **Moderator — Goal commitment**: Individuals who are more committed to the implicit leaderboard goal show disproportionately higher performance. The slope of commitment-performance is steeper in the leaderboard condition than in do-your-best or easy conditions, consistent with goal-setting theory: commitment matters more when goals are difficult.

- **Moderator (proposed, untested) — Task complexity**: Authors note (citing Wood et al., 1987) that goal-setting is more effective for simple tasks with clear effort-outcome links. Leaderboards on simple, countable tasks (sales, completions) likely outperform leaderboards on complex tasks (managerial quality, creative output).

- **Moderator (proposed, untested) — Social presence on leaderboard**: Leaderboard in this study used random initials -- not real peers. Authors note results may differ if scores were attributed to known coworkers or competitors.

## Study Design Details

- **Conditions**: 5-arm between-subjects RCT — do-your-best, easy goal (15 uses), difficult goal (39 uses), impossible goal (53 uses), leaderboard
- **Leaderboard design**: Pre-populated with five fictitious scores corresponding to impossible (53 uses = 371 pts), difficult (39 = 273 pts), two intermediate scores, and easy (15 = 105 pts) positions; displayed as points (7x multiplier); participants' live rank updated in real time
- **Control/Comparison**: Do-your-best = standard baseline ("list as many as possible")
- **Follow-up period**: None -- single-session, 12 minutes
- **Attrition/Exclusions**: 99 excluded from n=339 (80 failed manipulation check, 13 performance issues, 5 careless responding, 1 incomplete); final n=240
- **Blinding**: Single-blind (participants unaware of other conditions)

## Limitations

- **Short task duration**: 12-minute task; novelty effect cannot be ruled out as a performance driver. Authors explicitly flag Koivisto & Hamari (2014) novelty concern.
- **Undergraduate sample only**: All participants were university students recruited via subject pool for credit. Does not generalize to employees, older adults, or intrinsically motivated learners without course credit incentive.
- **Simple brainstorming task**: Convergent validity to complex real-world tasks is undemonstrated. Wood et al. (1987) meta-analysis shows effect sizes shrink with task complexity.
- **No real social presence**: Leaderboard used random initials, not real peers. The competitive social dimension -- likely part of leaderboard effectiveness in apps -- was absent.
- **Goal choice not tracked**: Unknown which specific leaderboard position participants actually targeted. Authors acknowledge it may have been progressive goal revision (start low, revise up) rather than initial top targeting.
- **Goal commitment may not be manipulable**: Authors note this is an individual difference that may be stable prior to gamification exposure, limiting its practical utility for designers.
- **No quality measure**: Performance = quantity only. Effects on idea quality, accuracy, or deeper engagement are unknown.
- **Unstated: demand characteristics**: Online study with manipulation check items about the leaderboard may have cued performance-relevant attention.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University undergraduates (student, not general adult)
- **Relevance to consumer app / EdTech design**: Medium-High -- brainstorming task is analogous to countable productivity tasks (contributions, completions, attempts); student population overlaps with EdTech target users
- **Red flags**: None (no clinical population, no addiction/compulsion context, standard motivated performance task)
- **Generalization caveat**: Findings are most directly applicable to simple, countable, discrete-output tasks (e.g., quiz completions, code commits, content submissions). Caution warranted for complex skill or quality-dependent tasks.

## Conclusions

"The presence of a leaderboard was successful in motivating participants to performance levels similar to that of difficult and impossible goal-setting, suggesting participants implicitly set goals at or near the top of the leaderboard without any prompting to do so." Goal commitment behaved as goal-setting theory predicts: it was a stronger moderator in the leaderboard condition than in low-difficulty conditions. Authors conclude that goal-setting theory is a productive lens for understanding why leaderboards work, and call for more theory-grounded gamification research.

## Extraction Notes

This is a clean, well-designed experiment with a clear theoretical framework and the primary merit of isolating the leaderboard as a single variable (most leaderboard studies bundle it with badges, points, etc.). The practical upshot for app designers is precise: leaderboards work because they induce implicit difficult goals, not simply because they're engaging or social. The commitment moderator has direct design implications -- users who don't buy into the competitive frame will not benefit, and may even disengage. The study's weaknesses (undergraduate sample, 12-minute task, no real social presence) are real but the theoretical mechanism is well-grounded in 40+ years of goal-setting research. Treat effect sizes as upper bounds for real-world app contexts with longer timescales and more complex tasks. Partial eta2=.220 is a large effect, but driven heavily by the contrast between leaderboard/difficult/impossible vs. do-your-best/easy -- not a claim that leaderboards are universally powerful.
