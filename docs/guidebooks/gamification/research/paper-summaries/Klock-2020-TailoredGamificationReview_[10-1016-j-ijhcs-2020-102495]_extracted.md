# Tailored Gamification: A Review of Literature

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Klock, A.C.T., Gasparini, I., Pimenta, M.S., & Hamari, J., 2020
- **Study Type**: Systematic Review (k=42 primary studies)
- **Sample**: 42 included studies; underlying participant samples range from n=74 to n=6,134 across included studies; dominant methods = surveys and questionnaires
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2020.102495
- **PMID**: Not available

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1: Most tailored gamification research is still in user-modeling mode, not implementation mode.**
  - 67% of the 42 included studies (28/42) focused on building or proposing user models for personalization, not on actually implementing or evaluating personalized gamification in practice.
  - Only 7 studies (16%) personalized, 5 (12%) adapted, and 2 (4%) recommended game elements. The field is more descriptive than prescriptive.
  - Effect size: None reported (qualitative synthesis).

- **Finding 2: No single game element works for all user types — there is no "silver bullet."**
  - Across all player typologies, genders, and personality frameworks reviewed, zero game elements were universally preferred by all users. Every element had a moderating characteristic.
  - This is the paper's central practical conclusion.

- **Finding 3: The most frequently studied game elements in tailored contexts are customization (55%), badges (52%), challenges and levels (43%), and competition and leaderboards (40%).**
  - The classic PBL triad (Points, Badges, Leaderboards) is no longer dominant in tailored gamification research; researchers have shifted to a wider element set.
  - Effect size: Frequencies of use in literature, not outcome effect sizes.

- **Finding 4: Bartle typology — specific game elements map to specific player types (strongest evidence base: 7 studies).**
  - Achievers: challenges and levels (3 studies), plus badges, customization, leaderboards (2 studies each).
  - Explorers: collections (3 studies), plus badges, challenges, customization, exploration, feedback, guilds, points (2 each).
  - Killers: badges, leaderboards, levels (3 studies each), plus challenges, points, prizes, social pressure, social status (2 each).
  - Socializers: guilds (3 studies), plus customization, social networks (2 each).
  - Divergence note: 18 elements suggested for achievers, 20 for explorers, 16 for killers, 13 for socializers — imbalance unexplained.

- **Finding 5: Hexad typology — strong evidence for specific element-type pairings (6 studies).**
  - Achievers: challenges and levels (strong evidence, ≥3 studies each); also learning, social status, unlockables (half of Hexad studies).
  - Free spirits: unlockables and customization (strong); exploration (moderate).
  - Players: leaderboards (strong); also badges, collections, competition, points, prizes, unlockables, virtual economy (moderate).
  - Socializers: social networks and competition (strong); also social status (moderate).
  - Philanthropists: gifting (moderate).
  - Disruptors: customization only (1 study — least evidence base).
  - Total game elements proposed: Players (44), Achievers (38), Socializers (32), Free spirits (23), Philanthropists (22), Disruptors (21).

- **Finding 6: Gender differences in element preference are real but nuanced.**
  - Women (3 studies each): badges, customization, leaderboards, levels. Also guilds, points, social status (2 studies).
  - Men (3 studies): competition. Also customization, guilds, prizes (2 studies).
  - Femininity identity dimension: competition, consequences, customization, feedback, prizes, signposting, social status (1 study, Busch et al. 2016).
  - Masculinity identity dimension: no game elements suggested in the literature — a significant gap.
  - Note: gender identity (femininity/masculinity) and biological gender interact in ways not yet studied together.

- **Finding 7: Big Five personality traits produce element recommendations, with extroversion most studied.**
  - High extroversion (3 studies): customization and leaderboards; plus badges, competition, feedback, levels, meaning, points, social networks (2 studies).
  - High neuroticism (2 studies): badges, levels, prizes.
  - High openness to experience (1 study): customization.
  - Extroversion accounted for 49% of all Big Five game element suggestions — other traits severely underresearched.
  - Low agreeableness, low conscientiousness, and low neuroticism polarities: not studied at all.

- **Finding 8: Educational context dominates (60% of studies), limiting generalizability.**
  - 25/42 studies (59.5%) were in education. Generic (23.8%), health (7.1%), academia, ecology, government, services (2.4% each).
  - Educational studies used badges, leaderboards, levels, challenges, customization, and points as primary elements.
  - Generic-context studies prioritized competition, prizes, customization, guilds, and learning — a different profile from education.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

No quantitative effect sizes (Cohen's d, OR, SMD) are reported by this review. The paper is a qualitative systematic review synthesizing 42 studies. Most underlying studies used surveys and questionnaires with correlation/regression methods; the review does not aggregate these statistically.

Sample sizes from underlying evaluation methods:
- Surveys: n=6,134 cumulative (avg. ~323/study, across 19 survey studies)
- Storyboards: n=3,351 cumulative (avg. ~838/study, 4 studies)
- Questionnaires: n=1,760 cumulative (avg. ~110/study, 16 questionnaire studies)

Outcome emphases across the 42 studies:
- Motivation: 36% of outcomes; cumulative n=1,247
- Persuasion: 27% of outcomes; cumulative n=4,569
- Other outcomes: enjoyment (5 studies), performance (4), perception (2), playfulness (2), attitude (2), effectiveness (2), interaction (7 studies)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Player type as primary moderator**: Player typology (Bartle, Hexad, BrainHex, Ferro et al.) is the dominant user characteristic studied — 45% of all included works used it. The typology used affects which elements are recommended; recommendations are not interchangeable across typologies.

- **Gender moderates element efficacy**: Competition, which is broadly used in gamification, is recommended for men but not women in the gender literature. Leaderboards are recommended for women and killers (Bartle) — but appear in multiple player type categories, indicating cross-cutting utility when applied to specific populations.

- **Personality (Big Five) moderates element utility**: Extroversion appears to predict broad gamification susceptibility — extroverts had the most game elements suggested. Neuroticism produces a narrower, distinct profile (badges, levels, prizes). Introversion was underresearched.

- **Context moderates element selection**: Educational contexts favor PBL-adjacent elements; generic contexts favor competition/prizes/social elements. Element recommendations from education may not generalize to other contexts.

- **Study duration as moderator (from cited prior review)**: Hallifax et al. (2019a) found that studies lasting less than two weeks were more likely to find positive effects than longer experiments. This finding is noted by the authors as a critical caution for interpreting included evidence.

- **Behavior/usage frequency**: More frequent users (daily/weekly) benefit from a wider range of elements. Low-frequency users (monthly): primarily badges. Time pressure works for users accessing at least every 3 days.

- **Goal orientation**: Badges suited for mastery-approach and performance-approach users. Leaderboards suggested for performance-avoidance (1 study only). Performance-avoidance users appear to differ meaningfully from others.

- **Culture (individualism/collectivism)**: Prizes preferred by collectivists (2 studies). Competition produced contradictory findings across the 2 studies — insufficient evidence to draw conclusions.

- **Age**: Competition and learning recommended for younger users (under 25-30). No recommendations exist for users over 30 — either a research gap or a genuine age-related boundary condition.

## Study Design Details

- **Approach**: Systematic literature review following Kitchenham et al. (2009) protocol.
- **Search period**: September 2018 (publication cutoff: 2013 onward — 5 years).
- **Search engines**: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Science Direct, SpringerLink.
- **Initial results**: 3,400 studies returned; 34 met selection criteria after 9-stage filtering.
- **Snowballing**: 1,720 references of selected studies reviewed; 8 additional studies added. Final corpus: 42.
- **Data extraction**: Coded by one researcher (threat to internal validity).
- **Evaluation**: No meta-analytic aggregation; qualitative synthesis of findings across studies.
- **Primary outcomes of included studies**: Mostly self-report (surveys, questionnaires); 9/42 studies had no empirical evaluation at all — proposal/framework only.

## Limitations

- **Dominated by user modeling, not implementation**: 67% of studies propose user models but don't test personalized delivery. Real-world personalization outcomes are severely underrepresented.
- **Educational context bias**: 60% education. Results may not transfer to habit-tracking, productivity, or wellness contexts.
- **Static modeling**: Most studies rely on one-time questionnaires to determine user type. Dynamic user characteristics (change over time, context) are not captured. User profiles may drift.
- **Single-characteristic focus**: Only 5/40 empirical studies examined more than one user characteristic simultaneously. Interaction effects between gender, personality, and player type are essentially unstudied.
- **No balance across player types**: Explorers and achievers have disproportionately more game element suggestions; survivors and philanthropists are barely studied. May reflect sample composition bias, not genuine preference differences.
- **Short study durations inflate positive effects**: Most evaluations are brief; Hallifax et al. found <2-week studies show more positive outcomes.
- **Neutral and negative effects unstudied**: No study examined which elements actively harm specific user types. Literature only documents positive pairings.
- **Single-researcher coding**: All studies coded by one researcher — conclusion and internal validity threat.
- **Age gap**: No game element recommendations for users over 30. Unclear if this is a research gap or a real boundary.
- **Search string may miss relevant work**: The string excluded "customization" as a search term (to avoid unrelated results), potentially omitting relevant papers that used that framing.
- **Conflicting findings across typologies**: Same element (e.g., challenges) recommended to different player types in different typologies — suggests typologies are measuring overlapping but distinct constructs.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed; predominantly student samples (educational LMS users: Moodle, Projet Voltaire). Some general adult samples in health and generic context studies.
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium. The element-to-user-type mappings have direct design implications for any gamified behavior change system, but the underlying evidence comes mostly from education, not general habit tracking or wellness.
- **Red flags**: No clinical populations. No addictive behaviors. The evidence base is largely self-report and observational, not experimental. Causal claims about which elements "work" for which users are not warranted by the current evidence.
- **Age scope**: Predominantly 18-30 (student samples). No evidence base for 30+ users.

## Conclusions

"These results reaffirm the importance of considering the user profile in gamification processes, since no game element was suggested regardless of the user characteristic (i.e., there is no silver bullet)." The review finds that tailored gamification is still in early stages — most research proposes user models rather than delivering and testing personalized experiences. The most reliably studied mappings involve Bartle and Hexad typologies in educational settings. Gender, personality traits, age, culture, and motivation orientation all meaningfully moderate element preferences, but evidence for most of these is thin (1-3 studies each). The field urgently needs dynamic modeling, multi-characteristic studies, and experimental designs outside education.

## Quotable Passages

> "These results reaffirm the importance of considering the user profile in gamification processes, since no game element was suggested regardless of the user characteristic (i.e., there is no silver bullet)." (Section 6, Conclusion)

> "Future research should consider dynamic modeling: Most of the current studies presented static user modeling proposals that rely on the fulfillment of a questionnaire or survey by each individual. However, if the collected data are unintentionally or deliberately inaccurate (or even missing, if someone refuses to answer), tailoring methods might not provide the most suitable game elements for that specific user." (Section 5, Research Agenda)

> "While studies focused on the positive effects (e.g., motivation, persuasion) promoted by the suggested game elements, neutral and adverse effects are still unknown. For instance, does suggesting collection for individuals with high neuroticism imply that all other polarities of Big Five personality traits do not care or bother about this game element (i.e., neutral effect) or that is not suggested to other cases because they create unfavorable experiences (e.g., anxiety, sadness, fear)." (Section 5, Research Agenda)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: General adults and students in digital systems; findings strongest for Hexad and Bartle player types; educational context; users aged 18–30.
- **Does NOT apply to**: Users over 30 (no evidence base); clinical populations; non-digital gamification; users with non-Western cultural backgrounds (individualism/collectivism findings are contradictory and thin).
- **Unknown/untested**:
  - Negative or neutral effects of any game element on any user type
  - Interaction effects between multiple user characteristics (e.g., a neurotic extrovert killer-type)
  - Long-term outcomes (most studies < 2 weeks)
  - Dynamic preference change over time
  - Masculinity dimension of gender identity
  - Any user characteristic in non-educational, non-generic contexts
  - Users over 30 years old

## Extraction Notes

This is a qualitative synthesis, not a meta-analysis — no pooled effect sizes exist. Its value to the NotionRPG pipeline is in the player-type-to-element mapping tables, which are directly applicable to designing personalization logic. Key actionable takeaways:

1. Hexad typology has the most directly applicable framework for a gamified habit tracker (it was designed for gamified systems, not video games like Bartle).
2. The competition/leaderboard element should not be applied uniformly — men and killers/players prefer it; women and socializers generally do not.
3. Customization appears across nearly all typologies and both genders — it may be the closest thing to a universally safe element.
4. Badges are popular but only moderately differentiated by user type — they work for many types but are not strongly distinctive.
5. The research is pre-2019 (search cutoff Sept 2018) — a meaningful gap for a 2020-published review. More recent work on tailored gamification likely exists.
6. The near-total absence of outcome effect sizes is a real limitation for evidence-tier assessment. This paper is better treated as a design reference than an efficacy source.
