# Measuring Flow in Gamification: Dispositional Flow Scale-2

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Hamari, J. & Koivisto, J. (2014). Computers in Human Behavior, 40, 133-143.
- **Study Type**: Cross-sectional psychometric validation (CFA + EFA)
- **Sample**: n=200, users of Fitocracy (gamified exercise tracking app), mean age=29.5, 51% female, mean 5.3 exercise sessions/week
- **Recruitment**: Online questionnaire posted to Fitocracy forum; $50 Amazon gift certificate incentive; registered users only
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.chb.2014.07.048

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — DFS-2 original model fits at original thresholds, not stricter modern ones.** The 9-factor first-order DFS-2 (Jackson & Eklund, 2002) achieved CFI=0.918, NNFI=0.907, RMSEA=0.066 in this gamification sample — adequate by the original authors' threshold of 0.90, but failing the current preferred threshold of 0.95. The second-order version was nearly identical: CFI=0.906, RMSEA=0.069. This is roughly the same performance as the original validation sample (n=574), suggesting the instrument translates to gamification contexts under original standards.

- **Finding 2 — Flow components divide into conditions vs. outcomes.** EFA and subsequent CFA converge on a distinction that aligns with Csikszentmihalyi's theoretical account: (a) *Conditions* — challenge-skill balance, clear goals, feedback, control, autotelic experience — these are highly intercorrelated and represent prerequisites for flow. (b) *Outcomes* — merging action-awareness, concentration, loss of self-consciousness, time transformation — these are experiential results of reaching flow. This structure is novel in that it empirically surfaces what was previously a theoretical claim.

- **Finding 3 — Autotelic experience behaves as a condition, not an outcome, in gamification.** In this gamified exercise context, autotelic experience (intrinsic motivation / self-purposefulness) loaded strongly with other conditions (CSB, goals, control) rather than with outcomes. This contrasts with gaming contexts (Procci et al., 2012) where autotelic experience was separable. Proposed explanation: video games can produce intrinsic enjoyment through audiovisual immersion independent of goal-oriented mastery; gamification typically lacks rich audiovisual stimuli, so intrinsic motivation is only activated through the mastery/challenge-skill loop.

- **Finding 4 — Gamification surfaces the "conditions" dimensions most strongly.** Mean scores across flow components (7-point Likert scale) showed that conditions-of-flow dimensions scored highest: autotelic experience (M=5.81, SD=1.02), clear goals (M=5.72, SD=1.03), feedback (M=5.69, SD=1.11), control (M=5.50, SD=1.11), challenge-skill balance (M=5.30, SD=1.01). Outcome dimensions scored lower: time transformation (M=3.54, SD=1.49) was notably the lowest, merging action-awareness (M=4.26, SD=1.17) was second lowest. This suggests gamification reliably induces the structural conditions for flow but not necessarily the deep immersive outcomes.

- **Finding 5 — Adequate fit achievable with respecification retaining all 36 items.** A model grouping CSB, goals, feedback, control, and autotelic into a second-order "Mastery" factor, alongside four separate first-order factors (time, MAA, concentration, LSC), achieved CFI=0.907, NNFI=0.900, RMSEA=0.069 with discriminant validity confirmed. This represents the best compromise between fit, parsimony, and retaining the full instrument.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Conditions vs. outcomes mechanism**: The paper proposes (and empirically supports) that challenge-skill balance, clear goals, feedback, and control are structural prerequisites that, when met, produce experiential outcomes (loss of self-consciousness, time distortion, merging of action and awareness). Gamification interfaces primarily operate on the conditions side. This implies a partial-flow effect: gamification reliably triggers the input side of flow but may not reliably produce deep immersive outcomes without richer contextual stimuli.

- **Gamification vs. gaming context moderator**: In pure video games, autotelic experience can arise from audiovisual engagement and narrative immersion independent of mastery. In gamification (which typically lacks rich AV), autotelic experience is contingent on mastery/challenge-skill match. This has design implications: gamification must nail the challenge-skill calibration to generate intrinsic motivation, whereas games have additional hedonic levers.

- **No explicit moderator analyses reported** beyond context comparisons discussed in the discussion section.

## Study Design Details

- **Instrument**: Dispositional Flow Scale-2 (DFS-2) — 36 items across 9 constructs, 7-point Likert scale; dispositional (tendency to experience flow in a context) rather than state (flow in a single instance)
- **Platform**: Fitocracy — a gamified exercise app using points, levels, achievements, quests, and social features
- **Analysis approach**: Confirmatory factor analysis (AMOS 21) followed by exploratory factor analysis (SPSS 21); 6-step iterative respecification process
- **No intervention / no control group** — purely observational, single-timepoint
- **Sampling**: Convenience sample from app's own forum; self-selection bias toward engaged users is likely

## Limitations

- **Highly active user sample**: Mean 5.3 exercise sessions/week, mean 7.2 hours/week — far above general population. These are committed exercisers who self-selected into a gamified system. Flow scores may not generalize to casual/reluctant users.
- **Cross-sectional, single timepoint**: No causal claims can be made. Cannot determine whether gamification caused flow or whether flow-prone users self-selected into gamified exercise.
- **Self-report only**: DFS-2 is entirely self-reported; dispositional measure captures general tendencies, not moment-to-moment experience.
- **Single platform**: Fitocracy's specific mechanic mix (points, levels, achievements, quests, social) may not generalize to other gamification implementations with different mechanic portfolios.
- **Convenience/forum sample**: Participants recruited from within the app's discussion forum — likely more engaged and community-oriented than the average user.
- **Threshold ambiguity**: The paper's own main finding (DFS-2 fits) depends entirely on which goodness-of-fit threshold is adopted. The original threshold (CFI>0.90) validates the instrument; modern thresholds (CFI>0.95) do not, requiring respecification.
- **n=200 is borderline**: Sufficient by cited methodological standards but on the lower end for complex SEM with 36 items.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Highly active adult fitness app users (Fitocracy), mean age 29.5
- **Relevance to consumer app gamification**: Medium-High — exercise app is a legitimate gamification context, but sample skews toward already-motivated exercisers
- **Relevance to education/knowledge work gamification**: Low-Medium — structural findings about flow conditions vs. outcomes are transferable; specific mean values are not
- **Red flags**: Extreme self-selection (dedicated exercisers in a fitness-specific community); no clinical population concerns

## Conclusions

The DFS-2 flow measurement instrument can be validly applied to gamification contexts at original model-fit thresholds, and with respecification at modern thresholds. More substantively, the paper provides empirical support for a conditions/outcomes distinction within flow: gamification reliably induces the structural conditions (clear goals, feedback, challenge-skill balance, control, intrinsic motivation) but not necessarily the deep experiential outcomes (time distortion, loss of self-consciousness, action-awareness merging) that define the classic flow state. As the authors state: "ﬂow should rather be seen as divided between the collection of conditions for reaching ﬂow state and the psychological outcomes that follow from reaching the ﬂow state instead of merely seeing all of the nine dimensions as reﬂective indicators of the entirety of ﬂow."

## Named Frameworks

- **DFS-2 (Dispositional Flow Scale-2)**
  - Developed by: Jackson & Eklund (2002)
  - Components (9): Challenge-skill balance, Merging action-awareness, Clear goals, Feedback, Concentration, Control, Loss of self-consciousness, Time transformation, Autotelic experience
  - Each construct: 4 items, 7-point Likert, 36 items total
  - Dispositional (general tendency in a context) vs. FSS-2 (state scale for single activity instance)
  - Research alignment in gamification context:
    - Challenge-skill balance -> Points, leveling systems calibrating difficulty
    - Clear goals -> Quests, missions, progress bars
    - Feedback -> Real-time score displays, achievement notifications
    - Control -> User agency in activity selection
    - Autotelic experience -> Intrinsic motivation (correlated with above in gamification, unlike in games)
    - Time transformation -> Weak in gamification; strongest in deep gaming immersion
    - Merging action-awareness -> Weak in gamification; requires richer AV context

## Extraction Notes

This is primarily a psychometric methods paper. Its direct value to the guidebook is not about effect sizes on behavior change but about two conceptual contributions:

1. **Flow operationalization**: Provides the clearest breakdown of which flow dimensions gamification reliably activates (conditions: goals, feedback, skill-challenge, control) vs. which it typically does not (outcomes: time distortion, loss of self-consciousness). This is directly useful for chapter writing on flow and engagement mechanics.

2. **The partial-flow insight**: Gamification creates the scaffolding for flow but may not produce full flow states. This is a practical and honest framing for a guidebook — don't oversell flow as the outcome; position gamification as a structural enabler of flow conditions.

The conditions/outcomes distinction is theoretically grounded (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) and empirically corroborated here. However, the sample (highly active Fitocracy users, N=200, single platform) limits generalizability claims. Treat the mean-score ordering of flow components as illustrative, not definitive.

Scope flag "include-with-caution" is appropriate: useful conceptual content, but the psychometric focus and narrow sample mean it should be a supporting cite for flow theory, not a primary evidence source for behavioral outcomes.
