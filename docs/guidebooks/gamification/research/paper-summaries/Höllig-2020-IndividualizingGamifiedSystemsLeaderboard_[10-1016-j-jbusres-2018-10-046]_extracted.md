# The Interaction of Trait Competitiveness and Leaderboard Design: An Experimental Analysis of Effects on Perceptions and Usage Intention

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Höllig, C. E., Tumasjan, A., & Welpe, I. M., 2020 (published at HICSS 2018; journal version in J. Business Research)
- **Study Type**: Experimental vignette study (between-subjects factorial design)
- **Sample**: n=192, general population (recruited online April/May 2017); 61.5% students, 25.5% employed; predominantly European; ages 20–29 (85%)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.jbusres.2018.10.046
- **PMID**: N/A

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Trait competitiveness drives all gamification perceptions**: Personal development competitiveness (PDC) positively predicted every measured perception outcome — playfulness, enjoyment, usefulness, reciprocal benefits, and recognition — at p < .001.
  - β values: playfulness β=.389, enjoyment β=.473, usefulness β=.524, reciprocal benefits β=.533, recognition β=.482 (all p < .001)
  - Model R² for each perception dimension ranged from .185 (recognition) to .333 (reciprocal benefits)

- **Finding 2 — PDC predicts usage intention**: Personal development competitiveness positively predicted usage intention (β=.497, p < .001). Model R²=.298 vs. baseline R²=.035 (controls only), confirming trait competitiveness as a dominant predictor.

- **Finding 3 — Team-based leaderboard moderates enjoyment for high-PDC users**: The interaction of PDC × leaderboard design significantly predicted enjoyment (β=.143, p < .05), with high-PDC individuals rating team-based leaderboards as more enjoyable than player-based.

- **Finding 4 — Team-based leaderboard moderates reciprocal benefits**: Interaction of PDC × leaderboard design significantly predicted reciprocal benefits (β=.135, p < .05). High-PDC individuals perceived greater social/mutual benefit from team-based leaderboards.

- **Finding 5 — Team-based leaderboard moderates usage intention**: The interaction of PDC × leaderboard design significantly predicted usage intention (β=.148, p < .05). High-PDC individuals showed greater intention to use the system when it used a team-based leaderboard.

- **Finding 6 — Team-based leaderboard reduces recognition**: A significant negative main effect of team-based leaderboard design on recognition was found (β=−.141, p < .05). Team leaderboards obscure individual contributions, reducing perceived recognition opportunity — regardless of competitiveness level.

- **Finding 7 — Leaderboard design type did not moderate playfulness, usefulness, or recognition**: Interactions for playfulness, usefulness, and recognition were non-significant, suggesting these perceptions are tied to the overall system rather than leaderboard format.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — PDC and social orientation**: Individuals high in personal development competitiveness value personal growth through competition rather than winning per se. They tend toward social concern, cooperative behavior, and high self-esteem, which explains their preference for team-based over player-based competitive structures.

- **Moderator — Leaderboard type (team vs. player)**: Team-based leaderboards amplified the positive effect of PDC on enjoyment, reciprocal benefits, and usage intention. Proposed mechanism: high-PDC individuals subordinate personal interests to group welfare; player-based leaderboards create perceived social threat or conflict.

- **Moderator — Recognition trade-off**: Team-based leaderboards reduced perceived recognition because individual actions become indistinguishable within team performance. This is a direct cost of team leaderboard design independent of competitiveness level.

- **Non-moderator**: Playfulness and usefulness were not moderated by leaderboard type — authors interpret these as global system perceptions not sensitive to leaderboard format differences.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Between-subjects experimental vignette; participants assigned to either player-based or team-based leaderboard condition within a fictional work gamification scenario ("Gamely" internal company information system)
- **Control/Comparison**: Player-based leaderboard vs. team-based leaderboard; controls-only baseline model included gender, age, education, employment status, nationality
- **Follow-up period**: Single time point (cross-sectional); outcomes are attitudinal/intentional, not behavioral
- **Attrition**: 21 participants excluded for failing manipulation check; no further attrition reported
- **Blinding**: Not applicable (vignette design; participants saw only one condition)
- **Measurement**: All constructs on 7-point Likert scales; PDC measured via 15-item scale (Ryckman et al., 1996), reduced to 10 items after loading checks

## Limitations

- **Vignette realism gap**: Experimental vignette methodology produces self-reported attitudes and intentions, not actual behavior. Results may not translate to real usage or performance outcomes.
- **Self-selected sample**: Participants were recruited online and may skew toward individuals already interested in gamification, inflating enthusiasm for the system.
- **Predominantly student sample**: 61.5% students, 85% aged 20–29; generalizability to mature organizational populations is limited.
- **Single competitiveness dimension**: Only personal development competitiveness (interpersonal success orientation) was tested. Hypercompetitiveness (winning-focused) was excluded, leaving a meaningful gap in coverage.
- **No behavioral outcome**: Usage intention is not usage behavior. No performance, engagement, or retention data were collected.
- **Work context only**: Findings are framed for workplace gamification; applicability to consumer apps, habit tracking, or health contexts is untested.
- **Cross-sectional**: No long-term effects; novelty effects and decay cannot be assessed.
- **Small interaction effect sizes**: ΔR² for significant interactions ranged from .018–.021 — statistically significant but practically modest.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult (predominantly student; 86% European)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium — leaderboard design findings are generalizable in principle, but PDC moderator effect is specific to a personality trait that requires pre-measurement; not readily actionable for default system design
- **Red flags**: No clinical population. Study is attitudinal only (no behavior). Workplace framing may limit direct transfer to consumer habit apps.

## Conclusions

Personal development competitiveness is a strong positive predictor of favorable perceptions and usage intentions toward competitive gamified systems in a work context. The leaderboard design type (team vs. player) moderates this relationship: high-PDC individuals respond better to team-based leaderboards on enjoyment, reciprocal benefits, and usage intention, but team leaderboards also suppress perceived recognition for all users. The authors argue that "one-system-fits-all" gamification approaches are inadequate and call for individualizing gamified systems based on user trait competitiveness.

## Extraction Notes

- Effect sizes are regression β coefficients only — no Cohen's d or confidence intervals reported. Common for this type of IS/HCI research but limits direct cross-study comparability.
- The key practical finding for NotionRPG is the team vs. player leaderboard tradeoff: team leaderboards help socially-oriented competitive users but reduce individual recognition signals — directly relevant to leaderboard design choices.
- The PDC moderator is interesting but requires a personality assessment to act on — less directly actionable than simple leaderboard design principles from Park et al. (2021).
- Interaction effect sizes (ΔR² ~.02) are small; the main PDC main effect (ΔR² .146–.277) is the dominant finding.
