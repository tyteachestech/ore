# Gamification for Health and Wellbeing: A Systematic Review of the Literature

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Johnson, D., Deterding, S., Kuhn, K., Staneva, A., Stoyanov, S., & Hides, L., 2016
- **Study Type**: Systematic literature review (narrative synthesis; formal meta-analysis not conducted due to heterogeneity)
- **Sample**: k=19 peer-reviewed empirical studies; individual n from 5 to 251 participants; published 2012–2015
- **Search date**: November 19, 2015; 8 databases (Ebscohost, ProQuest, ACM, IEEE, Web of Science, Scopus, Science Direct, PubMed) + manual reference search
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.invent.2016.10.002
- **PMID**: unknown
- **Inter-rater reliability**: ICC = 0.91 (95% CI [0.77, 0.96]) — excellent

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall: positive but far from universal**: 59% of studies (22/37 assessed impacts) reported positive effects; 41% (15/37) mixed or neutral; 0% purely negative. Effect direction varied strongly by outcome type.
  - Effect size: None reported; vote-count synthesis only.
  - Note: The 37 "impacts" count is higher than k=19 because some studies assessed multiple outcome domains.

- **Finding 2 — Behavioural outcomes are the strongest signal**: Physical health behaviour (activity, medication adherence, blood glucose monitoring, nutrition) showed the most consistently positive results. 13 of 19 behaviour assessments were positive; 6 were mixed/neutral.
  - Physical activity specifically: positive in 8 of 10 activity studies. Two exceptions: Maher et al. (short-term gain but no 20-week effect) and Zuckerman & Gal-Oz (no difference between gamified and non-gamified walking app).

- **Finding 3 — Cognitive outcomes are genuinely mixed**: 8 of 17 cognitive assessments positive, 9 of 17 mixed or neutral. This is roughly a coin flip. Cognitive outcomes included motivation, empowerment, quality of life, knowledge, and psychological flexibility.
  - No standardized effect sizes.

- **Finding 4 — Rewards dominated but intrinsic motivation was never tested**: 84% of studies (16/19) used rewards. Yet not a single study measured intrinsic motivation as a direct outcome or mediator. The authors conclude the operative logic of health gamification is positive reinforcement (behaviorist), not the intrinsic motivation that gamification's proponents claim.
  - Game design element breakdown across 46 total instances: Rewards = 16 (35%), Leaderboards = 6 (13%), Avatars = 6 (13%), Levels = 4 (9%), Social interaction = 5 (11%), Progress = 3 (7%), Story/theme = 3 (7%), Challenges = 2 (4%), Feedback = 1 (2%)

- **Finding 5 — User experience effects are unreliable**: Of 12 studies assessing user experience, 5 (42%) positive, 5 (42%) mixed, 2 (16%) negative. Notably, gamification was perceived negatively in mental wellness/mindfulness contexts: 60% of users in Ahtinen et al. expressed skepticism; rewards and achievements felt incompatible with mindfulness.

- **Finding 6 — Expertise moderates response**: Reynolds et al. (n=15 Wii Fit users) found beginners responded positively to gamified features; non-beginners (experienced exercisers) responded negatively, reporting gamification slowed their pace and that praise feedback felt exaggerated.

- **Finding 7 — Social mechanisms appear influential**: Cooperative and hybrid social modes outperformed competitive modes in physical activity. Chen & Pu (2014): cooperation = +21.1% steps, hybrid = +18.2%, competition = +8.8%. Hamari & Koivisto (2015) found social influence, positive recognition, and reciprocity predicted exercise willingness; more friends in system = larger effect.

- **Finding 8 — Novelty effect concern**: Maher et al. (2015) found significant gains at 8 weeks but no significant difference from controls at 20-week follow-up — the only study with >12-week follow-up. Kuramoto et al. (avatar system): motivation increased initially but dropped once all equipment-item awards had been collected.

- **Finding 9 — Evidence quality is low and bimodal**: 42% of papers categorized as weaker evidence, 16% moderate, 42% stronger (on a 5–15 scale, mean = 10.3). More than half (10/19) lacked a gamified vs. non-gamified comparison condition. Sample sizes ranged from 5 to 251.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Dominant operative mechanism — positive reinforcement (behaviorist), not intrinsic motivation**: Despite rhetoric about gamification supporting intrinsic motivation, the actual interventions used reward-based positive reinforcement. No study tested the SDT-based intrinsic motivation chain.

- **Social mechanisms**: Social influence (norms, recognition, reciprocity) positively predicted exercise willingness and behaviour. Cooperative social structures outperformed competitive ones for physical activity increases (Chen & Pu, 2014). More social connections amplified the effect.

- **Mechanism — avatar-mediated progress feedback**: Avatar systems that visually represented effort accumulation (e.g., avatar grew stronger with more standing time) positively affected motivation, but effect collapsed once reward pool was exhausted (Kuramoto et al., 2013 — saturation point).

- **Moderator 1 — User expertise/experience level**: Beginners responded positively to gamified features; experienced users responded negatively. Gamification may function best as an onboarding or habit-initiation tool, not for sustaining advanced practitioners.
  - Evidence: Reynolds et al. (2013) Wii Fit study; explicitly bifurcated result.

- **Moderator 2 — Context compatibility (mindfulness/wellness vs. achievement contexts)**: Gamification received skepticism in mental wellness/mindfulness contexts (60% of Ahtinen et al. users). Reward-based elements perceived as philosophically incompatible with mindfulness values.

- **Moderator 3 — Social structure (cooperative vs. competitive)**: Cooperative/hybrid social structures produced larger activity increases than purely competitive designs.
  - Evidence: Chen & Pu (2014); cooperation +21.1% vs. competition +8.8% steps.

- **Moderator 4 — Time (novelty/saturation)**: Effects shown to attenuate over time. Reward saturation (collecting all items) eliminated motivation gains. Longer interventions showed weaker effects.
  - Evidence: Maher et al. (2015) lost effect at 20 weeks; Kuramoto et al. motivation dropped after reward pool exhausted; Thorsteinsen et al. (2014) effect significant at weeks 5 and 9 but not week 12.

- **Moderator 5 — Pre-existing motivation**: Of 8 studies targeting participants without pre-existing motivation, 7 showed some positive results — suggesting gamification can work even without baseline intrinsic interest in the target behaviour.

## Study Design Details

- **Review type**: Systematic review following PRISMA/MOOSE guidelines; narrative (qualitative) synthesis
- **Search yield**: 365 initial records → 221 after deduplication → 19 final eligible studies
- **Quality assessment tool**: Connolly et al. (2012) 5-criterion tool, scored 1–3 per criterion (range 5–15); mean = 10.3
- **Evidence quality distribution**: 42% weaker (≤8), 16% moderate (8–12), 42% stronger (>12)
- **Study designs included**: RCTs (n=5 with gamified vs. control, but many lacked non-gamified comparison); quasi-experimental; pre-post; single-group; cross-sectional survey
- **Platforms**: Mobile apps (n=7), websites (n=6), social network apps (n=2), analog (n=2), bespoke devices (n=2)
- **Health domains**: Physical activity (n=10), nutrition (n=3), mental health/wellbeing (n=4), substance use (n=2), chronic disease self-management (n=3)
- **Key design flaw**: 10/19 studies did not compare gamified vs. non-gamified versions — making it impossible to attribute effects to gamification specifically

## Limitations

- **No quantitative synthesis**: Outcome heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis; vote-counting used instead — a methodologically weak approach that cannot estimate effect magnitude
- **No non-gamified comparison in majority of studies**: 10/19 studies had no control condition for the gamification element itself; they compared gamified-intervention vs. no-intervention, not gamified vs. non-gamified
- **Multiple elements bundled**: 18/19 studies implemented multiple game elements simultaneously; impossible to isolate individual element effects
- **Small samples**: Range 5–251; most studies severely underpowered
- **Short timeframes**: Most studies ran weeks to 3 months; minimal evidence on sustained effects
- **No intrinsic motivation measurement**: Despite SDT framing in the field, zero studies operationalized intrinsic motivation as an outcome or mediator
- **Self-selection bias unaddressed**: Many studies enrolled volunteers who likely had baseline motivation for the target behaviour
- **Publication bias**: Likely present; positive findings overrepresented (no purely negative health outcomes found in any study)
- **2015 search cutoff**: Field has grown substantially since then; these 19 studies are early-stage
- **Narrow operationalization**: "Gamification" strictly defined; adjacent persuasive tech and behavior change literature excluded

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed across studies — children (Kindergarten–8th grade), adolescents, young adults, working adults (age range 38 mean in one study, 35–73 in another), clinical populations (rheumatoid arthritis, chronic back pain, Type 1 diabetes, trait anxiety)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium-High — physical activity and wellbeing domains are directly applicable; clinical population studies add caution (gamification may work differently in health-mandated vs. voluntary contexts)
- **Red flags**: Several clinical populations (RA patients, chronic back pain, T1D adolescents) require explicit caution before generalizing to consumer app contexts; very small samples in several studies (n=5, n=9, n=13, n=15)

## Conclusions

The review concludes that gamification shows predominantly positive effects on health behaviours (59% of outcomes positive), but effects on cognitive outcomes (motivation, quality of life, wellbeing perceptions) are equivocal (~50/50 positive vs. mixed). The most striking gap: despite gamification's foundational promise of supporting intrinsic motivation, every studied intervention operated via behaviorist reward reinforcement, and not one study measured intrinsic motivation. The authors argue this is both a theoretical gap and a practical concern — the primary mechanism claimed to distinguish gamification from ordinary incentive programs (intrinsic motivation) is untested. Gamification works differently by context (fails in mindfulness), by user type (fails for experts, works for beginners), and may not sustain effects beyond early novelty.

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Voluntary health behaviour change contexts; physical activity promotion; habit initiation with novice/beginner users; children in structured settings (nutrition); adults in social/collaborative frameworks; clinical self-management (with modest effects)
- **Does NOT apply to**: Mindfulness or mental wellness contexts — evidence of negative user experience; experienced users already engaged in the target behaviour — gamification actively interferes; contexts requiring long-term (20+ week) sustained behaviour change without ongoing design refreshes
- **Unknown/untested**: Whether intrinsic motivation is actually the mechanism (no study measured it); independent effects of any single game element (all studies bundled elements); cost-effectiveness vs. simpler interventions; effects in non-health consumer app contexts; differences between mobile delivery modes; gaming affinity as a moderator (not measured in any study)

## Extraction Notes

This is the primary health-domain systematic review in the gamification corpus, authored in part by Sebastian Deterding (who also co-defined gamification in the canonical 2011 paper). Several notes for guidebook use:

1. **The 84% / 0% finding is the headline**: 84% of studies used rewards; 0% measured intrinsic motivation. This single fact exposes the central tension in health gamification — it claims to operate through intrinsic motivation but empirically operates through behaviorist reinforcement. This is directly quotable and design-relevant.

2. **Vote-counting, not meta-analysis**: Like Hamari 2014, this review cannot produce effect sizes. "59% positive" is a directional signal, not a magnitude estimate. For quantitative effects, defer to Mazeas-2022 (physical activity gamification meta-analysis).

3. **The expert/beginner split is directly actionable**: The Reynolds et al. finding — beginners benefit, experienced users are harmed — is a genuine practitioner insight. Gamification as onboarding and habit initiation, not as a tool for experts who already have the habit, is a clean design heuristic.

4. **Cooperative > competitive for social structures**: The Chen & Pu finding (+21.1% cooperative vs. +8.8% competitive) is specific enough to be design-relevant for social features.

5. **Mindfulness context failure**: Ahtinen et al.'s finding that 60% of users rejected gamification in a mindfulness/wellness app is relevant for any context where the emotional tone of the activity conflicts with achievement-signaling reward systems.

6. **Cross-reference with**: Mazeas-2022 (physical activity gamification quantitative meta-analysis), Hamari-2014 (general gamification review same era), Krath-2021 (theoretical basis of gamification — SDT coverage).
