# The Rise of Motivational Information Systems: A Review of Gamification Research

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Koivisto, J. & Hamari, J., 2019
- **Study Type**: Comprehensive systematic literature review (narrative synthesis; formal meta-analysis not conducted due to variable/method heterogeneity)
- **Sample**: N=819 total papers; 273 empirical studies analyzed in detail; 66 controlled experimental quantitative studies analyzed for results
- **Search period**: Through June 2015 (Scopus + AIS Electronic Library)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2018.10.013
- **PMID**: N/A

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Gamification mostly works, but mixed results dominate**: Among 66 controlled experimental quantitative studies, only 28.7% (n=19) reported fully positive results. Nearly half (47.0%, n=31) reported mixed-but-mostly-positive results. Mixed-but-mostly-negative results occurred in 3.0% (n=2). Fully negative results occurred in only 2 studies (3.0%). The authors note probable publication bias suppressing null/negative results.
  - Effect size: None reported; synthesis is vote-counting (result directionality)
  - Note: "Mostly positive" masks a substantial proportion of null or adverse outcomes within the same studies.

- **Finding 2 — Education is the dominant domain, accounting for nearly half of all empirical research**: 129 of 273 empirical papers (46.7%) studied gamification in education/learning contexts. Health/exercise was second (40 papers, 14.5%), followed by crowdsourcing (25 papers, 9.1%). These three domains account for >70% of empirical research.
  - Domain result breakdown for education (out of 28 controlled experimental studies): 35.7% fully positive, 32.1% mixed-mostly-positive, 25.0% null/equal.
  - Domain result breakdown for health/exercise (out of 13 controlled experimental studies): 30.8% fully positive, 53.8% mixed-mostly-positive, 15.4% null/equal.

- **Finding 3 — PBL monoculture: points, badges, and leaderboards dominate to the exclusion of richer mechanics**: Among all 273 empirical studies, the most studied affordances were: points/score/XP (138 studies), challenges/quests/clear goals (91), badges/achievements (85), leaderboards/rankings (82), levels (59), performance stats/feedback (46). Immersion-oriented affordances (narrative: 22 studies; avatar: 29; virtual world: 14) were far less common. Social affordances (networking features: 49; cooperation/teams: 47; competition: 25) ranked second overall.
  - This dominance is not evidence of PBL superiority — it reflects ease of implementation and pattern-based design guides, not efficacy.

- **Finding 4 — Controlled experiments are rare, and isolating individual affordances is rarer still**: Of 227 quantitative empirical studies (165 quantitative + 62 mixed methods), only 66 (29%) were controlled experimental designs. Of those 66, only 11 isolated the effects of a single affordance. Points were tested alone in 4 studies, badges in 4, leaderboards in 4.
  - This means the field cannot currently answer "what does X mechanic do?" with confidence for most mechanics.

- **Finding 5 — Sample sizes improved but remain problematic**: Among the 66 controlled experimental studies, the median sample size was 74.5 and the mean was 1,165 (the high mean indicates a few large outliers skewing the distribution). This is an improvement over Hamari et al. (2014), where n~20 was common. Yet many studies remain underpowered.

- **Finding 6 — Research is theoretically incoherent**: Psychological outcomes studied across 138 papers covered 30+ distinct constructs (enjoyment, perceived usefulness, motivation, engagement, flow, autonomy, competence, relatedness, self-efficacy, social comparison, etc.) with no consistent measurement instruments across studies. This makes cumulative knowledge building impossible and formal meta-analysis infeasible.

- **Finding 7 — Novelty effects are a persistent and acknowledged validity threat**: Multiple studies and the authors explicitly flag that short experimental timeframes inflate positive results. Farzan et al. (2008) IBM Beehive studies show effects attenuating over time. Studies with data collection windows of weeks cannot rule out novelty-driven engagement.

- **Finding 8 — Publication bias toward positive results is likely**: The review notes a "relatively small portion of studies that have either reported or acknowledged negative results." Only 2 of 66 controlled experiments yielded fully negative results — a proportion that is almost certainly suppressed by selective publication.

- **Finding 9 — Gamification research lacks theoretical grounding**: A large proportion of the 819 papers addressed theoretical foundations "only in passing." The review cites Seaborn & Fels (2015) finding that most papers did not address theoretical foundations at all, and observes similar patterns here. 68.5% of full papers were conference proceedings, further limiting theoretical depth.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Core mechanistic model (Hamari et al., 2014 framework, reaffirmed here)**: Gamification affordances → psychological outcomes (enjoyment, competence, autonomy, relatedness, engagement) → behavioral outcomes (activity, performance, participation). All three elements are context-situated.

- **Mechanism 1 — Gamification as feedback system**: Authors propose gamification operates through three feedback types: (1) cognitive — quantified progress data; (2) affective — emotional arousal such as enjoyment and excitement; (3) social — social comparison and community signaling. The specific feedback pathway linking affordances to psychological outcomes remains empirically uncharted.

- **Mechanism 2 — SMART goals operationalization**: Gamification makes goals Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Realistic, and Time-bound (SMART). The structuring function of game elements provides scaffolding for complex long-term behaviors like learning and health maintenance. This explains the concentration of research in domains where activities require long-term perseverance.

- **Moderator 1 — Domain/context type**: Results vary substantially by domain. Social networking contexts appear more receptive than eCommerce/utilitarian contexts. Education consistently shows the strongest results. Utilitarian/transactional contexts (eCommerce, financial tools) are more resistant.

- **Moderator 2 — Voluntariness of use**: The authors note that compelled participation may reduce gamification effectiveness, consistent with SDT predictions that external pressure undermines intrinsic motivation.

- **Moderator 3 — Individual user characteristics**: Demographic factors (age, gender), personality traits, player types, and goal orientations all affect gamification response. Users differ in achievement vs. social vs. immersion motivation orientations. One-size-fits-all PBL implementations ignore this.

- **Moderator 4 — Time/novelty**: Short experimental timeframes inflate results. Novelty has been empirically documented (Farzan et al., 2008; Koivisto & Hamari, 2014). The field lacks adequate longitudinal data.

- **Moderator 5 — Gamification as structural constraint**: Bringing activities to the "ludus" (structured play) end of the ludus-paidia continuum may limit creative action. In organizational/management contexts, over-structured gamification can obstruct the instrumental goals of the system.

- **Moderator 6 — Competitive affordances creating unintended competition**: Leaderboards and points afford social comparison even when competition was not the design intent. Competitive environments may discourage certain user types, producing adverse effects that studies designed to measure positive outcomes may not detect.

## Study Design Details

- **Review type**: Comprehensive systematic review following Webster & Watson (2002) and Paré et al. (2015) guidelines
- **Search**: Scopus (TITLE-ABS-KEY: gamif*) + AIS Electronic Library; June 2015 cutoff
- **Raw hits**: 807 (Scopus) + 35 (AISeL) = 842; after deduplication, false hits, non-English exclusion: 819 papers
- **Full paper analysis**: 514 full papers categorized; 273 with empirical data analyzed in depth
- **Experimental subset**: 66 controlled experimental quantitative studies analyzed for result directionality
- **Coding scheme**: Domain, data method, affordances, psychological outcomes, behavioral outcomes, results (5-category scale: Positive / Mixed-mostly-positive / Null / Mixed-mostly-negative / Negative)
- **Analysis method**: Vote-counting (result directionality). No pooled effect sizes possible.
- **Formal meta-analysis**: Not conducted. Explicitly stated as impossible due to heterogeneity in affordances, outcomes, and measurement instruments.

## Limitations

**Stated by authors:**
- Overview-level analysis means individual study specifics are lost in the abstraction
- Search limited to Scopus and AISeL; some publications in other databases may be missed
- Affordance categorizations made without detailed cross-study comparison due to review scale
- The coding of results into 5 categories requires judgment calls that may not always be perfectly consistent

**Obvious unstated limitations:**
- **June 2015 search cutoff**: The field grew explosively after 2015. This review captures the early phase of gamification research but misses the maturing literature including RCTs, meta-analyses, and negative replication studies published 2016–present
- **Vote-counting is methodologically weak**: Cannot determine effect magnitude, cannot distinguish trivial from substantive effects, biased by publication tendencies
- **No quality assessment**: All 273 empirical studies are treated equivalently regardless of methodological rigor; a study with n=6 and no control group counts equally with a large-scale RCT
- **Publication bias is acknowledged but unquantified**: The claim that only 3% of controlled experiments found negative results is almost certainly an undercount; the true rate is unknown
- **Hamari is a co-author**: Both Koivisto and Hamari have extensive work in the gamification literature being reviewed. Some self-citation effect is plausible, though no specific bias is evident in the review structure
- **Conceptual conflation**: The review includes papers using "gamification," "game-based learning," and "serious games" without strict differentiation in the education domain, potentially mixing distinct phenomena

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Highly mixed — ranges from K-12 students to university students, office workers, crowdsourcers, health patients, consumers, exercise app users
- **Relevance to casual behavior change (consumer apps)**: Medium — the review maps the landscape but individual study findings are domain-specific and cannot be directly applied cross-context
- **Red flags**: Many included studies use institutional/educational populations (students in specific courses) or organizational populations (IBM Beehive enterprise users), both of which involve participation incentives and contexts not present in voluntary consumer app use
- **Domains absent or thin**: Marketing/commerce (2 controlled studies), work (1), transportation (1). Consumer app behavior change is not a primary focus of the reviewed literature

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that gamification research has grown rapidly but without theoretical coherence or methodological consistency. While positive results are the modal finding, mixed results dominate, and the persistence of PBL as the near-universal implementation reveals more about the ease of deployment than about efficacy. The authors explicitly frame their 15 future research agenda points as correctives to a field that has advanced mainly without agenda, theoretical guidance, or a clear picture of what works for whom.

The review's central message for practitioners: gamification is not a silver bullet. The abundant mixed results, weak experimental designs, publication bias, and novelty effects mean that the field's apparent optimism is likely inflated relative to the true average effect. Context, user characteristics, and affordance choice matter substantially — but the evidence base to make those choices systematically does not yet exist.

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: All domains where gamification has been studied — especially education/learning, health/exercise, crowdsourcing, and social networking contexts. Long-term, complex behaviors that benefit from structure, goal clarity, and feedback.

- **Does NOT apply to (or applies only with major caution)**:
  - Strictly transactional/utilitarian services (eCommerce, financial tools): identified as a resistant context
  - Short-term outcomes: novelty effects mean short-study positive results may not persist
  - Universal user populations: individual differences in player type, personality, and goal orientation mean the same affordance can produce opposite effects in different users

- **Unknown/untested (as of June 2015 search cutoff)**:
  - Long-term effects of any specific gamification design beyond novelty period
  - Effects of individual affordances in isolation (only 11 of 66 controlled experiments tested single affordances)
  - Most domains beyond education, health, and crowdsourcing (all had k≤3 controlled experiments)
  - Consumer mobile app contexts specifically (underrepresented in the literature reviewed)
  - Effects of immersive affordances (narrative, avatars, virtual worlds) — rarely studied, even less often in controlled experiments
  - Cooperative and collective gamification approaches (dominated by individualistic PBL designs)

## Extraction Notes

This is the largest-scope gamification literature review published to date (N=819 papers), authored by Koivisto and Hamari — two of the field's most central researchers (Hamari co-authored the 2014 foundational review and is extensively cited throughout the gamification canon).

**What this paper is and is not:**

This is a **field map**, not an evidence base for specific design recommendations. Its value is structural: it shows where research has concentrated (education, PBL, individualistic affordances), where it hasn't (cooperation, immersion, most consumer domains), what methods have dominated (quantitative descriptive, prototype-based), and what the overall result landscape looks like (positive but mixed and likely inflated by publication bias and novelty).

**The PBL monoculture finding is the most practically important observation:**

Points, badges, and leaderboards account for the top four affordance slots in both overall empirical research and controlled experimental studies. The authors explicitly flag this as a problem — not because PBL is ineffective but because the field has studied this narrow slice so heavily that research cannot generalize to the broader range of gameful affordances (narrative, cooperation, adaptive challenge, immersion). Practitioners have been designing from a truncated evidence base.

**The 47% mixed-results figure deserves emphasis:**

The most common outcome is *mixed-mostly-positive* — not fully positive. This means in the typical gamification study, some outcomes improve while others don't, or the effect exists for some users but not others. The "gamification works" narrative glosses over this heterogeneity. The guidebook should represent this accurately.

**Relationship to other extractions in this project:**

- **Hamari et al. (2014)** is the predecessor: 24 studies, same vote-counting method, same conclusion but smaller scope. Koivisto & Hamari (2019) directly extends and confirms those findings at 33x the scale.
- **Krath et al. (2021)** provides the theoretical complement: maps which psychological theories actually appear in gamification research and finds the same theoretical fragmentation this review describes methodologically.
- **Sailer et al. (2020)** and **Mazeas et al. (2022)** provide the quantitative complement: formal meta-analyses with effect sizes for specific domains (learning and physical activity, respectively) that this review could not compute.

**The 2015 search cutoff is a significant caveat:**

The field has generated substantial additional research since June 2015. The pattern of findings likely holds (PBL dominance, mixed results, novelty concerns) but specific numbers would shift. Use this paper for structural/landscape framing rather than current prevalence statistics.
