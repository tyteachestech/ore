# The Gamification of Learning: a Meta-analysis

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Sailer, M. & Homner, L., 2020
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=19/16/9 studies (cognitive/motivational/behavioral); N=1686/2246/951 respectively; total unique participants ~4883 (overlap possible across subsets)
- **Publication range**: Primary studies 2013–2017
- **Literature search**: March 2017 across ACM, ERIC, IEEE, JSTOR, PubMed, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Google Scholar; 5548 screened → 38 included (40 experiments)
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Cognitive outcomes**: Gamification produces a significant small positive effect on cognitive learning (conceptual + procedural knowledge). Effect is stable under high-rigor subsplit.
  - Effect size: g=0.49 (95% CI [0.30, 0.69]), k=19, N=1686, SE=0.10, p<.01
  - Heterogeneity: Q(18)=57.97, p<.01, I²=72.21%
  - Fail-safe N=469 (robust)
  - High-rigor subsplit: g=0.42 (95% CI [0.14, 0.68]), k=9, N=686 — still significant

- **Finding 2 — Motivational outcomes**: Gamification produces a significant small positive effect on motivation (engagement, self-efficacy, attitudes). Effect disappears in high-rigor subsplit.
  - Effect size: g=0.36 (95% CI [0.18, 0.54]), k=16, N=2246, SE=0.09, p<.01
  - Heterogeneity: Q(15)=73.54, p<.01, I²=75.13%
  - Fail-safe N=316 (robust)
  - High-rigor subsplit: g=0.22 (95% CI [−0.11, 0.56]), k=7, N=1063 — NOT significant

- **Finding 3 — Behavioral outcomes**: Gamification produces a significant small positive effect on behavioral/skill outcomes. Effect disappears in high-rigor subsplit.
  - Effect size: g=0.25 (95% CI [0.04, 0.46]), k=9, N=951, SE=0.11, p<.05
  - Heterogeneity: Q(9)=22.10, p<.01, I²=63.80%
  - Fail-safe N=136 (robust)
  - High-rigor subsplit: g=0.27 (95% CI [−0.16, 0.70]), k=5, N=667 — NOT significant

- **Finding 4 — Social interaction as moderator (behavioral)**: Competitive-collaborative interaction significantly outperforms no interaction, and high-rigor subsplit confirms it outperforms pure competition.
  - Competitive-collaborative vs. no interaction: Q(2)=12.80, p<.01 for behavioral outcomes
  - Competitive-collaborative: g=0.70 (95% CI [0.41, 0.99]), k=3
  - Competitive only: g=0.12 (95% CI [−0.14, 0.38]), k=6
  - No interaction: g=−0.06 (95% CI [−0.36, 0.24]), k=3
  - High-rigor subsplit (motivational): competitive-collaborative g=0.61 (95% CI [0.36, 0.85]) vs. competitive g=−0.09 (ns)

- **Finding 5 — Game fiction as moderator (behavioral only)**: Including a fictional game world/narrative significantly increases behavioral outcome effects; no significant effect for cognitive or motivational.
  - Q(1)=5.45, p<.05 for behavioral; no residual variance (p=.08)
  - With game fiction: g=0.41 (95% CI [0.31, 0.51]), k=3
  - Without game fiction: g=0.10 (95% CI [−0.14, 0.34]), k=7
  - Fail-safe for this comparison: robust
  - For cognitive: Q(1)=0.04, p=.85 (ns); for motivational: Q(1)=0.13, p=.72 (ns)

- **Finding 6 — Research context (cognitive only)**: School settings show significantly larger gamification effects on cognition than higher education or informal settings.
  - Q(2)=12.48, p<.01; school > higher education (p<.01) and school > informal (p<.05)
  - School: g=1.12 (95% CI [0.68, 1.57]), k=3 (but note confound: school studies more likely use passive control)
  - Higher education: g=0.41 (95% CI [0.25, 0.57]), k=13, N=1181

- **Finding 7 — Randomization (methodological quality moderator)**: For motivational outcomes, quasi-experimental studies show significantly larger effects than experimental studies.
  - Q(1)=4.67, p<.05 for motivational outcomes
  - Experimental: g=0.13, ns; Quasi-experimental: g=0.51 (95% CI [0.35, 0.68])
  - Suggests quasi-experimental designs inflate motivational effects

- **Finding 8 — Duration (motivational outcomes)**: Interventions lasting half a year or less (but >1 month) show significantly larger motivational effects than 1-day interventions.
  - Q(1)=4.93, p<.05
  - Half a year or less: g=0.59 (95% CI [0.39, 0.79]); 1 day or less: g=0.19 (ns)
  - No significant duration effect for cognitive or behavioral outcomes

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — SDT framework**: Gamification hypothesized to satisfy needs for competence, autonomy, and social relatedness, thereby increasing intrinsic motivation and engagement with learning material. Feedback as central mechanism (Hattie & Timperley 2007 cited as foundation).

- **Moderator 1 — Social interaction type**: Competitive-collaborative interaction is the most effective social mode, particularly for behavioral and motivational outcomes. Pure competition may undermine relatedness needs (SDT); combining it with collaboration preserves need for relatedness while maintaining engagement pressure.
  - Evidence: competitive-collaborative g=0.70 vs. competitive g=0.12 for behavioral outcomes

- **Moderator 2 — Game fiction (narrative)**: Adding a fictional game world or story meaningfully improves behavioral outcomes but not cognitive or motivational. Likely mechanism: fiction increases in-task effort and engagement during the intervention itself (behavioral outcomes are typically process-measured, not post-hoc).
  - Caveat: behavioral outcomes in this dataset were almost exclusively measured DURING interventions, not after. Effect may reflect increased task engagement rather than retained learning.

- **Moderator 3 — Research context**: School-age students show larger cognitive gains from gamification than higher education students or informal learners. Partly confounded by control group differences (school studies more often use passive controls).

- **Moderator 4 — Duration (motivational only)**: Longer interventions (up to ~6 months) show larger motivational effects than very short (1-day) interventions. Suggests motivational benefits accumulate over time, but no delayed posttest data exists to assess endurance.

- **Moderator 5 — Randomization (methodological quality)**: Quasi-experimental designs show inflated motivational effects (g=0.51) compared to experimental designs (g=0.13, ns). Gamification motivational effects may partly reflect selection bias in non-randomized studies.

- **Goal-related note**: Leaderboards function as goal-setting mechanisms. Landers et al. (2017) cited within paper: leaderboards with difficult goals are more effective than leaderboards with easy goals — echoes goal-setting theory's specificity/difficulty principle.

- **Achievement design note**: Effectivity of achievements (badges/points) strongly depends on quantity and difficulty of achievements (Groening & Binnewies 2019, cited). Meta-analysis could not isolate this; mean design effect assumed.

- **Not significant moderators**: Learning arrangement of comparison group (passive vs. active vs. mixed instruction), instrument type, study design (pretest vs. posttest only) — none significantly moderated outcomes when testable.

## Study Design Details

- **Comparison**: Gamification conditions vs. non-gamified instruction (passive, active, mixed, or untreated control)
- **Excluded**: Studies comparing gamification vs. full games; within-subject designs; studies without control group
- **Outlier removal**: 3 studies with extraordinarily large cognitive effects excluded (Wasson et al. 2013; Grivokostopoulou et al. 2016; Sanmugam et al. 2016) from cognitive subsample after forest plot inspection. Note: the full dataset g for cognitive including outliers was ~0.75 (RE Model in forest plot)
- **Effect size metric**: Hedges' g (corrected for small-sample bias); pretest scores subtracted when available
- **Statistical model**: Random effects model (main analysis); mixed effects (moderator analyses via meta-regression)
- **Interrater reliability**: κ=.76 to 1.00 depending on moderator

## Limitations

- **Small k, especially behavioral outcomes (k=9/10)**: Low power; non-significant subsplit results may reflect underpowering, not absent effects. Authors caution against over-interpreting null subsplit results.
- **High heterogeneity throughout**: I²=63–75% across all three outcome types; substantial unexplained variance remains after moderator analyses. Many conceptual moderators could not be fully tested due to small subgroup sizes.
- **Behavioral outcomes measured during (not after) interventions**: Process data during gamification context may not transfer to behavior outside the gamified context. Limits interpretation of behavioral effects as skill acquisition.
- **Game element specificity absent**: Search terms were "gamification" / "gamif*" only, not specific elements. Meta-analysis captures average design effect; cannot identify which specific elements (badges, leaderboards, points, narratives, progress bars) drive effects.
- **Publication date range narrow (2013–2017)**: Field was nascent; primary study quality concerns noted throughout. Only 1 study had delayed posttest.
- **Confounding in research context moderator**: School vs. higher education difference may reflect control group differences (passive vs. mixed instruction), not true population effect.
- **Game fiction moderator confounded with randomization**: Studies including game fiction were less likely to apply random assignment (r=−.41). Game fiction effects on behavioral outcomes may partly reflect lower methodological rigor in fiction-including studies.
- **No individual-level game element data**: Cannot say which game elements (goals, progress bars, points, badges, leaderboards, avatars) produce the effects; all are aggregated.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — primarily higher education students (majority), some school-age students, some work-related/informal learning settings
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium. This is a learning-context meta-analysis; outcomes are cognitive learning, motivation, and behavioral task performance — directly applicable to gamified productivity/habit tools where "learning" and "skill building" overlap with behavior change
- **Red flags**: Learning-specific framing. Some studies involve motor skills / task performance in controlled settings. Not a direct behavior-change-in-real-life meta-analysis.
- **Cross-domain note**: Goal-setting is embedded: leaderboard design specifically cited as functioning via goal-setting mechanisms (Landers et al. 2017).

## Conclusions

"Gamification in the context of learning has received increased attention and interest over the last decade for its hypothesized benefits on motivation and learning... The present meta-analysis supports the claim that gamification of learning works because we found significant, positive effects of gamification on cognitive, motivational, and behavioral learning outcomes. Whereas the positive effect of gamification on cognitive learning outcomes can be interpreted as stable, results on motivational and behavioral learning outcomes have been shown to be less stable." The question of which factors contribute to successful gamification "remains partly unresolved, at least for cognitive learning outcomes."

Key practical implication: "Creating a gamification environment that allows learners to engage in both competitive and collaborative interaction can be beneficial: Letting learners work together in teams, while competing with other teams, can help to improve learners' quality of performance and skills."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Theory of Gamified Learning (Landers 2014)**
  - Components: Instructional content, behaviors/attitudes, game characteristics, learning outcomes
  - Research alignment: Game elements modify behaviors/attitudes → mediate or moderate path from instruction to learning outcomes; gamification indirect effect on outcomes

- **Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci 2002)**
  - Components: Competence, autonomy, social relatedness
  - Research alignment: Collaboration → relatedness; competitive-collaborative > pure competition supports relatedness need; game fiction → autonomy (shared goals, meaningful context)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Gamified learning interventions in school, higher education, and work-related training settings; effects on knowledge acquisition (cognitive), engagement/motivation (motivational), and task performance during interventions (behavioral)
- **Does NOT apply to**: Behavior change in daily life outside structured learning contexts; specific game element effects (can't isolate badges vs. points vs. progress bars vs. goals); long-term retention (only 1 study had delayed posttest)
- **Unknown/untested**: Individual differences (player type, gaming familiarity, personality); specific game element design parameters (difficulty of achievements, leaderboard framing as goals); effects after gamification ends (no maintenance data); whether behavioral gains measured during interventions transfer to non-gamified contexts

## Extraction Notes

Strong meta-analysis with appropriate methodological controls (random effects, publication bias testing, subsplit analyses by methodological rigor). The most important finding for NotionRPG is the instability of motivational and behavioral effects once study quality is controlled — the headline "gamification works" relies on a mix of quasi-experimental and experimental studies, and under experimental conditions alone, motivational and behavioral effects are non-significant. Cognitive effects remain stable.

The social interaction finding (competitive-collaborative > pure competition) is robust across both the main analysis and high-rigor subsplit for both motivational and behavioral outcomes — this is among the paper's most actionable conclusions.

The leaderboard-as-goal-setting note (Landers et al. 2017 cited within paper) is directly relevant to cross-domain synthesis with goal-setting literature. Game elements that set performance standards function as proximal goals and should follow goal-setting theory principles (difficulty, specificity).

Note: this paper cannot isolate effects of specific game elements (badges, points, levels, progress bars, leaderboards separately) because the literature search used "gamification" as the inclusion term, not specific elements. The Deterding-2011 definition was applied, and most studies used multiple game elements together.
