# The Effects of Gamification on Computerized Cognitive Training: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Vermeir, J. et al., 2020
- **Study Type**: Systematic Review + Meta-Analysis (2 parts: narrative review + RCT-only meta-analysis)
- **Sample (meta-analysis)**: k=9 RCTs; N=530 for cognitive outcomes; N=514 for motivation/engagement outcomes
- **Sample (systematic review)**: k=49 independent studies; N=4,003 participants total
- **Search period**: 2008–2018, searched February 2018 across 11 databases + grey literature
- **Preregistered**: PROSPERO CRD42018082309
- **DOI**: 10.2196/18644
- **PMID**: 32845239

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Motivation/Engagement**: Gamification produces a moderate, significant positive effect on motivation and engagement in cognitive training tasks.
  - Effect size: Hedges g=0.72 (95% CI [0.26, 1.19]), k=8, N=514, p=.002
  - Heterogeneity: Q(7)=39.73, p<.001 (high; moderator analyses warranted)
  - Publication bias: Egger t(6)=0.37, p=.72 — no evidence of bias
  - Sensitivity: Removing any single study did not significantly alter the result

- **Finding 2 — Cognitive Process Outcomes**: Gamification does NOT produce a significant effect on cognitive performance (attention, working memory, inhibition, etc.).
  - Effect size: Hedges g=0.27 (95% CI [−0.08, 0.62]), k=9, N=530, p=.14
  - Heterogeneity: Q(8)=29.91, p<.001 (high)
  - Publication bias: Egger t(7)=0.15, p=.88 — no evidence of bias
  - Sensitivity: Stable across leave-one-out analyses
  - Interpretation: Gamification boosts how people feel about the task, but not what they gain from it cognitively

- **Finding 3 — Clinical Outcomes**: Gamification produces no significant effect on downstream clinical outcomes (e.g., alcohol use, psychiatric symptoms).
  - Effect size: Hedges g=0.07 (95% CI [−0.19, 0.32]), k=4, N=276, p=.61
  - Heterogeneity: Q(3)=3.32, p=.35 (low)
  - Publication bias: Egger t(2)=5.59, p=.03 — EVIDENCE OF BIAS detected
  - Trim-and-fill correction: g=0.08 (95% CI [−0.36, 0.20]) after trimming 2 studies — effect still null

- **Finding 4 — Demand/Difficulty**: Gamification makes tasks feel significantly more demanding and difficult.
  - Effect size: Hedges g=−0.52 (95% CI [−0.89, −0.14]), k=3, N=136, p=.007
  - Heterogeneity: Q(2)=2.03, p=.36 (low)
  - Note: Sensitivity analysis showed result was unstable when removing individual studies (likely due to only 3 studies), so interpret with caution

- **Finding 5 — Flow/Immersion**: No significant effect of gamification on flow/immersion.
  - Effect size: Hedges g=0.10 (95% CI [−0.36, 0.55]), k=2, N=79, p=.68
  - Too few studies for bias assessment or sensitivity analysis

- **Finding 6 — No Moderators Found**: Age group, population risk type (low vs. high risk), number of game elements, and number of training sessions did NOT significantly moderate any outcomes.
  - All Q(b) statistics non-significant for both motivation/engagement and cognitive process outcomes
  - Practical limit: Too few studies per subgroup to test cognitive domain type or specific game element type as moderators

- **Finding 7 — Field quality is poor**: Only 9 of 49 reviewed studies (18%) were RCTs eligible for meta-analysis. Most studies lacked control groups (82%), adequate blinding (78–80%), and preregistration.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Primary meta-analysis outcomes (k=9 RCTs):**
- **Motivation/Engagement**: g=0.72 (95% CI [0.26, 1.19]), k=8, N=514, p=.002
- **Cognitive Process**: g=0.27 (95% CI [−0.08, 0.62]), k=9, N=530, p=.14 (NS)
- **Clinical Outcomes**: g=0.07 (95% CI [−0.19, 0.32]), k=4, N=276, p=.61 (NS)
- **Demand/Difficulty**: g=−0.52 (95% CI [−0.89, −0.14]), k=3, N=136, p=.007
- **Flow/Immersion**: g=0.10 (95% CI [−0.36, 0.55]), k=2, N=79, p=.68 (NS)
- **Feasibility** (single study only): g=−0.36 (95% CI [−0.97, 0.26]), N=49, p=.25 (NS)

**Trim-and-fill correction (clinical outcomes):**
- Raw: g=0.07; Corrected (2 studies trimmed): g=0.08 — effect remains null

**Heterogeneity:**
- Motivation/Engagement: Q(7)=39.73, p<.001 (high, unexplained)
- Cognitive Process: Q(8)=29.91, p<.001 (high, unexplained)
- Clinical: Q(3)=3.32, p=.35 (low)
- Demand/Difficulty: Q(2)=2.03, p=.36 (low)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — Motivation via game elements**: Gamification hypothesized to increase motivation/engagement through rewards (points, badges), feedback loops, and immersion features (themes, avatars, sound). Achievement/progression-oriented elements dominate real-world implementations (rewards in 82% of studies; feedback loops in 80%).

- **Mechanism — Distraction risk**: The authors note that game elements may add cognitive complexity to tasks — the significant demand/difficulty finding (g=−0.52) suggests gamification creates a competing attentional load, which may explain the absent cognitive transfer.

- **SDT framing**: Reward-based elements (dominant in field) are primarily extrinsic motivators — researchers cited concerns that these do not support intrinsic motivation or long-term engagement. Immersion-oriented features (themes, story) are argued to better support autonomy and intrinsic motivation per SDT.

- **Moderator — Age Group**: Not significant. Children/adolescents vs. adults showed similar motivation/engagement and cognitive effects.
  - Motivation/engagement: Q(b,1)=0.94, p=.33
  - Cognitive process: Q(b,1)=0.16, p=.69

- **Moderator — Population Risk (low vs. high)**: Not significant. Low-risk (students, healthy adults) vs. high-risk (elderly, clinical) groups showed no significant difference.
  - Motivation/engagement: Q(b,1)=0.14, p=.71
  - Cognitive process: Q(b,1)=0.89, p=.35

- **Moderator — Number of Game Elements**: Not significant (meta-regression).
  - Motivation/engagement slope: β=0.26 (95% CI [−0.57, 0.06]), p=.09
  - Cognitive process slope: β=0.21 (95% CI [−0.46, 0.04]), p=.08

- **Moderator — Number of Training Sessions**: Not significant (meta-regression).
  - Motivation/engagement slope: β=0.0002 (95% CI [−0.12, 0.12]), p=.99
  - Cognitive process slope: β=0.01 (95% CI [−0.07, 0.09]), p=.78
  - Caveat: Limited variability in number of sessions across studies constrains this analysis

- **Moderator — Cognitive Domain**: Not testable — insufficient studies per subgroup (< 3 per domain)

- **Moderator — Game Element Type**: Not testable — insufficient studies per subgroup. Studies combined element categories; pure social-only or pure achievement-only conditions did not exist.

- **Underused element — Social features**: Competition used in only 3/49 studies (6%), social interaction in 2/49 (4%). Cannot draw conclusions about their moderating effect.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Computerized cognitive training tasks (attention, working memory, inhibition, executive function, arithmetic, processing speed) with game elements overlaid
- **Control/Comparison**: Non-gamified or less-gamified version of the same cognitive training task (within same RCT)
- **Game elements present across review (mode=5 per study; range 2–9)**: Rewards (82%), feedback loops (80%), story/theme (78%), difficulty adjustment (69%), game levels (53%), challenge (49%), sound effects (39%), avatar (33%), competition (6%), social interaction (4%)
- **Delivery modalities**: Computer/laptop (53%), tablet (27%), smartphone (4%)
- **Setting**: Research site (lab, hospital, school; 39%), home (20%), web-based (10%)
- **Age range across review**: 8.98 to 82.70 years; 78% adult studies
- **Follow-up**: Only 1 of 9 meta-analysis studies reported any follow-up assessment
- **Blinding**: Inadequate or unclear in most studies — performance and detection bias rated HIGH in 7 of 9 RCTs
- **Attrition data**: Insufficient reporting across studies to assess attrition bias in most RCTs

## Limitations

- **Only 9 RCTs eligible for meta-analysis**: 82% of reviewed studies lacked a non-gamified control condition. Meta-analytic conclusions rest on a very narrow evidence base.
- **Small samples**: Nearly half of the 49 reviewed studies had N<50. Meta-analysis participant counts (N=136–530 per outcome) limit precision.
- **High unexplained heterogeneity**: I² was high for motivation and cognitive outcomes despite moderator testing. Unknown sources of variance remain.
- **No long-term follow-up**: Only 1 of 9 RCTs included any follow-up. All effects are immediate post-training only. Durability of motivation gains or cognitive effects is unknown.
- **Blinding failures pervasive**: Participants and personnel were unblinded in most studies (7/9 RCTs rated HIGH risk for performance bias). Demand effects likely inflate motivation/engagement ratings.
- **Cannot isolate individual game elements**: All studies used element bundles (mode=5 elements). Impossible to attribute effects to specific features (badges, points, leaderboards, feedback loops individually).
- **Self-report motivation measures unvalidated**: Most studies used self-developed instruments — psychometric quality is unknown, comparability across studies is limited.
- **Social features severely underrepresented**: Social/competitive elements nearly absent from the literature — a major design space remains untested.
- **Publication date cap at 2018**: Field has grown substantially since; results may underrepresent recent, higher-quality designs.
- **Unstated limitation — Demand/difficulty interaction**: If gamification increases perceived difficulty (g=−0.52) without improving cognitive outcomes (g=0.27, ns), the attention-splitting hypothesis is plausible but untested in this dataset.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — 78% adult samples, 22% children/adolescents. 67% high-risk clinical/at-risk populations (elderly, ADHD, anxiety, addiction). 31% low-risk (university students, schoolchildren).
- **Relevance to consumer app gamification**: Medium. Cognitive training is a distinct context from general app engagement, but the finding that gamification boosts engagement without improving underlying performance is directly relevant to product design — it signals that gamified wrapping can mask flat real-world outcomes.
- **Red flags**: Majority of studies use clinical or at-risk populations — findings may not generalize cleanly to healthy consumer contexts. High-risk population effects on motivation (g=0.82) trend larger than low-risk (g=0.63) though non-significant difference.
- **Boundary note**: The "computerized cognitive training" context is narrow (structured tasks measuring reaction time, error rates, memory span). This is not the same as general app use or habit formation.

## Conclusions

The meta-analysis provides the first quantitative synthesis of gamification applied specifically to computerized cognitive training. The primary conclusion is a clean engagement-performance dissociation: gamification significantly improves motivation/engagement (g=0.72) but produces no significant effect on cognitive performance (g=0.27, ns) or clinical outcomes (g=0.07, ns). The authors characterize gamification as effective for getting people to show up and feel engaged, but not for improving what those tasks are designed to build. They attribute the null cognitive effect either to distraction (added game layer competes for attention) or to poor design (lack of theoretical grounding). No moderating variables were identified, which the authors largely attribute to insufficient studies rather than a genuine absence of moderation.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci)**
  - Components: Competence, autonomy, social relatedness
  - Research alignment: Reward/achievement elements (dominant in field) support competence but not autonomy or relatedness; immersion/theme elements (story, avatar) support autonomy; social elements (underused) support relatedness. SDT predicts reward-dominant designs will produce extrinsic not intrinsic motivation — consistent with engagement gains without performance transfer.

## Quotable Passages

> "Overall, results from the meta-analysis of RCTs showed that gamified training tasks were more motivating/engaging and more demanding/difficult than non- or less-gamified tasks. No other significant effects were found, and no moderator variables were identified." (Discussion — Principal Findings)

> "Interestingly, we found no significant effect of gamification on cognitive outcomes (N=9 studies; Hedges g=0.27; P=.14). Thus, overall, gamification does not seem to have enhancing or deleterious effects on cognitive performance... Some critical views on gamification interpret such findings as an illustration that it does not work, whereas others argue that these mixed results are due to confounders such as poor design or failure to apply gamification in an appropriately considered or meaningful way." (Discussion — Effectiveness section)

> "With regard to theories of motivation, only around one-third of studies (n=17/49, 35%) in our systematic review mentioned using theoretical foundations to guide the development of their interventions... ## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Gamification of computerized, structured cognitive training tasks (working memory, attention, inhibition). Engagement/motivation effect likely generalizes to other structured digital tasks. Age-agnostic: no significant age moderation found for motivation or cognition.
- **Does NOT apply to**: Free-play consumer apps where the "task" is the game itself. Open-ended app behavior (browsing, social media). Long-term adherence (no follow-up data beyond immediate post-training). Clinical outcome improvement — no evidence gamification improves what cognitive training is meant to treat.
- **Unknown/untested**: Individual game element effects in isolation; social/competitive element effects (too rare in the literature); effects beyond 1–3 months; mobile/smartphone delivery vs. desktop; whether any specific combination of elements drives the motivation effect or all combinations equally.
- **Caution — inflation from unblinded designs**: All 9 RCTs had HIGH risk of performance bias (unblinded participants). Self-reported motivation/engagement outcomes (g=0.72) are especially susceptible to demand effects. Objective cognitive outcomes (g=0.27, ns) are more credible precisely because they are harder to fake.

## Extraction Notes

This is a methodologically sound preregistered meta-analysis (PROSPERO), but its conclusions are fundamentally constrained by a very thin evidence base: only 9 RCTs from 49 reviewed studies. The headline findings are clean and highly usable for the guidebook:

**The engagement-performance dissociation** (g=0.72 for motivation vs. g=0.27 ns for cognition) is the core finding and should be a central reference point for any chapter on gamification's limits. Gamification reliably makes people feel more engaged but does not reliably improve what they're doing.

**The demand/difficulty finding** (g=−0.52) is underappreciated. Gamified tasks feel harder. This is consistent with the attention-splitting hypothesis — adding game layer competes with task performance. For product designers: gamification is not free. It consumes some cognitive resources.

**The social elements gap** is noteworthy in context: only 3–4 of 49 studies used competition or social interaction features, making it impossible to test their moderating effect here. Cross-reference with Sailer-2020 (competitive-collaborative > pure competition) for evidence on social element design — that evidence comes from learning contexts, not cognitive training specifically.

**Inflation flag for motivation effect**: Unblinded RCTs with self-report motivation measures are high-risk for demand effects. The g=0.72 should be treated as an upper-bound estimate. The null cognitive findings (objective measures) are more credible.

**Scope flag set to include-with-caution** because the population is predominantly clinical/at-risk (67% high-risk groups), which may limit direct generalizability to consumer app contexts. That said, the null performance transfer finding is likely directionally correct and conservative.
