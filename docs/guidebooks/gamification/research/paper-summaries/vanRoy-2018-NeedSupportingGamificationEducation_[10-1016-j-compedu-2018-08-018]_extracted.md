# Need-Supporting Gamification in Education: An Assessment of Motivational Effects Over Time

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: van Roy, R. & Zaman, B., 2018
- **Study Type**: Longitudinal (4-wave repeated measures, growth curve modeling)
- **Sample**: n=40 master's students (21F/19M; mean age 24.15 years, SD=3.70), single university course, Belgium
- **Follow-up**: 15 weeks (4 measurement points: week 1 / week 5 / week 10 / week 15)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.compedu.2018.08.018
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Autonomous motivation: U-shaped curve, not linear increase.** The primary hypothesis (H1: need-supporting gamification would increase autonomous motivation over time) was only partially supported. Autonomous motivation initially decreased (b = -1.24, t(91.31) = -3.63, p < .001), then recovered in the second half of the semester (quadratic term b = .20, t(91.41) = 2.97, p < .01). The best-fitting model was quadratic with random intercepts.

- **Finding 2 — Single-item motivation declined continuously.** A simple "how motivated are you?" item showed a significant linear decline across the full semester (b = -0.32, t(32.37) = -3.75, p < .001). This contradicts the AMS subscale data, illustrating that a single-item measure misses motivational nuance. Hypothesis H1 would be fully rejected using only this measure.

- **Finding 3 — Intrinsic motivation: same U-shaped pattern.** Quadratic growth model fit best. Initial linear decrease (b = -1.10, t(88.59) = -3.14, p < .01) followed by quadratic recovery (b = .19, t(88.65) = 2.71, p < .01). High individual variation in starting levels (Var(u0j) = 1.01, χ²(1) = 19.85, p < .001).

- **Finding 4 — Identified and introjected regulations: parallel U-shaped curves.** Both showed the same curvilinear pattern. Identified: linear b = -1.41 (p < .001), quadratic b = .23 (p < .01). Introjected: linear b = -1.11 (p < .01), quadratic b = .22 (p < .01). The recovery of introjected motivation (a controlled type) was unexpected; authors speculate that SDT-designed elements may have unintentionally thwarted autonomy needs (e.g., group competition creating peer dependency).

- **Finding 5 — External regulations: no change.** External regulations showed significant individual variation at baseline (Var(u0j) = 1.53, χ²(1) = 81.18, p < .001) but no significant change over time. Need-supporting game elements did not increase the most externalized form of controlled motivation — a key positive result, consistent with avoiding overjustification.

- **Finding 6 — Controlled motivation overall also showed U-shaped curve.** Initial decrease (b = -0.78, t(89.62) = -2.57, p < .05) followed by quadratic recovery (b = .14, t(89.65), p < .05). Authors did not predict this; explains the introjected regulations finding above.

- **Finding 7 — Amotivation: inverted U-shape.** Amotivation rose initially (b = 1.63, t(124.29) = 14.21, p < .001) then decreased (quadratic b = -0.26, t(94.08) = -8.22, p < .001). Significant individual variation in slopes (Var(u1j) = .10, χ²(1) = 32.93, p < .001).

- **Finding 8 — Individual differences are pervasive.** Significant random effects for both intercepts and slopes across multiple motivation types. Students differed both in starting levels and in how their motivation evolved — supporting H2 (gamification effects are person-specific).

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Need-supporting design as internalisation mechanism**: The authors designed three game elements explicitly to satisfy SDT's basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) rather than afford controlled motivation. The theory holds that need satisfaction enables internalisation of extrinsic regulations into autonomous motivation.
  - Challenges (12 weekly) → competence support via progressive difficulty; voluntary completion → autonomy support
  - Non-announced badges (71 total) → competence feedback without triggering anticipatory reward ascription (avoids overjustification)
  - Group competition via podium leaderboard → relatedness + competence feedback; however, may have created peer dependency, unintentionally thwarting autonomy

- **Familiarisation effect (hypothesised, not tested)**: Authors speculate the initial motivational decrease reflects time needed for students to adapt to game elements before effects become visible. No empirical test.

- **Socialisation-to-control interpretation (alternative)**: University students may interpret any task-like addition as controlled (grade-linked), regardless of design intent. This would explain why introjected regulations also rose in the second half despite SDT-aligned design.

- **End-of-programme motivation confound**: The gamified course occurred in the final semester of a master's programme. Students may have elevated effort for graduation reasons unrelated to gamification, producing the late-semester upturn.

- **Individual differences as moderator**: Significant random effects indicate that the population-level U-shape masks highly variable individual trajectories. Who benefits from gamification and why remains untested.

- **No control group**: Cannot disentangle gamification effects from natural semester motivational dynamics. Known from prior literature that motivation declines during semesters; the U-shape may reflect a natural late-semester effort surge partially countered or unaffected by gamification.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: 3 SDT-designed game elements added to Google+ Communities used for a 15-week international master's course:
  1. 12 weekly challenges (voluntary, progressive difficulty)
  2. 71 non-announced badges (competence feedback without anticipated rewards)
  3. Group competition / podium leaderboard (4 teams based on assignment topic)
- **Game elements manually administered** by teaching staff 2–3 times/day (no automated system), allowing quality control but introducing delayed feedback
- **Measurement**: Academic Motivation Scale (AMS; Vallerand et al., 1992), adapted for course context; 28 items, 7-point Likert; measured at t0 (week 1, pre-gamification), t1 (week 5), t2 (week 10), t3 (week 15); plus single-item motivation item
- **Analysis**: Linear Mixed Models (IBM SPSS v23); AIC-selected best-fitting growth curves; Maximum Likelihood estimation for missing data (35 out of 160 data points estimated)
- **No control group**: Single-arm design; changes attributed partly to gamification on the basis of timing (game elements introduced after t0)
- **Students unaware of gamification study**: Told surveys evaluated "new educational methods" — reduces demand characteristics
- **Attrition**: t0=35, t1=35, t2=30, t3=25 (37.5% missing data by final wave); 20 students completed all 4 surveys, 5 completed only 1

## Limitations

- **No control group**: Cannot isolate gamification effects from natural semester dynamics, end-of-programme effects, or course-specific factors. Causal claims are very weak.
- **N=40, single course, single institution**: Extremely small sample for growth curve modeling. Results may not generalise beyond this tech-savvy master's cohort.
- **Missing data handled by ML estimation**: 28% of data points (35/160) are imputed, not observed. Missing data correlated with lower motivation, creating upward bias risk.
- **No automatic feedback**: Game elements manually administered mean feedback was not immediate, potentially underestimating effects of fully automated gamified systems.
- **AMS measures course motivation, not platform motivation**: Captures why students take the class, not why they engage with the gamified tool — potentially underestimating platform-specific motivation.
- **15 weeks framed as longitudinal but is one semester**: Authors acknowledge this is short relative to a full educational career. Long-run stabilisation or decay unknown.
- **Cannot rule out end-of-programme effort surge**: Late-semester upturn may reflect graduation motivation, not gamification response.
- **Platform usability confound**: Google+ Communities was unfamiliar to most students; usability friction may have suppressed motivational responses to game elements.
- **No moderator analyses conducted**: Individual differences documented but not explained — which student characteristics predict positive vs. negative trajectories is unknown.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University master's students (final year, international programme, tech-savvy relative to peers); mean age 24 years
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Low-Medium. This is a compulsory educational context with grade accountability, not voluntary self-initiated behavior change. Motivational dynamics differ substantially from app-based or daily habit contexts.
- **Red flags**: Captive student population; obligatory course participation; gamification platform use was voluntary but course was not. Cannot generalise to adults pursuing self-directed change.
- **Transferable insight**: The U-shaped trajectory finding and the distinction between motivation types (especially: external regulations stable; introjected rises; autonomous eventually recovers) has design relevance for any sustained gamified system.

## Conclusions

Authors conclude that (1) motivation is heterogeneous — different types respond differently to the same gamification design; (2) need-supporting game elements showed evidence of countering the typical semester-long motivational decline, but only in the second half, not immediately; (3) effects are strongly individual, calling for personalised gamification approaches. "Our results illustrated the importance of combining our three research perspectives when designing and evaluating gamified systems... they might form (part of) an explanation for the previously found mixed results regarding gamification's effectiveness in education."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Self-Determination Theory (SDT)** (Deci & Ryan, 2004)
  - Components: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness (basic psychological needs); Motivation continuum: Intrinsic → Identified → Introjected → External → Amotivation
  - Research alignment:
    - Voluntary challenges → Competence + Autonomy need support
    - Non-announced badges → Competence feedback without anticipated reward
    - Group leaderboard → Relatedness + Competence; unintended autonomy thwarting (peer dependency)
    - Internalisation process → Hypothesised pathway from extrinsic to autonomous motivation

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: University students in a structured semester-long course; voluntary platform engagement within a compulsory course; context where multiple SDT-aligned game elements are layered together; longer engagement windows (10–15 weeks) rather than cross-sectional or short-term studies
- **Does NOT apply to**: Voluntary adult behaviour change apps; populations outside higher education; short-term gamification interventions (<8 weeks); single-element gamification (study used 3 simultaneous elements, cannot isolate which drove effects); competitive or announced-badge designs
- **Unknown/untested**: Which individual characteristics predict positive vs. negative trajectories; whether effects persist beyond the 15-week window; whether findings replicate with a control group; contribution of each individual game element; role of feedback immediacy (all feedback was manually delayed)

## Extraction Notes

This is a methodologically notable paper primarily for two reasons:

1. **Design rarity**: It is the only study in the gamification literature that explicitly designed game elements using SDT theory (need-supporting) AND tracked motivation longitudinally at multiple time points AND used validated multi-subscale motivation measures — not just "how motivated are you?" This makes it highly relevant for understanding the temporal dynamics of gamification despite the small N.

2. **U-shape finding is the key signal**: The initial motivational decrease followed by late recovery has significant design implications. It suggests gamification systems need time to demonstrate benefit and should not be evaluated or abandoned early. The "familiarisation effect" hypothesis is plausible but untested.

**Caution flags for guidebook use:**
- N=40 is very small for growth curve modeling; standard errors are large and results should be treated as preliminary
- No control group means the U-shape may simply reflect natural semester motivational dynamics (initial enthusiasm drop, end-of-year effort surge)
- The distinction between external regulations (stable) and introjected (recovered) is theoretically important but may reflect measurement noise at this sample size
- The student population is captive and tech-savvy — motivational dynamics may not transfer to voluntary adult self-improvement contexts

**Practical synthesis value**: Despite limitations, this paper provides the best available longitudinal evidence that SDT-aligned gamification design (non-announced rewards, voluntary challenges, competence-based feedback) does not trigger overjustification effects and may produce late-stage autonomous motivation recovery. The warning about competitive leaderboards potentially thwarting autonomy via peer dependency is a useful design caveat.
