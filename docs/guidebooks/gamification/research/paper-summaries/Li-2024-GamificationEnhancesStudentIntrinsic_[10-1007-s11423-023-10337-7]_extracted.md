# Gamification Enhances Student Intrinsic Motivation, Perceptions of Autonomy and Relatedness, but Minimal Impact on Competency: a Meta-analysis and Systematic Review

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Li, L., Hew, K.F., & Du, J., 2024
- **Journal**: Education Technology Research and Development, 72, 765–796
- **Study Type**: Combined meta-analysis (quantitative, k=35 interventions) + systematic review (qualitative, 31 studies)
- **Sample**: k=35 independent interventions, N=2500 participants total; meta-analysis drawn from 24 articles
- **Search range**: January 2011 – October 2022; 10 databases searched; 3125 initial hits → 31 articles selected
- **Population**: K-12 and higher education students; 21 studies at higher education level (undergrad n=20, grad n=1), 10 at K-12
- **Disciplines covered**: Physical education, algorithms, mathematics, and others
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s11423-023-10337-7

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Intrinsic motivation (overall)**: Gamification produces a significant but small positive effect on students' intrinsic motivation compared to non-gamified learning.
  - Effect size: g=0.257 (95% CI [0.043, 0.471]), k=35, N=2500, p=.019
  - Heterogeneity: Q=206.403, I²=83.527%, p<.001 — substantial unexplained variance

- **Finding 2 — Perceived autonomy**: Gamification significantly enhances perceived autonomy; the effect is roughly 2.5x larger than the intrinsic motivation headline figure.
  - Effect size: g=0.638 (95% CI [0.139, 1.136]), k=11, p=.012
  - Note: wide CI signals high heterogeneity across these 11 studies

- **Finding 3 — Perceived relatedness**: Gamification significantly enhances perceived relatedness; effect is large but based on only 4 interventions.
  - Effect size: g=1.776 (95% CI [0.737, 2.814]), k=4, p=.001
  - Caution: extremely wide CI; k=4 is insufficient for stable estimation

- **Finding 4 — Perceived competence**: Gamification has a marginally significant, minimal effect on competence — the most-measured SDT need, and the weakest outcome.
  - Effect size: g=0.277 (95% CI [0.001, 0.553]), k=12, p=.049
  - CI barely excludes zero; effectively non-significant in practical terms

- **Finding 5 — Instructor equivalence as confound**: Studies where both groups were taught by the same instructor showed dramatically larger effects than studies with different instructors.
  - Same instructor: g=0.613 (k=9)
  - Different instructors: g=−0.143 (k=8)
  - No data reported: g=0.264 (k=18)
  - QB=12.596, df=2, p=.002 — significant moderator

- **Finding 6 — Duration and novelty decay**: Interventions of 1–3 months had the highest mean effect (g=0.610); interventions lasting over one semester had near-zero or negative effects (g≈−0.068). The authors interpret this as the novelty of gamification wearing off over time.

- **Finding 7 — Reward contingency**: Performance-contingent rewards outperform participation-contingent rewards. The best-performing combination was rewards tied to solving each unit, surpassing an absolute score, and exceeding a relative norm (g=0.633). QB=99.486, p=.000.

- **Finding 8 — Publication bias**: No evidence detected. Funnel plot shows symmetry. Kendall's tau τ=−0.027, p=.820; Egger's regression α=−0.150, p=.887. Fail-safe N=281 null studies required to nullify the effect.

- **Finding 9 — Implementation challenges (systematic review)**: Two failure modes dominate qualitative reports across 31 studies:
  1. Competence undermined (7/31 studies, 23%): public absolute leaderboards creating discomfort and embarrassment for low-ranked students
  2. Autonomy undermined (6/31 studies, 19%): learners perceived no meaningful choice in learning content or activities

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — SDT cascade**: The hypothesized causal path is: game elements → fulfill basic psychological needs (competence, autonomy, relatedness) → increase intrinsic motivation. Empirical results partially confirm this: autonomy and relatedness effects are larger than the intrinsic motivation headline, suggesting needs satisfaction may indeed mediate outcomes. Competence is the exception.

- **Mechanism — Reward informativeness**: Rewards tied to graded, attainable performance criteria enhance perceived competence and thus intrinsic motivation. Rewards for mere participation or task completion do not reliably enhance competence perceptions. This aligns with Cameron et al. (2001) meta-analytic findings on reward contingencies.

- **Moderator 1 — Instructor equivalence**: The most practically important confound identified. When the same instructor teaches both gamified and control conditions, effects are large (g=0.613). When different instructors are used, effects disappear or reverse (g=−0.143). This suggests many positive gamification effects in the literature may actually be instructor effects, not gamification effects.

- **Moderator 2 — Duration (novelty decay)**: Short-to-medium interventions (1–3 months) produce the largest effects; semester-long interventions produce near-zero or negative effects. Authors attribute this to novelty wearing off over time — a significant threat to ecological validity for deployed products.

- **Moderator 3 — Reward contingency**: Performance-linked reward combinations outperform participation-based rewards. The SDT explanation: performance-contingent rewards signal competence, while task-completion rewards signal minimal expectation.

- **Non-moderator — Number of game elements**: Using more game elements does not systematically increase effects (QB=0.975, ns). More is not better — what matters is design quality and need-alignment.

- **Leaderboard harm pathway**: Absolute (infinite) leaderboards publicly display all participants' ranks. For low performers, this generates social pressure, embarrassment, and a sense of incompetence — directly undermining the competence need that leaderboards are meant to satisfy. 7 of 31 qualitative studies flagged this as the primary failure mode.

- **Autonomy failure pathway**: Most gamified implementations provide little learner agency — predetermined activities, identical assignments for all, no path choices. Students perceive this as externally controlling rather than autonomy-supporting. 6 of 31 studies flagged lack of content/activity choice as a primary challenge.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention type**: Gamification (game elements applied to non-game learning contexts) vs. non-gamified instruction
- **Comparison**: Gamified classroom vs. standard (non-gamified) classroom; interventions required both a treatment and a control group reporting intrinsic motivation outcomes
- **Measurement**: 29 of 31 studies used self-report questionnaires (Intrinsic Motivation Inventory interest/enjoyment subscale and similar); 5 used interviews; none used behavioral free-choice measures
- **Effect size metric**: Hedges' g with random effects model (Comprehensive Meta-Analysis v4)
- **Publication types included**: Peer-reviewed journals, conference papers, and dissertations
- **Inter-coder reliability**: 98% for data extraction
- **Exclusions**: Studies without a non-gamified comparison group; studies measuring learning outcomes only (not intrinsic motivation); game-based learning/serious games (not gamification)

## Limitations

**Stated limitations:**
- Missing data widespread: many studies did not report sufficient statistical information; coded as "not available" rather than excluded
- Self-report measurement dominates (29/31 studies): self-report intrinsic motivation is prone to self-favoring bias, exaggeration, and demand characteristics. No study used behavioral free-choice measures, which are considered more objective
- Explicit reporting bias: coding was limited to what was explicitly stated in each article; actual study conditions may differ from what was documented

**Unstated but important limitations:**
- **Instructor equivalence confound is severe**: With 18/35 interventions providing no instructor data and different-instructor studies showing g=−0.143, a substantial portion of positive effects may be instructor effects. The field has not corrected for this systematically
- **k=4 for relatedness**: The large relatedness effect (g=1.776) is based on only 4 interventions with a CI spanning [0.737, 2.814]. This is a highly unstable estimate and should not be taken as indicative of a "large" true effect
- **Novelty confound not directly measured**: Duration is used as a proxy for novelty decay, but no study measured novelty directly. The mechanism is inferred, not demonstrated
- **Education-only population**: All studies are K-12 or higher education settings. Generalizability to consumer apps, workplace tools, or self-directed adult learners is assumed but untested
- **No delayed follow-up**: No study in the corpus measured intrinsic motivation after the gamification was removed. Maintenance effects are completely unknown
- **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity**: I²=83.5% for the overall effect; identified moderators explain only a fraction of the variance. Major unknown drivers remain

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Students (K-12 and higher education); 21 higher education, 10 K-12 — majority is young adults in structured academic settings
- **Relevance to consumer app gamification**: Medium. The SDT framework and specific failure modes (leaderboard harm, autonomy suppression) transfer directly to consumer product design. The effect magnitudes should not be assumed to transfer — classroom gamification differs substantially from voluntary consumer app use
- **Red flags**: No adult general-population samples. No voluntary/self-initiated contexts (all participation is course-embedded). All measurement is self-report. Instructor equivalence confound severely inflates published effects
- **Key application insight**: The autonomy finding (g=0.638) is the most actionable for product designers — it is robust, theoretically grounded, and directly maps to design choices (learner path choice, avatar selection, content options)

## Conclusions

The meta-analysis demonstrates that gamification produces a small but significant positive effect on intrinsic motivation (g=0.257). Crucially, the effects on SDT need fulfillment are differentiated: autonomy (g=0.638) and relatedness (g=1.776) show larger effects than competence (g=0.277) — despite competence being the most commonly targeted need through badges and leaderboards. The systematic review reveals why: absolute leaderboards consistently harm low performers' competence perceptions, and most implementations provide too little learner choice to satisfy autonomy needs. The authors conclude: "the fulfillment of all three needs has an additive impact on intrinsic motivation... if individuals perceive that the fulfillment of one of their basic psychological needs is diminished or hindered, a loss of motivation is likely to occur."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci)**
  - Components: Competence, Autonomy, Relatedness
  - Research alignment:
    - Competence → marginally supported (g=0.277, CI barely excludes zero); leaderboards often undermine rather than support this need
    - Autonomy → well-supported (g=0.638); choice and path flexibility are the key design levers
    - Relatedness → large effect but extremely thin evidence base (k=4); group competition/collaboration appears to drive this

## Quotable Passages

> "The gamification features adopted in most gamification studies did not cater to all basic psychological needs. This may be the reason for the small effect size found in our meta-analysis. Since the relationships between the three basic psychological needs are complementary, the fulfillment of all three needs has an additive impact on intrinsic motivation. Conversely, if individuals perceive that the fulfillment of one of their basic psychological needs is diminished or hindered, a loss of motivation is likely to occur." (Discussion — Effect of gamification on intrinsic motivation)

> "Absolute leaderboards (also known as infinite leaderboards) display the positions of all players and are often used in educational settings. On such a leaderboard, each participant can view the position of every other participant, and those at the top of the leaderboard may have a greater sense of achievement than those at the bottom. This can contribute to social pressure and frustration for students who are ranked low." (RQ4 — Challenges section)

> "Interventions that were 1 to 3 months long had the largest mean effect size (g=0.610), whereas those that were more than 1 semester long had an almost negligible and negative mean effect size. One possible reason for this finding is that gamification is a non-traditional teaching method and the interest shown by the participants in the short-term interventions may be due to the fact that the approach is a new and exciting game-like learning approach. Over time, however, this novelty wears off and students become less engaged or even negative." (Discussion — Effect of gamification on intrinsic motivation)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Gamification in structured educational settings (K-12 and higher education); short-to-medium intervention windows (1–3 months); contexts where instructors control both gamified and non-gamified conditions; performance-contingent reward designs
- **Does NOT apply to**: Voluntary/consumer contexts; adult self-directed learners; interventions beyond one semester; contexts where learner agency is structurally absent (the effect reverses); gamification with different instructors in treatment vs. control (effect disappears or reverses)
- **Unknown/untested**: Long-term intrinsic motivation after gamification is removed; effects in non-educational consumer contexts; individual differences in player type or gaming familiarity; which specific game elements drive which SDT needs; free-choice behavioral measures of intrinsic motivation (no study used these)

## Extraction Notes

This is the most directly SDT-grounded gamification meta-analysis in the corpus, and its key contribution is disaggregating gamification's effects by individual psychological need — something prior meta-analyses (Sailer & Homner 2020; Ritzhaupt 2021) did not do cleanly.

**The autonomy finding is the most actionable:** g=0.638 for perceived autonomy vs. g=0.257 for intrinsic motivation overall. This gap directly implies that autonomy-supportive design (choice of learning paths, content options, avatar customization, self-directed pacing) is the highest-leverage lever for gamification designers who want to drive genuine intrinsic motivation rather than surface compliance.

**The instructor equivalence confound is underappreciated in the broader field:** g=0.613 (same instructor) vs. g=−0.143 (different instructors) is a massive range — the difference is likely partly a teacher enthusiasm/Hawthorne effect. Many positive gamification studies in the literature share this confound. Chapter writers should note this when reporting gamification effect sizes from education research.

**The novelty decay signal is clear:** effects peak at 1–3 months and collapse over semesters. This is directly relevant for consumer products — the honeymoon period is real, and sustaining motivation requires mechanisms beyond novelty (SDT need fulfillment being the candidate).

**Relatedness (g=1.776) should not be cited as a reliable large effect** — k=4 with a CI spanning 2.077 points is not stable. File as directionally positive pending more evidence.

**Scope note:** All studies are educational settings; apply the consumer-app insights qualitatively (via the SDT framework), not quantitatively (the effect magnitudes don't transfer).
