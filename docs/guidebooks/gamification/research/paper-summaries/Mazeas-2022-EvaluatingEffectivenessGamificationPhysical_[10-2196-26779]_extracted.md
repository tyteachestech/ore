# Evaluating the Effectiveness of Gamification on Physical Activity: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Mazeas, Duclos, Pereira, & Chalabaev, 2022
- **Study Type**: Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs
- **Sample**: k=16 RCTs in meta-analysis (18 in qualitative review), N=2407, ages 9-73 years (mean 35.7, SD 17.2)
- **Search period**: 2010-2020 (5 databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane)
- **Preregistered**: Yes (PROSPERO CRD42020186882; OSF)
- **DOI**: 10.2196/26779
- **PMID**: Not extracted from file

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Primary PA effect**: After sensitivity analyses (removing 2 high-influence/bias studies), gamified interventions produced a statistically significant small-to-medium effect on physical activity outcomes (MVPA, steps, active minutes, walking time).
  - Effect size: Hedges g=0.42 (95% CI 0.14-0.69), k=14, n=2197; I²=74%
  - Pre-post change score version: g=0.38 (95% CI 0.07-0.69; I²=79%)

- **Finding 2 — Comparison type matters**: The effect is larger when gamification is compared against inactive controls than against active nongamified controls.
  - vs. inactive control (waitlist): g=0.58 (95% CI 0.08-1.07; I²=81%)
  - vs. active nongamified control (e.g., plain app or activity tracker): g=0.23 (95% CI 0.05-0.41; I²=37%)

- **Finding 3 — Long-term/follow-up effect**: PA gains persist at follow-up (12-24 weeks after intervention end, mean 14.4 weeks), but the effect is markedly smaller.
  - Effect size: g=0.15 (95% CI 0.07-0.23; I²=0%), k=5, n=1306 (sensitivity analysis)
  - Without sensitivity analysis: g=0.09 (95% CI -0.07 to 0.26; I²=21%) — non-significant

- **Finding 4 — Step count**: Gamification significantly increases daily steps.
  - SMD: g=0.49 (95% CI 0.05-0.93; I²=75%), k=9, n=1438
  - Mean difference: +1609.56 steps/day (95% CI 372.39-2846.73; I²=86%), k=7, n=1235

- **Finding 5 — MVPA not improved**: No significant effect on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity specifically.
  - Effect size: g=0.31 (95% CI -0.19 to 0.80; I²=82%), k=4, n=739 — non-significant
  - Explained by game mechanics: most interventions tracked steps, not intensity

- **Finding 6 — Objective vs. self-report**: Effects found across both measurement types.
  - Objective measures: g=0.45 (95% CI 0.08-0.82; I²=80%)
  - Self-reported: g=0.24 (95% CI 0.08-0.39; I²=0%)

- **Finding 7 — Short interventions only**: Statistically significant effect for interventions <12 weeks (g=0.44, 95% CI 0.19-0.69; I²=16%). Not significant for ≥12 weeks (g=0.41, 95% CI -0.19 to 1.01).

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Why steps but not MVPA**: Game mechanics in included studies primarily rewarded step count, not intensity. Only 2/18 studies targeted MVPA directly. Walking increases but intensity does not.
- **Social incentives**: 83% of studies included social features (teams, messaging, leaderboards). Sample too small to formally test this as moderator.
- **Theoretical grounding**: Most interventions grounded in SDT (n=6), sociocognitive models (n=5), or behavioral economics (n=3). Formal moderation by theory not testable due to small k.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Digital gamified interventions targeting PA/SB (smartphone apps, web-based, wearable with game functions). Duration ranged 1-24 weeks (mean 11.8 weeks). Most common game elements: badges/rewards (72%), teams/leagues (72%), levels (50%), points/scores (39%), leaderboards (39%).
- **Control/Comparison**: Inactive (waitlist/no treatment) or active (nongamified PA app, activity tracker alone).
- **Follow-up**: 12-30 weeks post-intervention (mean 14.4 weeks), assessed in 6/16 trials.
- **Blinding**: All studies rated high risk for participant/personnel blinding (impossible for this type of intervention). 3 studies high risk for outcome assessment (self-report only).
- **Attrition**: 5 studies at high risk for incomplete outcome data; did not report ITT analyses.
- **Statistical method**: Random-effects model; Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method (more conservative than DerSimonian-Laird); Hedges g corrects for small-sample bias.
- **Outliers excluded in sensitivity**: Corepal et al. (cluster RCT, no clustering correction) and Nishiwaki et al. (crossover, no washout).

## Limitations

- **Small k**: Only 16 studies in meta-analysis; precluded fine-grained moderator testing (e.g., specific game elements, social incentives, theoretical model used).
- **Substantial heterogeneity**: I²=74-86% in primary analyses. Sources not fully explained by tested subgroups. Likely driven by personality, motivational factors not measured.
- **Publication bias uncertainty**: Trim-and-fill added 3 missing studies; corrected effect g=0.24 (95% CI -0.24 to 0.73) is no longer statistically significant. Egger test was non-significant but trim-and-fill signals caution.
- **Cannot isolate gamification**: Cannot confirm effects are caused by gamification per se vs. other intervention components (app engagement, BCTs, social features).
- **Underpowered studies**: 7/16 studies had <45% statistical power based on sunset funnel plot; 4 had <18% power.
- **Active control quality unclear**: Nongamified comparators varied widely in quality and intensity.
- **Conflict of interest flag**: Lead author (Mazeas) is affiliated with Kiplin, a commercial gamification company, and the Cifre PhD grant is co-funded by Kiplin. Authors state no sponsor involvement in analysis/interpretation, but this is a relevant CoI.
- **Excluded: SB outcomes**: Only 3 studies assessed sedentary behavior — no meta-analysis possible.
- **Diverse outcome metrics**: Mixed objective and subjective measures. Gamified step-count apps inflate step outcomes.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed (adults 67%, adolescents 33%; healthy 78%, chronic disease 22% [obesity, T2D, CVD])
- **Age range**: 9-73 years
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium-High — PA promotion in daily-life contexts using apps/wearables is directly relevant; clinical subpopulation (22%) is manageable.
- **Red flags**: None that would trigger auto-exclude. Chronic disease subsamples are modest, not the primary focus.
- **Caution flag**: Exercise/physical activity domain — applies the `digital-delivery` inflation flag. Effect likely inflated vs. in-person delivery benchmarks.

## Conclusions

"This meta-analysis confirms that gamified interventions are promising for promoting PA in various populations." The primary effect (g=0.42) is significant but heterogeneous and sensitive to outlier removal and publication bias correction. The key practical finding is that gamification outperforms equivalent nongamified digital interventions (g=0.23), not just waitlist controls. Long-term effects persist but decay substantially (g=0.15 at 14-week follow-up). Effects generalize across age, gender, BMI, and health status. Gamification works better for walking/steps than MVPA — design aligns with mechanism.

## Quotable Passages

> "The main meta-analysis performed on 16 studies and 2407 participants revealed a small to medium summary effect of gamified interventions on PA behavior (Hedges g=0.42, 95% CI 0.14-0.69). No statistical difference among different subgroups (adults vs adolescents and healthy participants vs adults with chronic diseases) and no interaction effects with moderators such as age, gender, or BMI were found, suggesting good generalizability of gamified interventions to different user populations." > "The long-term effect (measured with follow-up averaging 14 weeks after the end of the intervention) was weaker, with a very small to small effect (Hedges g=0.15, 95% CI 0.07-0.23). These results indicate that the effect of gamification persists after the end of the program, suggesting that it is not just a novelty effect due to the playful nature of gamification. However, this long-term effect was weaker and decreased with time after the end of the intervention." (Discussion, Long-term Effect section)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Adults and adolescents (ages 9-73) engaging with digital gamified PA interventions (apps, web platforms, wearable+game hybrids) in daily-life contexts; generalizes across healthy populations and those with chronic diseases (obesity, T2D, CVD).
- **Applies more strongly to**: Step-count/walking outcomes rather than MVPA. Short programs (<12 weeks) rather than longer ones. Comparisons against inactive controls rather than active nongamified alternatives.
- **Does NOT reliably apply to**: MVPA outcomes (effect non-significant). Interventions >12 weeks (effect non-significant without sensitivity adjustments). Sedentary behavior reduction (insufficient data, k=3 only).
- **Unknown/untested**: Individual game element effects (badges vs. leaderboards vs. teams vs. points — k too small). Role of social features specifically. Specific theoretical models. Long-term (>6 months) effectiveness. Cost-effectiveness. Non-English or non-Western populations (most studies: Europe, US, Australia, Canada).
- **Caution**: Trim-and-fill correction renders primary effect non-significant (g=0.24, CI crosses zero). Excluding high-risk-of-bias studies also renders it non-significant. Results are sensitive to outlier inclusion. Lead author has commercial CoI (Kiplin gamification company).

## Extraction Notes

This is the first meta-analysis of RCTs specifically on gamification for physical activity. Key tensions to flag for guidebook use:

1. **The sensitivity/robustness problem**: The "headline" effect (g=0.42) depends on the sensitivity analysis removing two studies. The raw analysis (g=0.43, I²=86%) is less clean, and the publication-bias-corrected estimate (g=0.24) is non-significant. Writers should present the g=0.42 figure but note these robustness concerns explicitly.

2. **The step vs. intensity distinction** is practically important: gamification reliably increases steps (+1600/day), not MVPA. For a productivity/habit app focused on walking or daily movement goals, this is directly applicable. For fitness-intensity goals, evidence is weaker.

3. **The active-control effect (g=0.23)** is the most defensible benchmark for "does gamification add value beyond a plain app" — this is the relevant comparison for NotionRPG design decisions.

4. **Long-term persistence** (g=0.15 at ~14 weeks post-intervention) is real but small. Suggests gamification is not purely a novelty effect but does decay — relevant for "burst" gamification design rather than always-on approaches.

5. **Digital delivery inflation flag is warranted**: Paper is comparing digital gamified vs. digital nongamified, so the digital-delivery inflation may be partially baked into the comparison. The g=0.23 vs. active digital controls may be more inflation-resistant than the g=0.58 vs. waitlist.
