# Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000)
- **Study Type**: Theoretical review / integrative overview (not an original empirical study)
- **Sample**: No single sample -- summarizes a program of research across many studies and domains
- **Journal**: American Psychologist, Vol. 55, No. 1, pp. 68-78
- **DOI**: 10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68

**Note:** This is the canonical SDT overview paper. It defines the core constructs and synthesizes evidence across CET (Cognitive Evaluation Theory) and OIT (Organismic Integration Theory). No original effect sizes are reported -- it references and summarizes findings from dozens of other studies. Chapter writers should cite this paper for definitions and framework; cite underlying empirical papers (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999; Ryan & Connell, 1989; etc.) for specific effect magnitudes.

## Key Findings

### 1. The Three Basic Psychological Needs (Core SDT Constructs)

Three innate psychological needs are posited as universal and essential:

- **Competence**: The need to feel effective -- to experience mastery, efficacy, and capability in interacting with the environment
- **Autonomy**: The feeling of volition -- that one's actions emanate from the self rather than from external pressure. *Not* synonymous with independence or individualism; autonomous behavior can be interdependent or collectivist
- **Relatedness**: The need to feel belongingness and connectedness with others; to feel cared for and part of a social group

These are defined as basic needs in the physiological sense: when satisfied they conduce toward health and well-being; when thwarted, they contribute to pathology and ill-being.

### 2. The Self-Determination Continuum (Motivational Types)

SDT identifies six motivational positions along a continuum from non-self-determined to self-determined:

| Position | Type | Regulatory Style | Perceived Locus | Process |
|----------|------|-----------------|-----------------|---------|
| 1 | Amotivation | None | Impersonal | Nonintentional, lack of control |
| 2 | External regulation | External | External | Compliance, rewards/punishments |
| 3 | Introjected regulation | Introjection | Somewhat external | Self-control, ego-involvement, internal rewards/punishments |
| 4 | Identified regulation | Identification | Somewhat internal | Conscious valuing, personal importance |
| 5 | Integrated regulation | Integration | Internal | Congruence, synthesis with self |
| 6 | Intrinsic motivation | Intrinsic | Internal | Interest, enjoyment, inherent satisfaction |

Key distinction: **Identified** and **integrated** regulation are considered extrinsic (done for separable outcomes) but are highly autonomous -- nearly functionally equivalent to intrinsic motivation in quality of engagement.

### 3. Conditions That Support vs. Undermine Intrinsic Motivation (CET)

**Support intrinsic motivation:**
- Optimal challenges (task difficulty calibrated to skill)
- Effectance-promoting (positive, informational) feedback
- Choice and opportunities for self-direction
- Acknowledgment of feelings
- Autonomy-supportive social contexts (teachers, coaches, managers who are not controlling)
- A secure relational base (distal sense of relatedness)

**Undermine intrinsic motivation:**
- Expected tangible rewards contingent on task performance (all such rewards, per meta-analysis)
- Threats, deadlines, directives
- Pressured evaluations
- Imposed goals
- Controlling interpersonal style

Critical mechanism: These undermining factors shift the **perceived locus of causality** from internal to external, reducing the sense that behavior emanates from the self.

**Important caveat:** CET applies only to activities that hold intrinsic interest to begin with (novelty, challenge, or aesthetic value). For activities that are not intrinsically interesting, the CET principles do not apply -- internalization (OIT) must be used instead.

### 4. Competence Alone Is Not Sufficient; Autonomy Must Also Be Present

"Feelings of competence will not enhance intrinsic motivation unless accompanied by a sense of autonomy or, in attributional terms, by an internal perceived locus of causality." (Fisher, 1978; Ryan, 1982, as cited here)

Implication: A game or app can give users positive feedback that boosts perceived competence, but if users feel controlled by external metrics, that competence boost will not translate into intrinsic motivation.

### 5. Conditions That Facilitate Internalization of Extrinsic Motivation (OIT)

For behaviors that are not intrinsically interesting, the path to sustained autonomous motivation is internalization. All three needs play a role:

- **Relatedness**: Primary driver of initial internalization. People take in behaviors modeled or valued by others to whom they feel (or want to feel) connected. "Relatedness, the need to feel belongingness and connectedness with others, is centrally important for internalization."
- **Competence**: Supports internalization when people feel efficacious. Behaviors directed before developmental readiness yield only partial internalization (remaining external or introjected at best).
- **Autonomy**: Critical for integration specifically. Contexts yield integrated regulation only when they are autonomy-supportive, allowing the person to grasp the meaning of the regulation and synthesize it with their own values. Providing a meaningful rationale for an uninteresting behavior, along with autonomy and relatedness supports, promotes internalization and integration (Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, & Leone, 1994).

### 6. Outcomes Associated with More Autonomous vs. Controlled Motivation

Ryan & Connell (1989) -- school-age children:
- External regulation: less interest, less value, less effort, blame-shifting on negative outcomes
- Introjected regulation: more effort but also more anxiety, poor failure coping
- Identified regulation: more interest, enjoyment, positive coping, more effort

Across domains reviewed:
- Greater internalization associated with: engagement, better performance, lower dropout, higher-quality learning, better teacher ratings (education)
- Medication adherence, weight-loss maintenance, glucose control, addiction treatment attendance (health care)
- Physical exercise, political activity, environmental activism, intimate relationships

### 7. Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Goal Content and Well-Being

Kasser & Ryan (1993, 1996):
- Strong relative emphasis on **intrinsic aspirations** (affiliation, personal growth, community) was positively associated with self-esteem, self-actualization, and lower depression/anxiety
- Strong relative emphasis on **extrinsic aspirations** (wealth, fame, image) was negatively associated with well-being indicators
- Finding replicated in Russian sample (Ryan et al., 1999)
- Attainment of intrinsic goals enhanced well-being; attainment of extrinsic goals did not (Sheldon & Kasser, 1998, longitudinal)

### 8. Daily Need Satisfaction Predicts Daily Well-Being

Reis, Sheldon, Gable, Roscoe, & Ryan (in press at time of publication):
- Variations in fulfillment of each of the three needs independently predicted variability in daily well-being

Sheldon, Reis, & Ryan (1996) -- hierarchical linear modeling:
- Within-person daily fluctuations in autonomy and competence need satisfaction predicted within-person fluctuations in mood, vitality, physical symptoms, and self-esteem

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**None reported directly in this paper.** This is a theoretical overview. Key empirical sources to cite for effect sizes:

- **Reward undermining effects**: Deci, Koestner, & Ryan (1999) meta-analysis -- all expected tangible rewards contingent on performance reliably undermine intrinsic motivation (effect sizes in that paper)
- **Autonomy-supportive conditions**: Vallerand & Reid (1984) -- feedback effects on intrinsic motivation mediated by perceived competence
- **Internalization outcomes**: Ryan & Connell (1989) -- quasi-simplex pattern of correlations across motivation types in school children
- **Daily well-being**: Sheldon et al. (1996); Reis et al. (in press) -- hierarchical linear modeling, effect sizes in those sources

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Core mechanism -- Perceived Locus of Causality (PLOC):**
External events shift PLOC outward (undermining autonomy) or support an internal PLOC (sustaining autonomy). The functional significance of an event (e.g., a reward, feedback) depends on whether it conveys information about competence in an autonomy-supportive way, or whether it conveys control.

**Competence + Autonomy interaction:**
Competence boosts do not translate to intrinsic motivation unless autonomy is also present. Both must be satisfied simultaneously.

**Internalization pathway:**
Relatedness -> initial adoption -> Competence -> capability to sustain -> Autonomy -> integration into self

**Intrinsic vs. extrinsic goal content:**
Goals that directly satisfy basic needs (intrinsic aspirations) produce durable well-being; goals that only indirectly satisfy needs (extrinsic aspirations) do not, even when attained.

**Moderator -- Activity type:**
CET applies only to intrinsically interesting activities. OIT applies to non-intrinsically interesting behaviors. These are different processes requiring different interventions.

**Autonomy is not independence:**
Explicitly clarified: autonomy refers to volitional quality of action, not to individualism. Collectivist and interdependent behaviors can be highly autonomous. Korean-US data shows autonomy correlates more positively with collectivist attitudes than individualistic ones (Kim, Butzel, & Ryan, 1998).

## Study Design Details

This is a narrative theoretical review, not a primary empirical study. It introduces/summarizes two SDT subtheories:

- **CET (Cognitive Evaluation Theory)**: Specifies conditions that facilitate/undermine intrinsic motivation, framed around competence and autonomy needs
- **OIT (Organismic Integration Theory)**: Describes forms of extrinsic motivation and the continuum of internalization; framed around all three needs

Evidence base: Experimental laboratory paradigms + field studies across education, health care, sport, religion, work, parenting.

---

## Limitations

- No original data reported -- all conclusions summarize other work; specific effect sizes require consulting cited primary sources
- Primary evidence base is from Western (largely US) academic and clinical contexts, though some cross-cultural replication is noted (Russian, Korean samples)
- Most internalization research at time of writing was correlational or conducted with children/students; workplace and adult consumer-context evidence is thinner
- "Relatedness" as a need for intrinsic motivation (vs. internalization) is described as distal -- proximal relational supports may not be required once a secure base exists; this is less precisely operationalized than the autonomy/competence findings
- The paper acknowledges that need expression modes vary across cultures, but the extent to which specific gamification mechanics map onto different cultural need-satisfaction profiles is not addressed

## Conclusions

"We have assumed that humans have an inclination toward activity and integration, but also have a vulnerability to passivity. Our focus, accordingly, has been to specify the conditions that tend to support people's natural activity versus elicit or exploit their vulnerability."

Key takeaways:
1. Motivation quality matters as much as motivation quantity -- the type of motivation (autonomous vs. controlled) determines persistence, performance, and well-being outcomes
2. Three universal needs (competence, autonomy, relatedness) operate as psychological nutrients; deprivation of any one produces impoverishment of well-being even if others are satisfied
3. Extrinsic rewards can be used to motivate without undermining intrinsic motivation, but only when they are non-contingent on performance or when they convey informational (rather than controlling) feedback
4. The path from extrinsic to autonomous motivation runs through internalization -- supported by relatedness first, then competence, then autonomy

## Named Frameworks

### Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
- Overarching framework; metatheory for motivation and personality
- Components: CET (Cognitive Evaluation Theory) + OIT (Organismic Integration Theory) + basic needs framework

### Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)
- Focus: Intrinsic motivation variability
- Components: Perceived locus of causality (PLOC), competence-enhancing feedback, autonomy support
- Design alignment: Feedback systems, reward structures, challenge calibration in apps

### Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)
- Focus: Internalization of extrinsic motivation
- Components: Six-point continuum (amotivation -> intrinsic), internalization stages
- Design alignment: Onboarding, goal rationale provision, community/social layers to support initial adoption

## Extraction Notes

This is the most-cited SDT paper in gamification research and serves as the definitional anchor for the entire framework. Do not rely on this paper for effect sizes -- it is a theoretical overview. Use it to:
1. Cite precise definitions of autonomy, competence, relatedness, intrinsic motivation, and the OIT continuum
2. Establish the theoretical claim that all expected contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (citing the Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999 meta-analysis via this paper)
3. Support the design principle that competence feedback alone is insufficient -- autonomy must also be present

The key practical tension for gamification: points, badges, and leaderboards are tangible expected contingent rewards -- by CET, they should undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks that were already intrinsically interesting. This is the core "undermining effect" that gamification critics cite. Designers working with SDT must either (a) use non-contingent, informational rewards, or (b) accept that their users are not intrinsically motivated and instead focus OIT-style on moving them along the internalization continuum.
