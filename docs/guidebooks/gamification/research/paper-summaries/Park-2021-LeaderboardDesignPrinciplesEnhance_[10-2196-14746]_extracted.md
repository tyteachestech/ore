# Leaderboard Design Principles to Enhance Learning and Motivation in a Gamified Educational Environment: Development Study

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Park, S. & Kim, S., 2021
- **Study Type**: Design/Development Study (literature review + case analysis — NOT empirical; no original data collected)
- **Sample**: n=10 real-world leaderboard cases analyzed (Duolingo, Khan Academy, Memrise, Fitbit, NikePlus, Reebok CrossFit "The Open," Pokemon Go/Imgur, Science Level Up, Teaching Above the Test, Sololearn); no human participants
- **DOI**: 10.2196/14746
- **PMID**: Not listed

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Leaderboards outperform other mechanics for motivation**: O'Donovan et al. (cited) found leaderboards influence learners' motivation more than progress bars, end prizes, and awarded badges. Effect reported in narrative citation only — no effect size provided.

- **Finding 2 — Rank position drives divergent psychological responses**: Learners in upper/near-upper ranks engage in upward counterfactual thinking ("I could have ranked third") which sustains motivation. Learners in lower ranks engage in downward counterfactual thinking ("I will probably drop further"), which, if repeated, erodes confidence and motivation. This is theoretical framing drawn from Roese (1994, 1997) — no original empirical test.

- **Finding 3 — Leaderboard-induced competition increases engagement**: Butler (2013, cited) found that players who experienced competition played at least once more than those who did not. High scores were NOT correlated with fun. Games perceived as too easy reduce fun. No effect size provided.

- **Finding 4 — Large rank gaps undermine low-ranked learners**: When the gap between upper and lower ranks is large, satisfaction with learning decreases (cited from Buunk & Gibbons, 2000). Learners who see peers ranked lower than themselves show increased confidence and engagement (cited from Major et al., 1991). No original data.

- **Finding 5 — Macro-only leaderboards create systematic demotivation for lower performers**: Macro leaderboards aggregate all performance into a single ranking. This provides no granular feedback, makes lower-ranked learners feel inadequate, and cannot produce frequent micro-competitions. This is the central design problem the framework addresses.

- **Finding 6 — Case analysis validation**: Of 10 real-world leaderboard systems analyzed, Reebok CrossFit "The Open," Khan Academy, and Imgur/Pokemon Go were rated as most fully applying all 3 design principles. Science Level Up applied none of the principles (no macro leaderboard, only one micro leaderboard per content type, no demographic segmentation).

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism 1 — Counterfactual thinking**: Leaderboard position triggers either upward (motivating) or downward (demotivating) counterfactual thinking. Proximity to a higher rank (e.g., ranked 7th, comparing to 3rd) enables upward counterfactual. Large gaps prevent it.
  - Evidence: Cited from Sun et al. (2015) CHI conference; Roese (1994, 1997)

- **Mechanism 2 — Relative deprivation**: When learners compare themselves to significantly higher-ranked peers, they experience inadequacy. Smaller leaderboards or segmented leaderboards (by skill, gender, age) reduce this effect by making comparisons more proximate.
  - Evidence: Walker & Pettigrew (1984); cited framework only

- **Mechanism 3 — Motivational affordance via scarcity**: Lower-ranked learners may disengage from learning leaderboards entirely. Introducing non-learning leaderboards where they can rank higher reactivates engagement with learning leaderboards through a scarcity mechanism (wanting what feels attainable).
  - Evidence: Zhao & Tang (2016) octalysis analysis

- **Moderator 1 — Leaderboard granularity**: Micro leaderboards (segmented by specific skill/activity) produce more frequent competition opportunities and more chances for any learner to rank highly, reducing demotivation.

- **Moderator 2 — Demographic segmentation**: Segmenting by gender, age group, region increases the number of leaderboards a learner competes on simultaneously, increasing the probability of high-rank experience.

- **Moderator 3 — Non-learning activity inclusion**: "Geeks leaderboards" (for classroom tasks like attendance, library visits, questions asked) provide alternative pathways for lower-ranked academic learners to experience upward movement and reenter motivational flow.

## Study Design Details

- **Method**: Two-phase design study: (1) literature review to identify problems with existing leaderboards and derive design objectives; (2) Google search of real-world leaderboard cases, coded against the derived design principles
- **Case collection**: 10 cases; keywords: "leaderboard case(s)," "gamified leaderboard case(s)," "leaderboard in sport" — Google search only, no systematic database search
- **Rating scale**: Three-point scale (● completely applied, ◐ partially applied, ○ not applied) applied to each case against each of the 3 design principles
- **No control condition**: No experimental comparison; purely descriptive case analysis
- **Interrater reliability**: Not reported
- **Blinding**: Not applicable

## Limitations

- **No empirical validation**: The design principles are derived from theory and case analysis but have not been tested with real learners. The paper explicitly states this as a limitation and calls for future validation.
- **Case selection bias**: Cases were identified via non-systematic Google search. Sampling is not representative or exhaustive.
- **No reliability check on case coding**: Only two authors; no interrater reliability reported for the principle-application ratings.
- **Leaderboard effectiveness assumed, not demonstrated**: The claim that applying these principles improves satisfaction and performance is stated as an expectation, not a finding.
- **Education-context specificity**: Principles are developed for classroom/educational gamification. Generalizability to habit tracking, fitness, or other behavior change contexts is not tested.
- **Citation chain risk**: Many foundational claims rely on conference papers, book chapters, and non-peer-reviewed sources rather than primary empirical studies with effect sizes.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Students (K-12 and higher education classroom contexts); secondary reference to web-based learners on platforms like Duolingo, Khan Academy
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium — the structural design principles (macro/micro layering, non-learning leaderboards, demographic segmentation) are transferable to habit tracking contexts, but all examples and validation are education-specific
- **Red flags**: No clinical population. No compulsive behavior. Framework paper — no causal claims about behavior change supported by data. Thin empirical grounding; treat findings as design heuristics, not evidence-based conclusions.

## Conclusions

"This study contributes toward resolving problems associated with leaderboard design for the application of gamification in educational environments. Based upon our results, we strongly suggest that when teachers consider applying gamification in classrooms, the leaderboard design principles suggested in this research should be incorporated."

The three core principles are: (1) use macro and micro leaderboards together; (2) map every measurable achievement element to a separate micro leaderboard; (3) include non-learning activity leaderboards ("geeks leaderboards") to re-engage lower-ranked participants. These are theoretically grounded design heuristics, not empirically validated principles.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Macro/Micro Leaderboard Framework**
  - Components: Macro leaderboard (overall aggregate ranking) + Micro leaderboards (per-element rankings) + Non-learning leaderboards ("geeks leaderboards")
  - Research alignment:
    - Macro leaderboard: overall performance feedback, goal-setting for upper/mid ranks
    - Micro leaderboards: granular feedback, minimizes relative deprivation, enables upward counterfactual thinking for lower-ranked learners
    - Non-learning leaderboards: re-engagement pathway via scarcity and success experience

- **4 Design Objectives** (derived from literature review):
  - Objective 1: Minimize relative deprivation
  - Objective 2: Minimize failure experience / downward counterfactual thinking
  - Objective 3: Maximize success experience / upward counterfactual thinking
  - Objective 4: Design leaderboards that measure specific performance to induce frequent competition
