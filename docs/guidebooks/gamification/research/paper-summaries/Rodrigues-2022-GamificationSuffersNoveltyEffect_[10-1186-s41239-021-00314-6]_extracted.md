# Gamification Suffers from the Novelty Effect but Benefits from the Familiarization Effect: Findings from a Longitudinal Study

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Rodrigues, L., Pereira, F. D., Toda, A. M., Palomino, P. T., Pessoa, M., Carvalho, L. S. G., Fernandes, D., Oliveira, E. H. T., Cristea, A. I., & Isotani, S. (2022)
- **Study Type**: Quasi-experimental longitudinal (2x7 repeated-measures design)
- **Sample**: n=756 total (control n=138, experimental n=659); Brazilian undergraduate STEM students enrolled in introductory programming (CS1) courses at Federal University of Amazonas (UFAM). Age M=22.2 years (SD=4.2); 62.3% male, 37.7% female. 14 STEM programs represented.
- **DOI**: 10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6
- **PMID**: Not available

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall gamification advantage**: Over the full 14-week intervention, gamified students attempted problems 17.5% more, used the IDE 48.6% more, and accessed the system 220% more than controls.
  - Effect sizes (Hedges' g, overall): Attempts g=0.144 (small); IDE usage g=0.341 (moderate); System access g=0.948 (large)
  - Gamification was never negatively significant at any time-point across any measure.

- **Finding 2 — Novelty effect confirmed**: Gamification's effect began declining after ~4 weeks (time-point 2) across all three behavioral measures.
  - For attempts: effect dropped from ES=0.448 at TP1 to ES=0.033 at TP3 (nonsignificant, p=0.982).
  - For IDE usage: effect dropped from ES=0.599 at TP1 to ES=0.123 at TP5 (only marginally significant, p=0.056).
  - For system access: effect dropped from ES=0.794 at TP1 to its trough at TP4 (ES=0.561), a relative decline though remained significant throughout.

- **Finding 3 — Familiarization effect confirmed**: After the decline phase (2–6 weeks of reduced effect), gamification's impact shifted back to an uptrend without any design change.
  - For attempts: progressive recovery from TP3 (ES=0.033) to TP7 (ES=0.331, p<0.001).
  - For IDE usage: small rebound at TP6–7 (ES=0.199) after trough at TP5.
  - For system access: rebound at TP5 (ES=0.783) after trough at TP4 (ES=0.561), then maintained through TP7 (ES=0.749).

- **Finding 4 — U-shaped temporal pattern**: Effect sizes across all three measures followed an approximate U-shaped curve over the 14 weeks: high at start, declining in the middle (weeks 4–8 depending on measure), then recovering in the latter half. This pattern was identified via visual/exploratory inspection of confidence intervals, not a formal polynomial fit.

- **Finding 5 — Safe period heuristic**: Gamification's effect was maintained or increased for the first ~4 weeks before the novelty effect began acting. Authors identify 4 weeks as a practitioner "safe period" threshold.

- **Finding 6 — Recovery timeline**: The familiarization-effect uptrend began between weeks 6–10 of the intervention across measures. The initial effect level was not fully restored; attempts continued increasing through week 14, while IDE usage and system access plateaued.

- **Finding 7 — Minimum study duration implication**: Studies shorter than 12 weeks are likely to produce misleading results because they will either capture only the novelty peak (if very short) or only the trough (if ending at weeks 6–8), missing the familiarization recovery.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism 1 — Novelty effect**: Initial boost from the novelty/newness of the gamified interface fades as learners habituate. Authors propose that decline beginning at week 4 reflects the point at which the system is no longer novel.
- **Mechanism 2 — Familiarization effect**: After initial decline, users who remain engaged with gamification learn to use game mechanics more effectively, leading to a natural recovery in effect. Authors frame this as users needing time to "fully familiarize themselves with gamification" before it reaches its behavioral potential.
- **Mechanism 3 — Competitive-collaborative design as moderator**: The gamification design deliberately included fictional narrative and competitive-collaborative elements (rather than standard points/badges/leaderboards). Meta-analytic evidence (Sailer & Homner, 2019) cited by authors suggests these elements enhance gamification outcomes on behavioral measures. Authors propose this design choice may explain why the effect never turned negative, unlike designs relying on extrinsic elements alone.
- **Moderator — Behavioral outcome type**: The pattern and magnitude of effect varied substantially across the three outcomes. System access showed uniformly large, persistent effects (never dropped below ES=0.561). Attempts showed the strongest novelty-to-floor decline and also the clearest familiarization recovery. IDE usage showed an intermediate pattern with a weaker recovery.
- **Moderator — Age (assessed, found negligible)**: Age was a statistically significant predictor for attempts (beta=-0.05) and system access (beta=-0.04) but R²<1% for both; treated as practically irrelevant by authors.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Gamified version of Codebench (online judge/IDE for programming). Game elements: narrative (medieval fantasy world), avatar customization, badges tied to assignment performance, collaborative monster-defeating mission, competitive leaderboard (top 3 receive special reward). Students solved non-compulsory programming assignment lists worth ~6% of final grade.
- **Control/Comparison**: Same Codebench system without any game elements. Control participants = 2016 cohort; experimental = 2017–2018 cohorts.
- **Ecological setting**: Real university courses, real assignments; not a lab experiment. No randomization; assignment by enrollment year.
- **Follow-up period**: 14 weeks (full academic semester); 7 time-points measured in 2-week intervals.
- **Attrition**: 19.7% of control and 24.2% of experimental students excluded for failing by attendance. The differential attrition rate (higher in experimental group) is a potential confound not fully addressed.
- **Blinding**: Not applicable (ecological setting).
- **Analysis method**: Robust ANOVA on 20% trimmed means (WRS2 package in R); Yuen-Welch post-hoc tests; FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Visual/exploratory analysis of effect size trajectories rather than polynomial regression.

## Limitations

- **No random assignment**: Groups assigned by enrollment year (2016=control, 2017–2018=experimental). Cohort effects cannot be fully ruled out.
- **Unbalanced group sizes**: Control n=138 vs. experimental n=659. Authors note robust methods mitigate this but it remains a design weakness.
- **Different instructors across years**: UFAM rotation policy meant instructors varied across cohorts; partially mitigated by consistent pedagogical plans.
- **U-shape conclusion is exploratory, not confirmatory**: The U-shaped pattern was identified via visual inspection of CI plots, not a formal nonlinear model. Authors explicitly caution this is an exploratory characterization.
- **Single context, single gamification design**: Findings are limited to Brazilian STEM undergraduates completing programming assignments. Cannot disentangle effects of gamification design from task type (programming assignments may be uniquely suited to this gamification approach).
- **Behavioral outcomes only**: No motivational, cognitive, or long-term learning outcomes measured. Increased system access or attempts does not directly demonstrate improved learning.
- **Differential attrition**: Higher dropout in the gamified condition (24.2% vs. 19.7%) suggests possible self-selection survivor bias in the experimental group — those remaining may be more engaged at baseline.
- **Single country, single university**: All participants from UFAM, Brazil. Generalizability to other cultural or educational contexts is unknown.
- **No pre-test**: Cannot rule out pre-existing behavioral differences between the 2016 cohort and 2017–2018 cohorts beyond age.
- **Studies shorter than 12 weeks (self-referential implication)**: Authors note most prior gamification research would fail to capture the full novelty+familiarization arc.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (STEM undergraduates, introductory programming context), Brazil
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium — the core findings (novelty effect timeline, familiarization effect, U-shaped pattern) are theoretically generalizable to gamified habit/behavior systems, but the population is narrow (students) and the task is inherently academic (programming practice)
- **Red flags**: Not clinical. However, the educational context (mandatory course, grade incentive of 6%) differs meaningfully from voluntary self-improvement contexts. Gamification here operates within an institutional structure, which may artificially floor the control group's motivation compared to a fully voluntary scenario.

## Conclusions

The study provides empirical evidence that gamification in educational settings suffers from the novelty effect (effect begins declining after ~4 weeks) but recovers via a familiarization effect (uptrend beginning weeks 6–10), producing an overall U-shaped pattern of effectiveness across a 14-week semester. Across all measures and time-points, gamification was never harmful. Authors conclude: "gamification's impact on students' behaviors suffers from the novelty effect, but benefits from the familiarization effect, a counter-balance that is likely responsible for the overall positive effect gamification often demonstrates in maximizing behavioral learning outcomes." They recommend that practitioners consider a ~4-week "safe period" and that researchers using fewer than 12 weeks of data may be capturing an unrepresentative slice of the gamification effect curve.

## Named Frameworks

- **Novelty Effect**: The hypothesis that gamification's benefits are driven partly by the newness of the experience, and therefore diminish as users habituate. This study provides empirical support with a specific onset timeframe (~week 4) and duration estimate (2–6 weeks).
- **Familiarization Effect**: The counterpart hypothesis proposed and empirically supported in this paper — after the novelty-driven decline, users who continue engaging gradually learn to leverage game mechanics more effectively, producing a natural recovery in behavioral outcomes (uptrend beginning weeks 6–10).

## Quotable Passages

> "Supporting the novelty effect, the gamification's effect started to decrease after four weeks, decrease that lasted between two to six weeks. Interestingly, the gamification's impact shifted to an uptrend between six and 10 weeks after the start of the intervention, partially recovering its contribution naturally." (Abstract)

> "Studies lasting less than 12 weeks are likely to be insufficient for truly revealing gamification's impact. Our findings indicate that the novelty effect starts to act only after four weeks. In addition, our findings show that the final uptrend of the familiarization might start only after 10 or 12 weeks. Therefore, studies lasting less than this period of time might reveal unreliable findings, as they might fail to consider such changes that happen during learners' experiences with a gamified intervention." (Discussion — Implications, p. 19)

> "Despite a gamified intervention seemingly losing power or failing to work after some time, it is likely to gain power again in the future, although the effect might not go back to its initial values." (Discussion — Findings, p. 18)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: University students in structured academic courses; gamification designs featuring fictional/narrative elements plus competitive-collaborative mechanics; task contexts requiring repeated voluntary practice (programming assignments); interventions spanning a full semester (~14 weeks).
- **Does NOT apply to**: Short-term gamification deployments (<4 weeks may only capture the novelty peak); non-educational/voluntary habit contexts where there is no institutional incentive structure; gamification designs based purely on extrinsic elements (points, badges, standard leaderboards) — the fictional+collaborative design here may be a meaningful moderator; populations outside STEM higher education in Brazil; clinical or compulsive behavior change.
- **Unknown/untested**: Whether the U-shaped pattern holds for motivational or cognitive outcomes (only behavioral log-data measured); whether the familiarization effect appears in non-student/non-educational contexts; whether tailored/adaptive gamification can flatten or eliminate the novelty dip; individual differences in susceptibility to novelty vs. familiarization effects; whether the 4-week threshold generalizes beyond this specific task and gamification design.

## Extraction Notes

This is a methodologically sound quasi-experimental longitudinal study with a genuinely useful practical finding for the NotionRPG context: gamification effects on behavior follow a U-shaped arc over a semester, with a ~4-week "safe period" before novelty fades and a natural recovery at weeks 6–10. The headline numbers (17.5% more attempts, 48.6% more IDE use, 220% more system access) are compelling but the system access result likely reflects the gamification UI itself (users logging in to see their game state) more than genuine learning behavior change — worth flagging if citing for habit-tracking applications.

Key concern: the gamification design here is quite rich (narrative + avatar + collaborative monster quest + competitive leaderboard) compared to simpler gamification implementations. The finding that this sophisticated design never produced negative effects may not generalize to simpler badge-only or points-only systems. The differential attrition (higher dropout in the gamified group) also means the experimental group at later time-points is a positively selected survivor sample, which may inflate later effect sizes for the familiarization recovery.

The "minimum 12-week study duration" implication is particularly relevant for NotionRPG product thinking — it suggests that user retention/engagement data collected before 12 weeks should be interpreted cautiously.
