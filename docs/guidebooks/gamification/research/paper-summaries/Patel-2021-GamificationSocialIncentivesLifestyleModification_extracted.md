# Effect of Behaviorally Designed Gamification With Social Incentives on Lifestyle Modification (Patel et al., 2021)

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Patel et al., 2021
- **Study Type**: 4-arm RCT (iDiabetes trial)
- **Sample**: n=361, adults with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c ≥8%), BMI ≥25, mean age 52.5 years; 56% women, 51% Black, 40% White
- **Setting**: University of Pennsylvania health system; remotely monitored via wearables + app
- **Duration**: 1-year intervention (January 2017 to January 2020)
- **DOI**: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.10255
- **Registration**: NCT02961192

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Competition works, sustained**: Gamification with competition increased daily steps by 606 vs. control over 1 year (95% CI 201 to 1011; P=.003). No significant activity decline from months 1-6 to months 7-12 (-143 steps; 95% CI -414 to 128; P=.23), indicating sustained effect.

- **Finding 2 — Support works, but fades**: Gamification with support increased daily steps by 503 vs. control (95% CI 103 to 903; P=.01). However, activity declined significantly in the second half of the trial (-423 steps months 7-12 vs. months 1-6; 95% CI -624 to -223; P=.003).

- **Finding 3 — Collaboration among strangers fails**: Collaboration with unknown peers produced only 280 additional steps vs. control (95% CI -115 to 674; P=.16). This arm showed a significant within-arm decline from months 1-6 to 7-12 (-394 steps; 95% CI -512 to -276; P<.001).

- **Finding 4 — No differential effect on weight or HbA1c**: All arms (including control) reduced weight from baseline. At 12 months: control -2.0 kg, support -3.6 kg, collaboration -3.4 kg, competition -2.9 kg. None of the intervention arms differed significantly from control (support: -1.9 kg, P=.09; collaboration: -1.5 kg, P=.22; competition: -1.1 kg, P=.36). HbA1c also declined in all arms with no between-group differences.

- **Finding 5 — Behavioral economics mechanisms embedded**: Game used loss framing (points deducted for missed weigh-ins leveraging prospect theory), level progression creating goal gradients and status, precommitment pledges, fresh-start weekly resets, and physician reporting for social accountability.

- **Finding 6 — Engagement sustained above 50% in gamified arms**: Gamification arms maintained >50% weekly weigh-in rate for all 52 weeks; control arm dropped below 50% by week 12.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Primary outcome — daily step count change vs. control, months 1-12 (adjusted):**
- Support: +503 steps (95% CI 103 to 903), P=.01
- Collaboration: +280 steps (95% CI -115 to 674), P=.16 [non-significant]
- Competition: +606 steps (95% CI 201 to 1011), P=.003

**Step count change vs. control, months 1-6 (adjusted):**
- Support: +691 steps (95% CI 267 to 1115), P=.001
- Collaboration: +468 steps (95% CI 43 to 892), P=.03
- Competition: +639 steps (95% CI 204 to 1074), P=.004

**Within-arm trajectory: last 6 months vs. first 6 months (adjusted):**
- Support: -423 steps (95% CI -624 to -223), P=.003 [significant decline]
- Collaboration: -394 steps (95% CI -512 to -276), P<.001 [significant decline]
- Competition: -143 steps (95% CI -414 to 128), P=.23 [no significant decline]
- Control: -43 steps (95% CI -235 to 149), P=.60

**Note on magnitude**: +503-606 steps = ~12-13% increase from baseline (~4,400-4,700 steps/day). No standardized Cohen's d reported; raw step differences only.

**Weight at 12 months, adjusted difference vs. control:**
- Support: -1.9 kg (95% CI -4.2 to 0.3), P=.09
- Collaboration: -1.5 kg (95% CI -3.8 to 0.9), P=.22
- Competition: -1.1 kg (95% CI -3.5 to 1.3), P=.36

**HbA1c at 12 months, adjusted difference vs. control:**
- Support: +0.05% (95% CI -0.56 to 0.65), P=.88
- Collaboration: -0.14% (95% CI -0.80 to 0.52), P=.68
- Competition: -0.32% (95% CI -0.91 to 0.27), P=.28

**LDL-C at 12 months, adjusted difference vs. control:**
- Support: -4.6 mg/dL (95% CI -18.6 to 9.4), P=.50
- Collaboration: -8.2 mg/dL (95% CI -24.7 to 8.2), P=.29
- Competition: -3.9 mg/dL (95% CI -18.1 to 10.4), P=.57

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Social tie strength determines collaboration effectiveness**: Collaboration worked when group members had preexisting strong ties (families in Framingham Heart Cohort study, ref 18), but failed in both this trial and a prior consulting-employee trial (ref 19) where participants were strangers. Weight-loss collaboration trial found cohabitants lost more (4.5 vs. 2.7 kg). The authors' synthesis: collaborative social incentives require strong preexisting relationships; they do not generate social cohesion from scratch.

- **Competition suits weak-tie groups**: Competition was effective in both this trial and the prior consulting employee trial, and effects held up at 12 months. Authors hypothesize competition may be better suited to groups of strangers because it doesn't require mutual investment in others' outcomes.

- **Support sustainability problem**: Support relies on a known family member/friend sponsor who receives weekly progress emails. This mechanism may degrade as novelty fades for the sponsor, or as the ongoing involvement becomes a burden. Support effects were strong in months 1-6 but declined in 7-12.

- **Loss framing embedded in points system**: Weekly 70-point starting balance with 10-point deductions for missed weigh-ins (not a reward system -- a loss-avoidance system). Levels convey social status. These are intentional behavioral economics levers, not decorative game elements.

- **Re-engagement checkpoints**: Participants in blue/bronze levels at months 3, 6, and 9 received a phone call, level reset to silver, and opportunity to recalibrate goals. This attenuates extreme attrition in gamified systems.

- **No moderator analyses reported** for demographic subgroups (race, sex, income) on step outcomes. Baseline activity levels were similar across arms.

## Study Design Details

- **Arms**: (1) Control -- wearable + scale + app feedback only; (2) Gamification + support -- points/levels + known sponsor receives weekly reports; (3) Gamification + collaboration -- team of 3 strangers, one randomly selected daily to represent team, team loses points if that person missed weigh-in; (4) Gamification + competition -- leaderboard of 3 strangers ranked on cumulative points weekly
- **Game mechanics**: Points (70/week starting balance), levels (blue-bronze-silver-gold-platinum), weekly fresh start, precommitment pledge, goal setting (step goal, weight goal, HbA1c goal), physician progress letters
- **Control**: Received wearable and scale feedback only -- a reasonably active control (devices themselves may drive some activity)
- **Follow-up period**: 1 year, no post-intervention follow-up period reported
- **Attrition**: Low -- 22-25 of 361 discontinued (6-9% per arm); intention-to-treat analysis with multiple imputation
- **Missing data**: Step data missing rate ~40.5% in control, lower in gamification arms; weight/lab data rates described in supplement
- **Blinding**: Investigators and statisticians blinded to arm assignments until analysis complete

## Limitations

- **Clinical population only**: T2D patients with HbA1c ≥8% and BMI ≥25 -- highly specific. Generalizability to consumer apps and healthy adults is limited. These participants had a medical motivation to change behavior that most app users lack.
- **Single health system**: All from Penn Medicine; smartphone ownership required; may skew more health-engaged.
- **Step count as sole activity measure**: No data on intensity (moderate/vigorous vs. light), which limits health outcome interpretation.
- **Strangers-only collaboration**: Only 4 of 361 participants were in groups where members knew each other; the design essentially tested worst-case collaboration, not collaboration generally.
- **No communication data captured**: Cannot evaluate whether competition/collaboration groups actually interacted, or why collaboration failed mechanistically.
- **No dietary data**: Weight outcomes confounded by unmeasured caloric intake changes.
- **Medication effects confound HbA1c**: 72.3% were on insulin at baseline; study participation and connected devices may have improved medication adherence in all arms, masking any gamification signal.
- **Conflict of interest**: Lead author (Patel) receives personal fees from health tech companies (Catalyst Health LLC, HealthMine, Life.io). Volpp has industry funding. Both are at the Penn Medicine Nudge Unit. Results are broadly consistent with prior work; no overt distortion evident, but commercial relationships noted.
- **No post-intervention follow-up**: Cannot assess whether gains are maintained after the game ends.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Clinical -- adults with uncontrolled T2D, mean HbA1c 9.6%, mean BMI 37.1, mean age 52.5. Sedentary at baseline (~4,400-4,700 steps/day).
- **Relevance to consumer apps**: Medium. The game mechanics (points, levels, leaderboards, social incentives) are directly applicable. The population's medical motivation and clinical monitoring context don't replicate typical consumer settings. Effects may be smaller in less-motivated populations.
- **Red flags**: Clinical chronic disease population. The social dynamics findings (collaboration among strangers fails, competition among strangers works) may generalize, but the effect sizes for engagement cannot be assumed to transfer to healthy adults with lower intrinsic motivation.

## Conclusions

Among adults with uncontrolled T2D, a behaviorally designed gamification RCT found that support and competition each significantly increased physical activity (~500-600 steps/day, 12-13% above baseline) vs. control over 1 year. Competition effects were sustained; support effects declined in months 7-12. Collaboration among strangers failed in this trial and a prior one; prior evidence suggests collaboration requires strong preexisting social ties. No gamification arm produced differential improvements in weight or HbA1c beyond the control group's own improvements. The trial is the longest behaviorally designed gamification RCT known at publication.

## Extraction Notes

This is a high-quality, mechanistically rich RCT with strong design (blinded analysts, ITT, 1-year duration, multiple behavioral economics levers explicitly mapped). The social incentive differentiation is the paper's most guidebook-relevant contribution: the collaboration-requires-strong-ties finding is replicable across 3 trials (this one + refs 18 and 19) and is a genuinely useful design constraint for anyone building social features in consumer apps.

Key caution: the clinical population creates a relevance gap. These participants had uncontrolled diabetes and were recruited from a health system -- their motivation baseline and context differ substantially from consumer app users. The 12-13% step increase is a reasonable signal to report but should not be treated as expected effect size for a consumer fitness app.

The lack of effect on weight/HbA1c is noteworthy: gamification moved physical activity but not downstream health outcomes. This speaks to the limits of single-lever interventions -- the game cannot compensate for unmeasured diet, medication behavior, etc.

The competition arm's sustained 12-month effect, while support's faded, is a practically important asymmetry. Socially competitive mechanics appear more robust to engagement decay than supporter-notification mechanics over time.
