# Revealing the Theoretical Basis of Gamification: A Systematic Review and Analysis of Theory in Research on Gamification, Serious Games and Game-based Learning

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Krath, J., Schürmann, L., & von Korflesch, H.F.O., 2021
- **Study Type**: Systematic meta-review (review of reviews)
- **Sample**: k=32 peer-reviewed review articles (screened from 973 initial records); these reviews collectively covered hundreds of primary empirical studies
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.chb.2021.106963
- **PMID**: Not available

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1: SDT is the dominant theory.** Self-determination theory (SDT) was used in 82 primary studies — by far the most prevalent theoretical foundation in the field. Flow theory was second (47 studies), followed by experiential learning theory (40), constructivist learning theory (31), situated learning theory (29), goal-setting theory (29), cognitive load theory (24), and social cognitive theory (16).

- **Finding 2: The field uses 118 different theories — no single theory suffices.** Across 32 review articles, researchers identified 118 distinct theoretical foundations applied to gamification, serious games, and game-based learning. 54 of those 118 theories have been applied in only one study. No single theory can fully explain how gamification works.

- **Finding 3: SDT's basic needs map directly onto gamification elements.** Studies show game mechanics address SDT's three basic psychological needs: customization → autonomy; achievements/badges → competence; teams/social networks → relatedness. Some studies find this mapping partial (Frost et al., 2015; van Roy & Zaman, 2019), others find it complete (Xi & Hamari, 2019).

- **Finding 4: Ten cross-theory principles explain how gamification works.** By analyzing conceptual overlaps across the 21 most prevalent theories, the authors derived 10 "how gamification works" principles grouped into three categories: (a) guiding toward intended outcomes, (b) fostering individual relevance, and (c) enabling social interaction. These principles are conceptually derived, not empirically validated in this paper.

- **Finding 5: Immediate feedback is the most cross-theoretically supported mechanism.** P3 (Immediate Feedback) is grounded in the most theories simultaneously: SDT, self-efficacy theory, goal-setting theory, ARCS model, social cognitive theory, theory of planned behavior, technology acceptance model, transtheoretical model, and flow theory. Points, levels, and progress bars are identified as the primary delivery mechanisms.

- **Finding 6: Positive reinforcement requires informational framing.** Per SDT's cognitive evaluation theory sub-theory, external stimuli (rewards, badges, trophies) are effective reinforcers only when they are informational in character — communicating progress and relevance — rather than controlling. Monetary incentives and punishments are flagged as counterproductive under SDT.

- **Finding 7: Social elements operate through two distinct mechanisms.** Social comparison (P5) works through self-efficacy and competitive dynamics; social norming (P6) works through normative beliefs and relatedness needs. Leaderboards primarily activate P5; team challenges and guilds primarily activate P6. These should be selected separately based on context.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

The paper's core contribution is identifying mechanisms rather than moderators. The ten derived principles:

**Category A — Guides toward intended behavioral outcomes:**
- **P1: Clear and relevant goals** — badges, achievements, quests, levels, and narratives can function as goal-setting mechanisms comparable to classical goal-setting; shown to improve performance vs. classical goal-setting in some studies (Groening & Binnewies, 2019). Theoretical basis: goal-setting theory, flow theory, SDT, ARCS model, constructivist learning theory, theory of reasoned action, theory of planned behavior.
- **P3: Immediate feedback** — points, levels, progress bars, NPC dialogue, and feedback messages all provide real-time performance information. Theoretical basis: SDT, self-efficacy, goal-setting, ARCS, social cognitive theory, TPB, TAM, transtheoretical model, flow theory.
- **P4: Positive reinforcement** — badges, trophies, praise messages, in-game rewards, and status symbols are effective when informational rather than controlling. Monetary incentives and punishments are counterproductive per SDT. Theoretical basis: reinforcement theory, ARCS, self-efficacy, transtheoretical model, SDT, activity theory, social learning theory.
- **P8: Guided paths** — suggestions, tips, warning messages, highlighting, role-playing; nudges users toward necessary actions. Theoretical basis: constructivist learning theory, sociocultural theory, theory of planned behavior, self-efficacy theory.
- **P10: Simplified user experience** — tutorials/onboarding, sub-task chunking, abstraction of real-world complexity. Theoretical basis: TAM, multimedia learning theory, cognitive load theory.

**Category B — Fosters individual relevance:**
- **P2: Individual goals** — leaderboards as personal goal-striving tools; customizable learning journeys with milestone "level bosses"; avatars representing future ideal self; performance tracking for self-monitoring. Theoretical basis: SDT, achievement goal theory, goal-setting theory, social cognitive theory.
- **P7: Adaptive content** — knowledge maps, skill trees, machine learning-adjusted difficulty tied to levels; fictional avatars tracking behavioral change stages; game elements selected to match transtheoretical stage. Theoretical basis: transtheoretical model, SDT, flow theory, self-efficacy, goal-setting, constructivist learning, sociocultural theory.
- **P9: Multiple choices** — nonlinear gameplay, branching decisions, multiple learning paths (e.g., Duolingo), free exploration in serious game environments. Theoretical basis: experiential learning theory, situated learning theory, SDT.

**Category C — Enables social interaction:**
- **P5: Social comparisons** — leaderboards, status symbols, rankings, duels, contests, reputation systems. Motivating for most users; leaderboard effects vary by sample characteristics (Christy & Fox, 2014). Theoretical basis: self-efficacy, social cognitive theory, social learning theory, cognitive load theory, social comparison theory.
- **P6: Social norming** — team formation, shared tasks, interdependence mechanisms, guilds, mentoring influencers, presentation of average statistics as normative benchmarks, synchronous/asynchronous communication. Theoretical basis: theory of reasoned action, activity theory, SDT, theory of planned behavior, TAM.

**Key moderator note:** The authors caution that not every principle is appropriate in every context. Social comparison (P5) should be omitted when collaboration is the primary goal, to avoid counterproductive competitive dynamics.

## Study Design Details

- **Study type**: Systematic meta-review (umbrella review) — a review of reviews, not primary data
- **Search strategy**: 9 scientific databases; search string applied to title, abstract, and keywords; articles published up to April 2021
- **Inclusion criteria**: Peer-reviewed journals and conference papers; systematic reviews or mixed-method studies containing a systematic review; examination of theoretical foundations used across multiple primary studies
- **Screening process**: 973 records → 627 after deduplication → 183 for full-text screening → 38 after full-text → 32 after critical appraisal
- **Reporting standard**: ROSES (advances PRISMA for qualitative/narrative syntheses)
- **Review quality**: 18 of 32 included reviews published in Q1 journals; all 32 met peer-review standards
- **Coding reliability**: 20% double-coded; intercoder agreement 85%–95% across stages
- **Principles derivation**: Conceptual/analytical, not empirically validated — derived by mapping shared core assumptions across the 21 most prevalent theories

## Limitations

- Meta-review methodology may have missed novel theories proposed in empirical studies not captured by any included review, or in book chapters and grey literature — the review is not fully exhaustive
- Five of 32 reviews did not provide counts of primary studies per theory, so the study-count totals in Table 3 are potentially underestimated
- Principles are conceptually derived from the top 21 theories only; 95+ additional identified theories were not incorporated into the principles derivation
- The derived 10 principles have not been empirically validated by this study — the paper explicitly invites further research to test them
- The dashed-line "hypothesized" connections in the theoretical landscape figure are the authors' inference, not established linkages
- Restricted to English-language publications; non-English research excluded

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Not applicable — this is a meta-review of theoretical literature, not a study of human participants
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: High — the 10 derived principles are directly applicable to behavior change app and system design; the review explicitly covers health, workplace, and education contexts
- **Red flags**: None for NotionRPG use. The paper synthesizes motivational, behavioral, and learning theories — all relevant to habit-tracking gamification design. Note that most empirical work underlying the reviews focuses on education and healthcare populations; direct applicability to casual consumer apps is an inference, not directly tested.

## Conclusions

"This systematic review has shown that scientific work on gamification, serious games and game-based learning has used a variety of theoretical foundations from different perspectives to design and evaluate gamified interventions and explain the psychological mechanisms by which gamification achieves its positive outcomes." The 10 derived principles — clear goals, individual goals, immediate feedback, positive reinforcement, social comparisons, social norming, adaptive content, guided paths, multiple choices, and simplified UX — provide a consolidated framework for understanding how gamification achieves its effects. SDT is the field's dominant framework. The authors conclude that "no single theory can explain how gamification works" and call for multi-theory research designs that trace the full path from motivation to behavior to learning outcomes.

## Named Frameworks

- **Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci)**
  - Components: Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness (basic psychological needs); Cognitive Evaluation Theory (sub-theory on informational vs. controlling rewards); Organismic Integration Theory; Goal-Contents Theory
  - Research alignment:
    - Autonomy → customization features, multiple choice paths, individual goal-setting
    - Competence → achievements, badges, progress bars, immediate feedback
    - Relatedness → teams, guilds, social networks, group challenges
    - Cognitive Evaluation → informational badges/rewards effective; monetary incentives/punishments counterproductive

- **Goal-Setting Theory (Locke, 1968; Locke & Latham, 2002)**
  - Components: Goal specificity, goal difficulty, commitment, feedback, task complexity
  - Research alignment:
    - Specific/difficult goals → badges, levels, leaderboards as goal mechanisms
    - Feedback → points, progress bars
    - Ability as moderator → adaptive difficulty systems

- **Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi)**
  - Components: Balance of challenge and skill; clear goals; immediate feedback; deep involvement
  - Research alignment:
    - Clear goals → level systems, quests
    - Challenge-skill balance → adaptive content (P7)
    - Immediate feedback → P3
    - Note: effect of gamification on flow not yet clearly established per this review

- **Transtheoretical Model (Prochaska & DiClemente)**
  - Components: Pre-contemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance stages
  - Research alignment:
    - Pre-contemplation → statistics and information messages
    - Preparation → badges and rewards for efficacy
    - Action/Maintenance → level-ups and leaderboards

## Quotable Passages

> "Self-determination theory is an omnipresent theoretical framework in gamification research. It is by far the most used theory to this date. It was used in 82 papers, followed in popularity by flow theory, constructivist learning theory, experiential learning theory and cognitive load theory as the most common theories." (Section 6.1)

> "While from a self-determination perspective (Ryan & Deci, 2020), punishments or monetary incentives can be counterproductive as reinforcers, game elements such as badges and trophies, in-game rewards, praise messages or status symbols...are more informational in nature about the performance and relevance of the user's progress and thus can represent effective forms of reinforcements." (Section 5, P4 derivation)

> "The great variety of 118 different theoretical foundations in use also shows that there is no single theory that can explain how gamification works...one of the future goals of gamification research should be to consider a broader variety of theoretical foundations to demonstrate empirically how gamification works, rather than choosing only one of these theories." (Section 6.1)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Design of gamification systems across education, health, workplace, and consumer behavior contexts; most applicable when motivation fades over time and structured engagement is needed
- **Does NOT apply to**: Direct empirical effect size estimation — this review produces principles, not effect sizes; also does not resolve conflicting empirical results (e.g., leaderboard backfire effects are noted but not resolved here
- **Unknown/untested**: The 10 principles are conceptually derived and have not been empirically validated as a unified framework by any study cited; the specific combination of principles optimal for casual behavior change apps (vs. education or clinical settings) is untested; long-term engagement and novelty decay effects are not addressed

## Extraction Notes

This paper is unusually valuable as a theory-mapping resource. Its primary contribution is NOT effect size data — it has none — but rather a consolidated map of which theories underpin gamification research and how they inter-relate. For NotionRPG:

1. The SDT dominance (82 studies) combined with the direct mapping of SDT needs → game elements gives strong theoretical grounding for NotionRPG's design choices (badges/achievements = competence; customization = autonomy; social features = relatedness).

2. The 10 principles (P1–P10) are directly actionable as design heuristics. Especially P3 (immediate feedback), P4 (positive reinforcement with informational framing), P1 (clear goals), and P7 (adaptive content).

3. The paper's explicit warning that informational rewards outperform controlling rewards under SDT is important for NotionRPG feature design — points and progress bars that communicate progress are preferable to external monetary-style incentives.

4. The "no single theory suffices" finding is useful for deflecting overly reductive claims in the guidebook.

5. Limitation to note: all principles are cross-sectional and conceptually derived. The paper does not address novelty decay, long-term retention of gamification effects, or population heterogeneity (see Rodrigues-2022 for novelty decay; Mogavi-2022 for backfire cases).
