# An Empirical Test of the Theory of Gamified Learning: The Effect of Leaderboards on Time-on-Task and Academic Performance

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Landers, R. N. & Landers, A. K., 2014 (pub. 2015)
- **Study Type**: Experimental (true random assignment, field setting)
- **Sample**: n=86 consenting participants (109 total enrolled, 86 consented); upper-division I/O psychology undergraduates at a large U.S. east coast university; predominantly online/distance students; 75% female; mean age 32.4; mean GPA 3.19; diverse majors
- **Condition split**: nGamified=42, nControl=44
- **DOI**: 10.1177/1046878114563662
- **PMID**: unknown

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Leaderboard increases time-on-task (H2 supported)**: Students in the leaderboard condition made 29.61 more wiki edits on average than controls. This is a causal finding by experimental design.
  - Effect size: r=.33, r2=.12, t(84)=3.40, p=.001
  - Interpretation: ~12% of variance in time-on-task explained by leaderboard presence; described by authors as a moderate effect.

- **Finding 2 — Time-on-task predicts academic performance (H1 supported)**: More wiki edits predicted higher scores across all four graded dimensions (Formatting, Content, Visuals, Citations).
  - r2 range: Formatting=.118, Content=.152, Visuals=.232, Citations=.225
  - All four regressions significant at p≤.001
  - Raw correlations (time-on-task × outcomes): Formatting r=.343, Content r=.390, Visuals r=.482, Citations r=.474

- **Finding 3 — Time-on-task mediates leaderboard → performance (H3 supported)**: Both Sobel tests and bootstrapped CIs confirmed mediation for all four outcomes. Confidence intervals excluded zero.
  - Bootstrapped point estimates for indirect effect: Formatting=0.680, Content=0.706, Visuals=0.839, Citations=1.664
  - Bootstrap 95% CIs (lower limits): Formatting=0.278, Content=0.287, Visuals=0.327, Citations=0.533
  - Bootstrap 95% CIs (upper limits): Formatting=0.674 (Citations), consistent significance across outcomes
  - Sobel z-statistics: Formatting=2.460 (p=.014), Content=2.515 (p=.012), Visuals=2.715 (p=.007), Citations=2.827 (p=.005)

- **Finding 4 — Direct effect of gamification on performance was negligible**: Gamification condition correlated with outcomes at r=-.032 to .167, none significant (p>.05). The entire effect flowed through the behavioral mediator. This is a critical design implication: gamification that doesn't change behavior will appear to fail.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Leaderboard → time-on-task**: b=29.61 interactions, r=.33, r2=.12, t(84)=3.40, p=.001
- **Time-on-task → Formatting**: r=.343, r2=.118, p=.001
- **Time-on-task → Content**: r=.390, r2=.152, p<.001
- **Time-on-task → Visuals**: r=.482, r2=.232, p<.001
- **Time-on-task → Citations**: r=.474, r2=.225, p<.001
- **Gamification condition → Formatting** (direct): r=-.032, n.s.
- **Gamification condition → Content** (direct): r=.063, n.s.
- **Gamification condition → Visuals** (direct): r=.167, n.s.
- **Gamification condition → Citations** (direct): r=.006, n.s.
- **Inter-rater reliability (ICC 2,2)**: Formatting=.862, Content=.896, Visuals=.933, Citations=.979

No heterogeneity stats (single-study experiment, not meta-analysis).

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — Behavior change as the causal path**: Leaderboards work by changing what learners *do*, not by directly transmitting knowledge. The path is: game elements → behavioral change → learning outcome. Gamification that targets learning directly (bypassing behavioral change) is theorized to be less effective and potentially harmful.

- **Mechanism — Three game elements in leaderboards**:
  - *Conflict/challenge*: Competition motivates effort when competitors are roughly equal in ability. If ability differentials are large, lower-performing students disengage; higher-performing students lose challenge. Design implication: leaderboard criteria should be effort-contingent, not ability-contingent.
  - *Rules/goals*: Leaderboard tasks must meet SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound) to activate goal-setting theory mechanisms. Non-SMART leaderboard goals reduce motivational effect.
  - *Assessment*: Formal tracking and visible ranking creates social meaning that reinforces the other two elements.

- **Moderator — Design of leaderboard criteria**: Leaderboards targeting behaviors where effort = outcome (e.g., number of reads, edits) are more effective than leaderboards targeting quality judgments where prior ability confounds performance.

- **Moderator — Cultural context (stated, untested)**: U.S. student sample may be more receptive to competitive formats than students from less individually competitive cultures.

No formal moderation analyses were conducted.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Two leaderboards added to a wiki-based semester-long research project. Leaderboard 1: 10 action goals (first/second/third to complete each task, with name visible to all). Leaderboard 2: weekly paired head-to-head comparison, TA-judged, with weekly winner highlighted in bold.
- **Control**: Identical wiki without leaderboards. Students were blind to the existence of two conditions.
- **Follow-up period**: One full semester (approximately 15 weeks)
- **Leaderboard stakes**: No direct grade impact — leaderboard performance was entirely decoupled from grades. A student could earn a top grade without appearing on the leaderboard.
- **Attrition**: 109 enrolled → 86 consented (79% consent rate). Chi-square confirmed no differential consent by condition.
- **Blinding**: Students were blind to condition; all inter-condition communication tools disabled.
- **Outcome measurement**: Wiki edit count (objective, automated) as time-on-task proxy. Academic performance rated blind by both authors (ICC>.86 for all dimensions).

## Limitations

- **Time-on-task proxy**: Edit count captures saves, not actual time or effort quality. A student could make many trivial edits or few high-quality ones. True time investment is unmeasured.
- **Single behavioral pathway tested**: Other mediators (engagement, motivation, self-efficacy, user experience) were not measured. Time-on-task may not generalize as the relevant mediator to other gamification contexts.
- **Student sample only**: Participants are undergraduates in a single course at one university. Motivation structure may differ for employees in mandatory vs. voluntary training.
- **U.S. cultural context**: Competitive leaderboard framing may not generalize to collectivist cultures where public competition is aversive.
- **No no-effect condition explored**: Study confirms the mediating process works; it does not identify boundary conditions where leaderboards fail or backfire. The authors note in the conclusion that leaderboards are not universally appropriate.
- **Small n**: n=86 total; n=42/44 per cell. Modest statistical power for detecting small effects or interactions.
- **Single instructor, single course**: One instructor's course is the setting. Instructor enthusiasm and course structure are potential confounds not isolated.
- **No follow-up measurement**: Learning outcomes measured at end of semester only; no retention test to assess durability.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University undergraduates (online/distance learners), upper-division, diverse majors, mean age 32.4 (skews older than typical undergrad)
- **Relevance to consumer app/education product design**: Medium-High. The leaderboard mechanism and mediation model transfer directly to EdTech product design. The competitive framing is structurally identical to leaderboards in consumer apps. Age range (20–58, mean 32.4) makes this closer to adult learners than typical undergraduate samples.
- **Red flags**: None. No clinical population, no addiction-adjacent behavior, no compulsive disorder.

## Conclusions

"We have provided a rigorous empirical test of the mediational process of the theory of gamified learning. Gamification can be used successfully to affect targeted learning-related behaviors, and these behaviors are linked with improved learning outcomes."

The core implication: leaderboards caused +29.61 interactions (r=.33), that behavioral increase caused better grades across all four performance dimensions, and the effect was entirely mediated by the behavior change — the direct gamification-to-grades path was statistically zero. This validates the theory that gamification works through behavioral modification, not through some direct motivational boost to performance.

The authors explicitly caution: "This article should not be interpreted as a general recommendation to add leaderboards whenever student motivation is low. Instead, processes that could improve learning (such as increased time-on-task) must be identified, and those processes must be targeted by gamification interventions in order to affect learning indirectly."

## Named Frameworks

- **Theory of Gamified Learning (Landers, in press at time of study)**
  - Components: Mediating process (gamification → behavior/attitude change → outcome) + Moderating process (gamification → behavior/attitude change → strengthens instruction-outcome relationship)
  - Research alignment: Mediating process confirmed experimentally in this study. Moderating process not tested here.

- **Goal-Setting Theory (Locke & Latham, 1990)**
  - Applied via SMART criteria for leaderboard goal design
  - Research alignment: Rules/goals game element → SMART goals → increased effort → time-on-task

## Extraction Notes

This is a high-value paper for the guidebook for two reasons:

1. **Methodological rigor is unusually high for gamification research**: True random assignment, field setting, student blind to condition, objective behavioral measure, blind outcome coding with ICC>.86. The causal claim (leaderboards caused behavioral change) is well-supported.

2. **The mediation finding is the key insight**: The direct gamification-to-performance correlation was near zero (r=-.032 to .167). Without the mediation analysis, a researcher would conclude leaderboards don't work. The lesson for product designers: measure the behavior you're trying to change, not just the downstream outcome. If the behavior doesn't change, the mechanic is failing at step one.

Caution: Effect sizes are modest (r=.33 for the main effect), sample is small (n=86), and the setting is a single online course at one university. The paper establishes the mechanism conceptually more than it establishes the magnitude of effect for generalizable use.
