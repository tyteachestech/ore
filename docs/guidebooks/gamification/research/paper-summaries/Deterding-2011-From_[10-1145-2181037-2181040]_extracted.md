# Measuring how motivation affects information quality assessment: A gamification approach

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Pozenel, M., Zrnec, A., & Lavbic, D. (2022)
- **Study Type**: Quasi-experimental online study (single-condition; no separate control group; comparison made between motivation quartiles derived from in-game performance)
- **Sample**: n=1,062 valid responses (from 1,225 initial participants; exclusions for incomplete responses and implausible completion times); 30.7% female, 69.3% male; ages 15–69, median 20 years; 57.6% undergraduate students, 42.4% non-students
- **Context**: Web-based gamified survey on information quality (IQ) assessment; recruitment via direct contact, email, and social media; data collected April 2015–March 2020
- **DOI**: 10.1371/journal.pone.0274811
- **PMID**: Not available (PLoS ONE open access)

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Motivation improves inter-rater agreement overall**: The composite IQ measure (CIQ, average across 4 dimensions) increased from ICC=0.29 (lowest motivation quartile, Q4) to ICC=0.56 (highest motivation quartile, Q1) — an increase of 0.27. According to Koo & Li (2016) thresholds, this moves the result from "poor" to "moderate" agreement.
  - Effect reported as ICC(2,1); no standardized d or SMD provided.
  - Note: Motivation was operationalized via game points earned — a behavioral proxy, not a validated motivation scale.

- **Finding 2 — Completeness is most reliably assessed**: ICC for completeness improved from 0.51 (Q4, "moderate") to 0.77 (Q1, "good"), an increase of 0.26. This was attributed to the conceptual clarity of the dimension — raters could identify whether information uniquely specified a location.
  - ICC Q4=0.51, Q3=0.67, Q2=0.74, Q1=0.77

- **Finding 3 — Accuracy shows the largest gain from even slight motivation increase**: Accuracy ICC jumped from 0.31 (Q4, "poor") to 0.56 (Q3) with just a modest motivation increase — the biggest Q4-to-Q3 jump of any dimension. Final ICC for highest motivation group: 0.61.
  - ICC Q4=0.31, Q3=0.56, Q2=0.57, Q1=0.61
  - ICC increase from Q4 to Q1: +0.30

- **Finding 4 — Objectivity remains difficult to assess consistently**: ICC for objectivity improved from 0.15 (Q4) to 0.41 (Q1), an increase of 0.26 — fair agreement even for the most motivated group. Scientific jargon in hints contributed to poor inter-rater reliability.
  - ICC Q4=0.15, Q3=0.33, Q2=0.38, Q1=0.41

- **Finding 5 — Representation is the hardest dimension**: ICC for representation improved from only 0.10 (Q4) to 0.20 (Q1), an increase of 0.10. Even the most motivated participants remained in the "poor" agreement zone. The task (judging consistency of temperature units across hint formats) was described as the most cognitively demanding.
  - ICC Q4=0.10, Q3=0.15, Q2=0.22, Q1=0.20
  - Note: Q1 (most motivated) slightly underperformed Q2 on representation — the only exception to the monotonic trend.

- **Finding 6 — Results exceed all prior IQ inter-rater agreement studies**: The Q1 group's ICC values on all dimensions surpassed those reported in Arazy & Kopak (2011) and Fidler & Lavbic (2017), the closest prior comparator studies.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism 1 — Motivation increases cognitive effort applied to rating tasks**: Higher-motivated participants (as proxied by game score) invested more effort in evaluating hint quality, producing more internally consistent ratings and higher inter-rater agreement. The gamification system (points, levels, leaderboard) was designed to elicit both intrinsic motivation (play, challenge) and extrinsic motivation (hall of fame recognition).

- **Mechanism 2 — Gamification mediates motivation via SDT needs**: The system aimed to satisfy Self-Determination Theory needs for competence (scoring high), autonomy (voluntary participation, self-paced), and social relatedness (leaderboard comparison). The authors hypothesize these SDT needs, when fulfilled, increased intrinsic motivation and consequently assessment quality.

- **Moderator 1 — IQ dimension type**: Dimension type strongly moderates how much motivation helps. Completeness benefits most and achieves good agreement; representation benefits least and remains in poor agreement even for the most motivated. Likely mechanism: completeness has clearer objective criteria; representation (unit consistency) is more ambiguous and cognitively demanding.

- **Moderator 2 — Task cognitive load**: The harder the judgment task (representation > objectivity > accuracy > completeness in cognitive demand), the less motivation can compensate. A ceiling on motivation's impact exists for highly ambiguous tasks.

- **Moderator 3 — Domain familiarity (implicit)**: The use of scientific terminology in objectivity hints left some participants unable to evaluate quality regardless of motivation level, suggesting domain knowledge is a boundary condition.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: A web-based gamified application in which participants navigated a "rescue a baby bird" narrative across 4 levels (one per IQ dimension: completeness, accuracy, representation, objectivity). Points were awarded for finding correct objects in minimum attempts. Game elements: points, levels, leaderboard, progress bars.
- **Motivation proxy**: Total game score (points earned). Participants divided post-hoc into quartiles Q1–Q4 by score; Q1 = highest scorers = most motivated.
- **Control/Comparison**: No separate control group. Within-study comparison across performance quartiles (quasi-experimental design).
- **IQ measurement**: Each participant rated 24 hints on 7-point Likert scales (6 hints per 4 dimensions), once before attempting to find the target object, with optional recalibration after finding it.
- **Follow-up period**: None; cross-sectional single-session study.
- **Attrition**: 163 participants excluded (13.3%): incomplete responses or implausible completion times (<5 min or >3 hours).
- **Blinding**: Participants were not aware of the research goal; they were instructed only to achieve the highest score.
- **Data collection period**: April 2015–March 2020 (5 years).
- **No randomization**: Participants self-selected; distribution into quartiles was based on their in-game performance, not random assignment.

## Limitations

- **No control group**: Without a non-gamified baseline condition, it is impossible to determine whether the gamification specifically caused the motivation improvement vs. other factors (e.g., the gamified context selecting for inherently more motivated participants).
- **Motivation operationalization is indirect**: Game score is a behavioral proxy for motivation, not a validated psychological measure. High scorers may simply have domain knowledge advantages or greater familiarity with the task format.
- **Self-selection into high motivation**: Participants who scored high may be inherently more careful, analytical, or engaged — independently of gamification. Gamification may have attracted this group rather than created it.
- **Young, male, student-skewed sample**: Median age 20, 69.3% male, 57.6% students — limits generalizability to broader adult populations.
- **Single problem domain**: All 4 game levels used one narrative context (bird rescue). Results may not transfer to other domains.
- **Short text format only**: Hints were paragraph-length, not full documents. Effects may differ with longer, more complex source material.
- **No direct gamification vs. no-gamification comparison**: Authors reference prior studies (Arazy & Kopak 2011) as informal comparators, but those studies used different rater populations, materials, and protocols.
- **No longitudinal follow-up**: Whether motivation effects persist across multiple sessions is untested.
- **No moderator analysis on demographic variables**: Age, gender, and education level were collected but moderating effects on ICC were not formally tested.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — primarily undergraduate students (57.6%) and young adults, median age 20, with broader community recruitment. Not a general adult population by age distribution.
- **Relevance to gamification guidebook**: Medium-low as a primary evidence source. The paper is primarily an IQ measurement study that uses gamification instrumentally, not a study of gamification per se. The research question is about information quality inter-rater reliability, not about gamification design outcomes.
- **Relevance as supporting evidence**: Moderate. It provides a concrete behavioral demonstration that motivational game mechanics (points, levels, leaderboards) affect effort investment in a cognitive rating task — supporting the mechanism-chain claim that gamification → motivation → behavioral quality improvement.
- **Red flags**: Narrow age distribution; student-heavy; single domain; no baseline.

## Conclusions

The study demonstrates a positive correlation between gamification-induced motivation (proxied by in-game score) and inter-rater reliability in information quality assessment, with overall ICC improving from 0.29 (poor) to 0.56 (moderate) — an increase of 0.27. The effect was consistent across most IQ dimensions, with completeness benefiting most and representation benefiting least. The authors conclude that motivation is a "vital cue" in IQ assessment and recommend that future IQ research incorporate gamification as a motivational tool. They note that thoughtful gamification design (improving the assessment process) outperforms "rhetorical gamification" (adding surface-level game elements).

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Cognitive rating/evaluation tasks (not just IQ assessment) where rater effort is the primary constraint on agreement quality; contexts where gamification can plausibly address an effort-investment bottleneck
- **Does NOT apply to**: Tasks where disagreement stems from genuine conceptual ambiguity (see: representation dimension remaining at poor ICC even for most motivated group); tasks requiring deep domain expertise; populations substantially older or more diverse than the median 20-year-old student sample
- **Unknown/untested**: Whether gamification itself caused the motivation difference vs. self-selection of inherently more careful participants; whether effects persist beyond a single session; whether results transfer to non-student, older, or more diverse populations; which specific game element(s) drove the motivation effect
