# The Winner Takes It All – Effects of Leaderboard-Based Feedback on Cognitive Performance and Motivation

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Pickal, A.J., Stadler, M., Sailer, M., Bai, S., Ninaus, M., Greiff, S., Becker, N., & Koch, M., 2026
- **Study Type**: Experimental (preregistered RCT with five conditions)
- **Sample**: n=427, German university students (332 female, 91 male, 4 diverse); mean age 21.64 years (SD=4.49)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.lindif.2025.102836
- **PMID**: unknown

## Key Findings

- **Leaderboard position and trend significantly predict intrinsic motivation**: ANOVA revealed significant condition effects on interest/enjoyment (F4,340=3.18, p=0.014, η²=0.04) and perceived competence (F4,340=17.12, p<0.001, η²=0.17). Effect on perceived competence was notably larger than on enjoyment.

- **Rank order of conditions for interest/enjoyment**: Higher start–upward shift (M=3.37, SD=1.15) > Higher start–downward shift (M=3.08, SD=1.03) > No feedback (M=3.00, SD=0.96) > Lower start–upward shift (M=2.83, SD=0.97) > Lower start–downward shift (M=2.57, SD=0.97).

- **Rank order for perceived competence**: Higher start–upward shift (M=3.62, SD=0.83) > No feedback (M=3.12, SD=0.99) > Higher start–downward shift (M=2.82, SD=0.85) > Lower start–upward shift (M=2.78, SD=0.87) > Lower start–downward shift (M=2.45, SD=0.96).

- **Negative feedback worse than no feedback**: The "lower start–downward shift" condition scored below the no-feedback control on both motivation outcomes. The "higher start–downward shift" and "lower start–upward shift" conditions also fell below no-feedback on perceived competence. Only the purely positive condition (higher start + upward shift) consistently exceeded the control.

- **No substantial effect on cognitive performance**: All groups improved accuracy (T1 M=68.10 → T4 M=82.59) and speed (T1 M=71.74 → T4 M=86.53) over time due to practice effects. Upward-shift conditions showed statistically significant but practically negligible additional gains compared to no-feedback (small marginal R²=0.08–0.09 for the full fixed-effect model). Authors conclude leaderboard conditions had no meaningful differential impact on performance.

- **Manipulation check confirmed**: Participants perceived their performance and improvement differently across conditions in line with assigned leaderboards, validating that fictitious feedback was accepted as real. Perceived performance: Higher start–upward shift M=3.53, Lower start–downward shift M=2.51 (F4,339=11.22, p<0.001, η²=0.12). Perceived improvement: Upward shifts substantially outperformed downward shifts (F4,339=22.73, p<0.001, η²=0.21).

- **Achievement motives did not moderate**: Hope for success and fear of failure showed no significant moderation of condition effects on either cognitive performance or intrinsic motivation outcomes. Inclusion of these covariates did not increase model R² beyond ~0.08–0.09.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Interest/enjoyment (condition main effect)**: F4,340=3.18, p=0.014, η²=0.04 (small)
- **Perceived competence (condition main effect)**: F4,340=17.12, p<0.001, η²=0.17 (medium-large)
- **Manipulation check – perceived performance**: F4,339=11.22, p<0.001, η²=0.12
- **Manipulation check – perceived improvement**: F4,339=22.73, p<0.001, η²=0.21
- **Accuracy over time (fixed effects only)**: marginal R²=0.08
- **Speed over time (fixed effects only)**: marginal R²=0.08–0.09
- **Achievement motives on motivation (moderation, n.s.)**: hope for success η²=0.02, fear of failure η²=0.02; both non-significant

Note: No Cohen's d or pairwise post-hoc effect sizes reported in text. Η² values are the primary effect size metric used throughout.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism – competence need satisfaction**: Higher leaderboard positions support feelings of competence (per SDT), leading to higher intrinsic motivation. Lower positions threaten perceived competence, reducing motivation below even no-feedback baseline.
- **Mechanism – social comparison**: Leaderboards trigger upward vs. downward social comparisons. Downward comparisons (higher-ranked users) boost competence experiences; upward comparisons (lower-ranked users) decrease them.
- **Mechanism – Pygmalion Effect**: Fictitious positive feedback internalized as reflecting actual ability, producing motivational boost even without any real performance signal.
- **Mechanism – autonomy threat**: Negative feedback perceived as controlling, undermining autonomy; no-feedback condition allows users to maintain sense of control over their learning process.
- **Moderator – achievement motives (hope for success, fear of failure)**: Tested as exploratory moderators. No significant moderation found for intrinsic motivation outcomes. One small negative interaction found for hope for success × lower start–upward shift on speed (t(414)=−2.12, p=0.035) but negligible practical import (model R² unchanged).
- **Moderator – individual competitiveness**: Not tested in this study; prior literature shows mixed results.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Five between-subjects conditions using a preregistered 2×2+1 design. Four conditions crossed starting position (higher=6th of 30; lower=25th of 30) × trend (upward or downward shift across 4 rounds) plus a no-feedback control. Leaderboards were static images, entirely fictitious (not tied to actual performance).
- **Task**: Attention Swiping Task (AST) — flower-sorting sustained attention task, 4 rounds × 3 minutes each. Measures: accuracy (hits minus misses minus false alarms) and speed (total correct).
- **Motivation measure**: Short Intrinsic Motivation Scale (KIM; Wilde et al., 2009): interest/enjoyment (ω=0.85) and perceived competence (ω=0.83) subscales, measured at posttest only.
- **Control condition**: n=90 participants received no leaderboard feedback between rounds.
- **Data collection**: Mixed in-class (first cohort) and online via Zoom (subsequent cohorts due to COVID-19). Study duration ~20 minutes.
- **Groups**: Higher start–upward shift n=86; Higher start–downward shift n=81; Lower start–upward shift n=75; Lower start–downward shift n=95; No feedback n=90.
- **Blinding**: Participants not informed of leaderboard manipulation; debriefing occurred after data collection.

## Limitations

- **Student-only sample, predominantly female** (332/427 = 78%): Limits generalizability, especially since women tend to be less competitive than men; effects may be attenuated relative to more gender-balanced or male-heavy samples.
- **Short task duration (~20 min)**: Cannot determine whether motivational or performance effects persist over longer learning sessions or in real educational contexts.
- **Fictitious feedback only**: No condition with accurate, performance-based leaderboard feedback was included. Cannot compare real vs. fictitious feedback effects.
- **Single-session, posttest-only motivation measure**: Intrinsic motivation was measured only at end of study — no within-session tracking, and no longitudinal follow-up.
- **Conflict of interest risk**: Samuel Greiff is Editor-in-Chief of the publishing journal; Michael Sailer is Special Issue Editor; both are co-authors. Authors note neither was involved in peer review, but structural conflict exists.
- **Narrow task type**: The AST flower-swiping task has no domain relevance, which may suppress achievement motive effects and reduce ecological validity for educational applications.
- **No actual performance condition**: Confounds specific to fictitious-vs-real leaderboard design cannot be resolved.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (German; mean age 21.6; 78% female)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium — competition and rank feedback are central to gamified productivity apps; findings on positive-only framing are directly applicable to product design decisions
- **Red flags**: Student-only sample limits generalization to working adults; near-homogeneous gender skews competitiveness-related effects

## Conclusions

Authors conclude that leaderboard feedback matters primarily for motivation, not performance. Positive feedback (high position, upward trend) reliably boosts intrinsic motivation, while negative feedback (low position, downward trend) depresses motivation below even the no-feedback baseline. The authors recommend selective use of leaderboards in educational settings, with framing matched to learner performance level: "positive feedback (e.g., high initial positions or upward trends) can be motivating...negative or downward-shifting feedback should be avoided unless it is paired with opportunities for recovery or improvement. In some cases, withholding comparative feedback altogether may be more beneficial than providing demotivating comparisons."

## Extraction Notes

This is a methodologically clean, preregistered experiment with a meaningful manipulation check — a relative rarity in gamification research. The key design choice (using fictitious leaderboards) isolates leaderboard feedback from actual performance, which is theoretically useful but limits external validity.

The most actionable finding for NotionRPG: the no-feedback control outperformed the "lower start–downward shift" condition on both motivation outcomes. This is direct evidence that poorly framed comparative feedback is worse than no feedback. This aligns with Holllig 2020 and Bai 2021 regarding the differential effects of rank position.

The large η² for perceived competence (0.17) vs. the small η² for enjoyment (0.04) suggests that rank feedback primarily operates through competence perceptions, not hedonic enjoyment — a distinction relevant for SDT-informed design.

The conflict of interest (journal editor as co-author) warrants a brief note but doesn't invalidate the findings given the preregistration and open data commitment.
