# Defining Gamification: A Service Marketing Perspective

- **Study Type**: Conference paper — theoretical framework / conceptual argument (no empirical data)
- **Sample**: N/A — theoretical paper

- **PMID**: Not available
- **Citations at time of file access**: 754 (high-impact foundational paper in the gamification literature)

## Key Findings

This paper contains no empirical findings. It is a theoretical framework paper that proposes a new definition of gamification grounded in service marketing theory. All claims are conceptual arguments, not empirical results.

- **Core argument — the systemic definition is insufficient**: The prevailing definition at the time (Deterding et al., 2011: "use of game design elements in non-game contexts") adopts a purely systemic view. The authors argue this is insufficient because: (a) no set of game elements is unique to games, and (b) elements alone do not guarantee gameful experiences. Systemic definitions cannot explain when gamification has actually occurred.

- **Proposed definition**: "Gamification refers to: a process of enhancing a service with affordances for gameful experiences in order to support user's overall value creation." This shifts emphasis from methods (elements used) to goals (experiences created).

- **Value is always in use, not in elements**: Drawing on service-dominant (S-D) logic (Vargo & Lusch, 2004), the authors argue that gamification value is not embedded in game mechanics — it is created only when a user subjectively experiences gameful play. Designers make value propositions; users determine value outcomes.

- **Gamefulness as the unique experiential condition**: The authors argue "gamefulness" — an experiential state unique to games — is what distinguishes a game from a non-game. Because gamefulness is subjective, the game/non-game boundary is determined by the player's perception, not by the designer's intent. This dissolves the Deterding et al. requirement that only non-game contexts can be gamified.

- **Games as service systems**: Using service marketing vocabulary, games are reframed as service systems co-produced by developers (who create rules, design patterns, visuals) and players (who create value each time they play). The core service of a game is to provide hedonic, challenging, and suspenseful experiences — or gameful experiences.

- **Gamification can fail**: The definition explicitly does not imply that gamification must succeed. A gamified service can only attempt to support gameful experiences; whether it does is determined by the user.

- **Four gamification providers**: Based on who provides the enhancing service layer, four provider types exist: (1) the core service provider, (2) a third-party provider, (3) the customer themselves, (4) another customer. These can act alone or in combination.

- **Ethics flag — the marketing trap**: If gamification is designed solely to increase marketing metrics (clicks, retention) rather than gameful experiences, designers risk a conflicting situation: reducing free choice conflicts with intrinsic motivation, which is definitionally central to gameful experience.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Primary mechanism proposed — subjective value creation:**
- Game elements (systemic layer) → affordances for gameful experience → user subjectively determines value (experiential layer) → value-in-use created or not
- Designers operate only on the first arrow; users control the second

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Enabling services** (required to offer the core service) vs. **enhancing services** (support the core service and increase its value or differentiate it) — gamification is an enhancing service layered onto a core service
- **Core service vs. enhancing service**: The distinction is made by the gamification provider before the fact; after the fact, the customer's subjective perception is decisive

**Moderator: User subjectivity**
- The same gamified system may produce gameful experiences for one user and not another — due to differences in skill, preference, or context
- No controllable moderator; designers can only increase the probability of gameful experience through affordance design

**Moderator: Alignment of gamification goal with gameful experience**
- Gamification aimed at marketing metrics alone (clicks, retention) risks undermining the intrinsic, voluntary nature of gameful experience
- Reducing free choice moves the design away from what produces gameful experience

## Study Design Details

Not applicable — theoretical/conceptual paper. No intervention, no control condition, no follow-up period, no participants.

**Paper structure**: Reviews game studies definitions of games → introduces service marketing concepts (service, service system, service package, value-in-use, S-D logic) → maps games onto service marketing framework → proposes new definition → identifies four gamification provider types → discusses implications.

**Illustrative examples used** (not empirical case studies):
- Foursquare badges at restaurants/cafes → gamifies the venue's service, not Foursquare itself
- LinkedIn profile completion progress bar → psychological progress bias as enhancing service
- Loyalty stamp card at dry cleaner → enhancing service invoking progress-related biases
- Drinking game during a hockey match → customer-provided gamification of the sports bar experience
- Gym + Heya Heya fitness app → third-party gamification of gym experience
- Geocaching: only gamification if the park offers the geocaching game; not gamification if geocaching brings customers to the park (core/enhancing distinction)

## Limitations

- **No empirical data**: The entire argument is theoretical. The proposed definition has not been tested for explanatory or predictive validity.
- **"Gamefulness" left undefined**: The paper explicitly states that defining "gamefulness" is outside its scope — yet the entire proposed definition hinges on what counts as a gameful experience. This is the definition's central load-bearing term, and it is left unresolved.
- **Circular dependency**: The definition requires distinguishing the "core service" from the "enhancing service," but the paper acknowledges this distinction is theoretically determined by customer perception after the fact — making it practically difficult to apply in advance.
- **No empirical test of provider taxonomy**: The four-provider taxonomy is a conceptual classification, not one derived from observation or tested against cases.
- **Boundary with Deterding et al. partly overstated**: The paper argues Deterding's systemic approach is incompatible with service logic, but Deterding et al. (2011) explicitly discuss experiential aspects of games. The incompatibility is real but less total than presented.
- **Short conference paper format**: 6 pages. The argument is compressed and several key concepts (servicescape expansion, S-D logic implications for future research) are raised but not developed.
- **No conflict of interest disclosure**: Standard for 2012 conference proceedings; noting the absence rather than presence of problematic funding.

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that game study literature and service marketing literature are "for large parts complementary" but that the prevailing Deterding et al. definition adopts a systemic approach "incompatible with the understanding of value creation in service literature." Their proposed definition — gamification as enhancing a service with affordances for gameful experiences to support value creation — shifts focus from game element inventories to the experiential outcome. The paper identifies four possible gamification providers and suggests future research directions including customer loyalty programs as gamified services and expansion of the servicescape model.

## Named Frameworks

### Service Marketing Reframing of Gamification

**Core concepts imported from service marketing:**
| Concept | Definition | Gamification Application |
|---------|-----------|--------------------------|
| **Value-in-use** | Value is created only when the customer uses the service, not embedded in the product | Game elements have no intrinsic value — gameful experience is the value, determined by user |
| **Service system** | Arrangements of resources connected by value propositions | Games = service systems co-produced by developer and player |
| **Service package** | Core service + enabling services + enhancing services | Gamification = enhancing service layered on a core service |
| **Co-production** | Customer always participates in producing the service | Player co-produces the game experience; value creation is shared |
| **Value proposition** | Providers offer propositions; value is determined by the beneficiary phenomenologically | Gamification can only propose gameful experiences, not guarantee them |

**Four gamification provider types:**
| Provider | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Core service provider | The business being gamified provides the gamification itself | Dry cleaner loyalty stamp card |
| Third-party provider | External platform provides the gamification layer | Foursquare gamifying restaurants |
| Customer (self) | The customer gamifies their own experience | Self-imposed drinking game at sports bar |
| Another customer | A different customer provides the gamification for others | Foursquare user tips left for other waiting customers |

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Any service context (consumer app, physical space, e-commerce, education, entertainment) where a gamification layer is being added to a core offering; most useful for product designers thinking about whether their gamification is actually producing engagement vs. just deploying game element syntax
- **Does NOT apply to**: Clinical or therapeutic contexts where the user is not a voluntary consumer; situations where the goal is compliance (not gameful experience)
- **Unknown/untested**: Whether the service-marketing reframing produces better or worse design outcomes than element-based definitions (Deterding); how to operationalize "affordances for gameful experiences" in practice; whether the four provider types map cleanly onto real-world implementations; how to measure gameful experience as a design success criterion

## Extraction Notes

This is a foundational definitional/conceptual paper, not an empirical study. Its value to the guidebook is significant despite having no effect sizes:

1. **Definitional provenance**: This paper is the origin point of the "affordances for gameful experiences" definition of gamification, which is widely cited as a corrective to the Deterding et al. element-based definition. With 754 citations, it is one of the most-cited gamification papers. Any chapter covering "what is gamification" should acknowledge this framework.

2. **Experience-first vs. element-first framing**: The core argument — that gamification is about what the user experiences, not what elements the designer deploys — is practically important for the guidebook. It supports the position that "adding points and badges" is not gamification if users don't experience it as gameful. This is a useful corrective against surface-level gamification.

3. **Value-in-use as design principle**: The S-D logic import (designers make value propositions; users determine value) is an underused framing in gamification discourse. It naturalizes the finding that identical mechanics produce wildly different engagement outcomes across users — not because the mechanics are inconsistent but because the user determines value.

4. **Ethics thread**: The paper's note about the "marketing trap" — that metric-optimized gamification (clicks, retention) conflicts with the intrinsic motivation basis of gameful experience — is an early version of the engagement-vs.-exploitation tension that the guidebook needs to address throughout.

5. **Limitation for evidence grading**: Zero empirical support; "gamefulness" is explicitly left undefined; the core/enhancing service distinction is practically hard to apply. Use this paper for definitional vocabulary and the experience-first argument. Do not use it for any quantitative claims about gamification effectiveness.

6. **Short paper caveat**: This is a 6-page conference proceedings paper, not a journal article. It reads as a conceptual sketch that was developed further in Huotari & Hamari's 2017 journal article (if available in the registry). Check the registry for a more developed version.
