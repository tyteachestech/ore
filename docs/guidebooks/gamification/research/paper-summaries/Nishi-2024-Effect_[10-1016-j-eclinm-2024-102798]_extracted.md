# Effect of Digital Health Applications with or without Gamification on Physical Activity and Cardiometabolic Risk Factors

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Nishi, S.K. et al., 2024
- **Study Type**: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of RCTs
- **Sample**: k=36 trials, 49 trial comparisons, n=10,079 adults; median trial size 59–195 participants depending on outcome
- **Population**: Adults (≥18 years) across all health backgrounds; predominantly free from disease but mix includes overweight/obese, CVD, and T2DM participants; mostly women (~55–76% female), middle-aged (median age 36–55 years across outcome subsets); predominantly US-based (40%)
- **Comparison**: Gamified health app vs. matched non-gamified version of the same app (active control)
- **Search**: MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library through May 21, 2024
- **Intervention minimum**: ≥8 weeks
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102798
- **PMID**: Not recorded in source file; published in eClinicalMedicine (The Lancet group), October 2024
- **Funding**: None (all agency-funded or unfunded; no industry funding for this review itself)

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Steps/day (primary outcome)**: Gamification in apps produced a statistically significant but trivially-sized increase in daily step count compared to non-gamified apps.
  - Effect: MD = +489 steps/day (95% CI: 64, 914); p=0.024
  - k=34 trial comparisons, n=5,119
  - Heterogeneity: I2=93.8%, PQ<0.0001 (substantial)
  - GRADE: High certainty
  - Magnitude classification: Trivial (MID for steps = ±2,000 steps/day; effect is <1 MID)

- **Finding 2 — Body fat percentage**: Small important reduction in body fat.
  - Effect: MD = −1.92% (95% CI: −2.71, −1.14); p<0.0001
  - k=3 trial comparisons, n=230
  - Heterogeneity: I2=0%, PQ=0.66 (no heterogeneity)
  - GRADE: High certainty (downgraded for indirectness due to small k)
  - Magnitude: Small important (≥1 MID; MID = ±2%)

- **Finding 3 — BMI**: Trivial but significant reduction.
  - Effect: MD = −0.28 kg/m2 (95% CI: −0.44, −0.12); p=0.00068
  - k=11 trial comparisons, n=1,880
  - Heterogeneity: I2=35.8%, PQ=0.27 (no substantial heterogeneity)
  - GRADE: Moderate certainty (downgraded for imprecision)
  - Magnitude: Trivial (<1 MID; MID = ±0.4 kg/m2)

- **Finding 4 — Body weight**: Trivial but significant reduction.
  - Effect: MD = −0.70 kg (95% CI: −1.18, −0.22); p=0.0043
  - k=17 trial comparisons, n=6,088
  - Heterogeneity: I2=35.8%, PQ=0.071 (borderline)
  - GRADE: Moderate certainty (downgraded for imprecision)
  - Magnitude: Trivial (<1 MID; MID = ±1 kg)

- **Finding 5 — Waist circumference**: Small important reduction.
  - Effect: MD = −1.16 cm (95% CI: −1.93, −0.39); p=0.0032
  - k=8 trial comparisons, n=802
  - Heterogeneity: I2=44.7%, PQ=0.081 (borderline)
  - GRADE: Moderate certainty
  - Magnitude: Small important (≥1 MID; MID = ±1 cm)

- **Finding 6 — MVPA**: No significant effect.
  - k=15 trial comparisons, n=195 (median, range 36–301)
  - GRADE: Low certainty (downgraded for imprecision and indirectness)

- **Finding 7 — No effect on cardiometabolic markers**: No significant effects on LDL-C, non-HDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, fasting glucose, total energy, total sugars, sodium, or saturated fat intake.

- **Finding 8 — Duration durability for steps**: Subgroup analysis found no significant effect modification by follow-up duration for steps — studies ≥6 months showed comparable effects to studies <6 months. This is notable because it argues against a simple novelty effect explaining the step count benefit.

**Heterogeneity note**: Steps showed substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I2=93.8%). The Francis et al. trial, when removed, explained much of this heterogeneity without affecting significance. Sensitivity analyses replacing actigraph and Fitbit step measurements did not alter conclusions.

**Publication bias**: Egger's and Begg's tests showed significant evidence of publication bias for MVPA, steps, and DBP. Trim-and-fill analysis did not change significance or magnitude for any of these, suggesting small-study effects are present but do not materially inflate results.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism 1 — Intrinsic motivation via game elements**: Points, badges, leaderboards, and challenges are proposed to tap intrinsic motivation, making health tasks more engaging and enjoyable. This is offered as explanatory but is not directly tested in this meta-analysis.

- **Mechanism 2 — Goal setting and progress tracking**: Gamification provides mechanisms for breaking health goals into smaller achievable steps, enhancing self-efficacy. Explicitly aligned with established behaviour change techniques (BCTs per Michie et al. taxonomy).

- **Mechanism 3 — Social accountability**: Social gamification features (competitions, teams, leaderboards) foster community and accountability, proposed to increase sustained engagement.

- **Moderator 1 — Study purpose (MVPA only)**: Significant effect modification (P=0.028). Apps specifically targeting physical activity/mobility showed greater increases in MVPA than those with mixed purposes.

- **Moderator 2 — Funding source (MVPA and body weight)**: Significant effect modification for MVPA (P=0.028) and body weight (P=0.003). Agency-funded studies showed greater effects than industry-funded or mixed-funded studies.

- **Moderator 3 — BCT integration (BMI only)**: Significant effect modification (P=0.008). Counterintuitively, interventions that did NOT mention using a BCT framework showed greater BMI reductions.

- **Moderator 4 — Follow-up duration (BMI and body weight)**: Significant effect modification (BMI: P=0.036; body weight: P=0.039). Greater reductions in shorter follow-ups (<6 months) vs. longer (≥6 months). Continuous meta-regression also showed positive association between follow-up duration and BMI (P=0.038) and body weight (P=0.035).

- **Moderator 5 — Continent (BMI only)**: Significant effect modification (P=0.042). Studies from Europe and South America showed greater BMI decreases than US-based studies.

- **Moderator 6 — Duration and steps (null finding)**: Notably, follow-up duration did NOT significantly moderate steps. No novelty-effect fade detected for step count across study duration.

- **No significant moderators found for**: Sex, age (continuous), health status, gamification type, setting (community vs. professional), randomization method, or outcome measurement method (objective vs. self-report) for steps or most adiposity markers.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention definition**: Gamification = one or more of: goal setting, personalized feedback, badges, leaderboards, competitions/challenges, rewards, avatars, social teams, point systems, social support features. Multi-element gamification (M category) was most common (up to 85% of trials).
- **Control condition**: Equivalent app or digital tool WITHOUT gamification components. Most commonly: equivalent mobile app without gamification (56–100% across outcomes). This is a true active control design, not usual care — this is the methodologically strong feature.
- **Follow-up range**: 2 months to 32.5 months; median 3.0–12.0 months depending on outcome
- **Study design**: All parallel RCTs (100%)
- **Outcome measurement**: Steps and blood lipids objectively measured in 100% of trials for those outcomes; dietary assessments self-reported
- **Statistical model**: DerSimonian & Laird random-effects model (inverse variance method); fixed effects used when <5 comparisons available
- **Blinding note**: Digital interventions inherently cannot be double-blinded; measurement and outcome assessment methods vary

## Limitations

- **Substantial heterogeneity for steps**: I2=93.8% for the primary outcome. This means the pooled +489 steps/day estimate masks substantial variability across studies; some studies showed much larger or smaller effects.
- **Trivial magnitudes for primary outcomes**: Steps (+489 vs. MID of ±2,000) and BMI/weight reductions fall below clinical significance thresholds. Authors classify these as "trivial" per GRADE guidance.
- **Small k for several outcomes**: Body fat (k=3), waist-to-hip ratio (k=1), dietary markers (k=1–2). Fixed effects models used; no subgroup analyses possible for these outcomes.
- **Serious imprecision in 10/19 analyses**: MID crossed by CI, meaning clinically important benefit could not be confirmed nor harm ruled out for most outcomes.
- **Indirectness concerns**: Serious to very serious indirectness for 10/19 outcomes due to small k, clinical populations, or app interventions targeting intermediate outcomes.
- **Cannot isolate individual gamification elements**: Most trials used "multiple forms of gamification" (M category: up to 85%); impossible to determine which specific elements drive effects.
- **Majority of trials target physical activity/weight loss**: Limited evidence for other cardiometabolic outcomes (lipids, blood pressure, glycemia). Absence of effect may reflect absence of trials, not true null effect.
- **Publication bias detected**: Significant Egger's/Begg's for MVPA, steps, DBP; trim-and-fill did not change conclusions, but inflated positive results cannot be fully excluded.
- **Population skew**: Predominantly US-based (40%), female (55–76%), middle-aged (median ~50 years); generalizability to younger, male, or non-Western populations uncertain.
- **No data on engagement or attrition with gamification features**: How many users actually engaged with game elements is not captured; non-engagement would dilute effects.
- **Novelty effect caveat for adiposity**: For BMI and body weight (not steps), shorter follow-up outperformed longer follow-up, suggesting some time-dependent erosion of effect for body composition outcomes.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed — free of disease (majority), plus overweight/obese, CVD patients, T2DM patients; mostly female, middle-aged adults; predominantly US/European/East Asian settings
- **Relevance to casual behavior change (consumer app design)**: High — this is directly about gamification in consumer health apps; active control design isolates gamification feature effect
- **Red flags**: Clinical subpopulations (T2DM, CVD) are included in many trial subsets; effects may differ in healthy users vs. patients. The "professional setting" (recommended by HCP) represents ~31% of included trials, which is a different delivery context than self-initiated consumer app use.
- **Scope note**: This is the strongest available evidence base for gamification's additive value specifically within the health/fitness app context. The +489 steps/day figure is the most quotable single number in this literature.

## Conclusions

"Current evidence provides a good indication that gamification features in apps targeting physical activity or weight loss result in trivial improvements in steps per day, BMI, and body weight, and small important improvements in body fat and waist circumference, compared to non-gamified apps." Authors specifically recommend that recommendations to use an app for physical activity or weight loss should favor those with gamification features. The durable effect on step count (no attenuation at ≥6 months) is highlighted as a novel and important finding. Authors note that only 4% of existing health apps include gamification, representing an untapped design opportunity.

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Adult health app users (18+) targeting physical activity or weight loss; community and professional settings; iOS/Android mobile apps and mobile+web combined tools; most studied in Western contexts (USA primary, Europe, East Asia)
- **Applies most strongly to**: Step count as outcome — highest certainty evidence (GRADE: high), largest k (34 comparisons), no novelty attenuation
- **Applies with caution to**: BMI and body weight (effect present but trivial magnitude, potential fade at >6 months); adiposity markers (body fat, waist circumference — high certainty but very small k=3 and k=8)
- **Does NOT apply to (within health apps)**: Lipid profiles, blood pressure, glycemic control, dietary intake — no significant effects detected across all these outcomes
- **Does NOT apply to**: Children/adolescents (excluded from this review); non-health contexts; non-digital gamification; clinical populations as distinct from general adult population
- **Unknown/untested**: Effects of specific individual gamification elements (all studies bundled multiple types); effects in younger adults (<30) or older adults (>65); effects by gamification engagement level (non-use of game elements not captured); effects in lower-income or lower-health-literacy populations; effects beyond 32.5 months (maximum follow-up in included trials)
- **Inflation caveat**: Digital delivery is a known inflated domain (see `inflation_flag: digital-delivery`). However, the active control design (gamified vs. non-gamified app) removes the usual app-vs-no-app inflation — this comparison isolates the gamification increment specifically. The digital-delivery flag applies less strongly here than for app-vs-control comparisons.

## Extraction Notes

1. **Strongest available evidence for gamification in health apps**: This is the largest and most rigorous meta-analysis on this topic (k=36, N=10,079, active control design). Prior reviews had much smaller k (e.g., Mazeas 2022 had k=7 for the comparable steps analysis). This supersedes earlier estimates.

2. **Active control design is the key methodological strength**: Unlike most app studies that compare to usual care or no intervention, this review specifically required the control arm to be the same app WITHOUT gamification. This isolates the gamification feature effect rather than the app-as-a-whole effect. This is the correct comparison for a guidebook chapter asking "does gamification add value to an app?"

3. **The +489 steps/day figure needs careful framing**: It is statistically significant with high GRADE certainty, but classified as "trivial" against clinical thresholds (MID = ±2,000 steps/day). For a product designer, the question is not clinical significance but user behavior change — a reliable ~5% increase in daily walking from a design feature added to an existing app may be commercially meaningful even if clinically trivial.

4. **No novelty fade for steps is the most important novel finding**: The literature widely assumes gamification effects attenuate due to novelty. This is the first large meta-analysis to test this directly for health app gamification. The null moderation by follow-up duration for steps specifically challenges the novelty-fade assumption, at least for physical activity.

5. **Heterogeneity in steps (I2=93.8%) is a critical caveat**: The pooled estimate hides enormous variability. Some trials likely show no effect or negative effects; others show large effects. The +489 is a central tendency, not a reliable per-app prediction. Designers should not assume their specific gamification implementation will reliably produce this effect.

6. **Cross-reference with**: Mazeas-2022 (earlier, smaller physical activity gamification meta-analysis that Nishi supersedes); Sailer-2020 (learning context); Hydari-2019 (wearables gamification). Nishi is the primary citation for any claim about gamification in health/fitness apps.

7. **No funding conflicts for the review itself**: One author (CWCK) has extensive food industry relationships; JLS has broad industry ties. These are nutritional scientists whose COI disclosures are standard for the field but do not relate to the gamification interventions being evaluated.
