# Spacing Effects in Learning: A Temporal Ridgeline of Optimal Retention

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Cepeda, N.J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J.T., & Pashler, H., 2008
- **Study Type**: Experimental (Internet-based)
- **Sample**: n=1,354 (completed all three sessions), population=general adults from online research panel, mean age 34 years (SD=11, range=18-72), 72% female
- **DOI**: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Optimal gap increases final recall**: For a fixed amount of study time, the optimal gap, as compared with a zero-day gap, provided a 64% increase in final recall
  - Effect size: d=1.1
  - Sample: n=1,354

- **Optimal gap increases final recognition**: For a fixed amount of study time, the optimal gap, as compared with a zero-day gap, provided a 26% increase in final recognition
  - Effect size: d=1.5
  - Sample: n=1,354

- **Optimal gaps vary by retention interval (recall)**: For RIs of 7, 35, 70, and 350 days, the optimal gaps (of those included in the study) were 1, 11, 21, and 21 days, respectively, for recall

- **Optimal gaps vary by retention interval (recognition)**: For RIs of 7, 35, 70, and 350 days, the optimal gaps were 1, 7, 7, and 21 days, respectively, for recognition

- **Interpolated optimal gaps (recall)**: Approximately 3, 8, 12, and 27 days (corresponding to 43%, 23%, 17%, and 8% of the RIs, respectively)

- **Interpolated optimal gaps (recognition)**: Approximately 1.6, 7, 10, and 25 days (24%, 19%, 14%, and 7% of the RIs)

- **Improvement in recall by RI**: The 7-, 35-, 70-, and 350-day RIs yielded 10, 59, 111, and 77% improvement in recall for the optimal gap, as compared with the zero-day gap

- **Improvement in recognition by RI**: The corresponding improvements in recognition were 1, 10, 31, and 60%

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Optimal gap vs. zero-day gap (recall)**: d=1.1, 64% increase
- **Optimal gap vs. zero-day gap (recognition)**: d=1.5, 26% increase
- **Retention surface model fit**: R²=.98

[Specific improvement percentages by RI:]
- **7-day RI**: 10% improvement in recall, 1% in recognition
- **35-day RI**: 59% improvement in recall, 10% in recognition
- **70-day RI**: 111% improvement in recall, 31% in recognition
- **350-day RI**: 77% improvement in recall, 60% in recognition

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism: Retention surface**: Final test performance is a function of both gap and retention interval (RI). The relationship is nonmonotonic—at any given test delay, an increase in the interstudy gap at first increased, and then gradually reduced, final test performance.

- **Moderator: Test delay (retention interval)**: The optimal gap increased as test delay increased. However, when measured as a proportion of test delay, the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay.

- **Moderator: Gap duration**: For any RI greater than zero, an increase in study gap should cause recall to first increase and then decrease (inverted-U relationship).

- **Asymmetry of gap costs**: There are costs to using a gap that is longer than the optimal value, but these costs are much smaller than the costs of using too short a gap—as the gap increases, accuracy increases steeply and then declines much more gradually.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Three sessions: (1) Initial learning of 32 obscure trivia facts to criterion of one perfect recall per fact; (2) Second learning session after a gap (0 days to 6 months) with two test-feedback cycles; (3) Final test after retention interval (7 to 350 days) with recall and recognition tests, no feedback

- **26 gap-by-RI combinations**: RIs tested were 7, 35, 70, and 350 days. Gaps ranged from 0 days (actually ~3 min or 0.00256 days) to 6 months. Each RI had 6-7 different gap conditions.

- **Control/Comparison**: Between-subjects design; each participant assigned to one gap-by-RI combination

- **Follow-up period**: Up to 1 year (350 days maximum RI)

- **Attrition**: Noncompletion rates increased at longer delays. Initial knowledge of facts did not differ reliably between subjects who completed all three sessions and those who did not. No detectable differences in age, gender, or demographic characteristics.

- **Study time equated**: Number of restudy trials held constant in second study session (two complete cycles through all 32 facts with feedback)

## Limitations

- **Internet-based data collection**: Potential for distractions and "cheating" (writing down answers), though authors argue evidence suggests this was minimal. Average performance on final test was 41% in Internet study vs. 45% in benchmark lab study for comparable conditions.

- **Limited gap values tested**: Only 6-7 different gaps per RI. Optimal gaps were estimated via cubic spline interpolation rather than directly observed.

- **Single material type**: Only obscure trivia facts (single-word answers, 5-6 letters). Generalizability to other material types unknown.

- **Between-subjects design**: Each participant experienced only one gap-by-RI combination, not within-subjects comparisons.

- **Fixed study-to-criterion in first session**: Subjects cycled through items until achieving one perfect recall, which may not reflect all educational contexts.

- **Preliminary benchmark study cited**: The benchmark study (Cepeda et al., in press) mentioned as "key" had only n=150 and tested a 6-month RI with gaps from 10 min to 6 months. Found optimal gap of 1 month (suggesting 15-20% ratio).

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult (Internet research panel, wide age range, various countries)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium (learning/retention principles apply broadly, but study focused on trivia facts)
- **Red flags**: None—healthy adult population engaging in voluntary memory study

## Conclusions

"The results presented here document the existence of very large and nonmonotonic spacing effects that unfold over very long periods of time, when study time is equated across conditions."

"If a person wishes to retain information for several years, a delayed review of at least several months seems likely to produce a highly favorable return on the time investment—potentially doubling the amount ultimately remembered compared with a less temporally distributed study schedule, with study time equated."

"The optimally efficient gap between study sessions is not some absolute quantity that can be recommended, but rather depends dramatically on the RI."

"It is also of interest to note that although there are costs to using a gap that is longer than the optimal value, these costs are much smaller than the costs of using too short a gap, as evidenced by the fact that as the gap increases, accuracy increases steeply and then declines much more gradually."

## Extraction Notes

This is a landmark spacing effect study covering retention intervals up to 1 year. The nonmonotonic relationship (inverted-U) between gap and retention is clearly demonstrated across multiple RIs. Key practical takeaway: optimal gap is not a fixed percentage of RI—it declines from ~20-40% at 1 week to ~5-10% at 1 year. The effect sizes are very large (d=1.1 for recall, d=1.5 for recognition), suggesting spacing has major practical implications for educational design. The retention surface model (R²=.98) provides a mathematical framework for predicting optimal gaps given a target retention interval.
