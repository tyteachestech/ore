# Worth the Effort: the Start and Stick to Desirable Difficulties (S2D2) Framework

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: de Bruin, A. B. H., Biwer, F., Hui, L., Onan, E., David, L., & Wiradhany, W., 2023
- **Study Type**: Review Article / Theoretical Framework (not empirical; no original data collected)
- **Sample**: Not applicable — narrative review synthesizing prior research; primary empirical focus is on older adolescents and adult learners in self-study contexts
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s10648-023-09766-w
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Effort-learning negative correlation is pervasive**: A meta-analysis (Baars et al., 2020) cited in this paper found that learners' effort ratings correlate negatively with their judgments of learning when engaging in a learning task (r = -0.355). This "data-driven" interpretation — high effort signals poor learning — is the default mode, and it directly suppresses engagement with desirable difficulties.
  - Effect size: r = -0.355 (Baars et al., 2020 meta-analysis; no CI reported in this review)
  - Note: Correlation was weaker for prospective than retrospective judgments; stronger for problem-solving than paired-associate tasks.

- **Finding 2 — Goal-driven interpretation neutralizes the negative correlation**: The subset of studies in the Baars et al. (2020) meta-analysis in which learners adopted a goal-driven effort interpretation showed no negative correlation between effort and learning. Goal-driven learners interpret high effort as a positive signal that learning is happening, rather than evidence of failure.
  - Effect size: Not quantified in this review; cited as a subgroup result from Baars et al., 2020.

- **Finding 3 — Perceived effort directly predicts strategy choice, independently of perceived learning**: Hui et al. (2022) found that learners' effort ratings directly predicted their use of retrieval practice; perceived learning did not play a role before performance feedback was introduced. After individual performance feedback was given, a partial mediation model emerged: effort predicted strategy choice both directly and indirectly through perceived learning.
  - Effect size: Not reported in this review (original study cited).
  - Implication: Effort-as-cost belief can be a standalone barrier, bypassing any rational weighting of expected learning gains.

- **Finding 4 — Perceived effort for interleaving decreases with experience, driving adoption**: Onan et al. (2022) found that over repeated learning trials, perceived effort for interleaving decreased and perceived learning increased, while ratings for blocking remained stable. The effect of decreasing perceived effort on choosing interleaving was fully mediated by increases in perceived learning.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically in this review.
  - Implication: Persistence through initial high-effort phases of a desirable difficulty is self-reinforcing; the strategy feels progressively easier.

- **Finding 5 — Individual performance feedback outperforms general instruction for increasing retrieval practice adoption**: Hui et al. (2021b) found that individual feedback on learning results led to more long-term choices for retrieval practice compared with a general explanation of benefits. The mechanism: feedback conditions learners to pair high effort with positive learning outcomes, reframing effort positively.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically in this review.

- **Finding 6 — Rewarding cognitive effort increases preference for harder tasks in transfer**: Clay et al. (2022) found that rewarding high effort on an N-back working memory task led to higher selection of more difficult tasks in a subsequent unrewarded transfer task (math). Suggests reward-of-effort can generalize beyond the rewarded context.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically in this review.

- **Finding 7 — Framing discomfort as improvement signal increases goal achievement**: Woolley & Fishbach (2022) found that reframing feelings of task discomfort as a sign of improvement in knowledge or skill motivated continued engagement and improved goal achievement.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically in this review.
  - Note: Direct applicability to desirable difficulties is flagged by authors as "an important question for further research."

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

This is a theoretical review paper. It does not report original effect sizes. The only quantitative value cited with a specific number is:

- **Effort-learning negative correlation**: r = -0.355 (Baars et al., 2020 meta-analysis — effort ratings vs. judgments of learning during a learning task)
  - Subgroup: Weaker for prospective vs. retrospective JOLs
  - Subgroup: Stronger for problem-solving vs. paired-associate tasks
  - Subgroup: Goal-driven studies — r approximately 0 (no negative correlation)

All other effects are referenced qualitatively from cited studies. No I², τ², or heterogeneity statistics are reported here (those belong to the original meta-analyses cited).

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Core mechanism — the perceived effort / perceived learning triangle**:
Learners monitor both perceived effort (how hard this feels) and perceived learning (how much I'm gaining). These two signals interact to drive self-regulated engagement with or disengagement from desirable difficulties. The default (data-driven) interpretation misreads high effort as low learning, which causes avoidance.

- **Mechanism 1 — Data-driven effort interpretation (default, maladaptive)**: Learners apply the "easily-learned-easily-remembered" heuristic. High effort during a learning task is interpreted as evidence of poor learning. This is accurate for ineffective strategies but is systematically wrong for desirable difficulties, where high effort is the mechanism of the learning benefit.

- **Mechanism 2 — Fluency bias**: Perceptions of ease (fluency) are conflated with learning. Because desirable difficulties feel disfluent, learners rate their learning as low even when actual learning is high. This is structurally similar to the processing-fluency effect in judgment research.

- **Mechanism 3 — Opportunity cost model**: Perceived effort costs of a desirable difficulty are weighed against the easier alternative. The mere availability of a low-effort alternative (e.g., a peer's summary) inflates the subjective cost of the harder strategy, even though objective learning benefit of the harder strategy is unchanged.

- **Mechanism 4 — Goal-driven interpretation (adaptive, needs cultivation)**: When learners set explicit learning goals and track progress, high effort is reinterpreted as a positive signal (effort = investment in the goal). This disrupts the negative effort-learning correlation. Less common than data-driven interpretation in naturalistic settings.

- **Mechanism 5 — Effort reduction via experience/familiarity**: Repeated engagement with a desirable difficulty reduces its perceived effort as the strategy becomes more proceduralized and familiar. This is the natural "gets easier" pathway to adoption.

**Key Moderators**:
- **Feedback timing and type**: Individual performance feedback (showing actual learning results) shifts effort interpretation toward goal-driven. General instruction about strategy benefits is less effective.
- **Task difficulty calibration**: If material is too hard, some learners do not benefit from the desirable difficulty at all. At that point, the difficulty is undesirable, and feedback will cause resistance, not engagement.
- **Prior knowledge and strategy familiarity**: Learners who already use a strategy find it less effortful; those encountering it for the first time face maximum perceived cost.
- **Importance/deadline pressure**: High personal stakes can "silence" perceived effort entirely, enabling a compensatory state of focused engagement. This is temporary and not sustainable.
- **Prospective vs. retrospective monitoring**: Prospective effort judgments (before/during task) show weaker negative correlation with learning than retrospective ones.

## Study Design Details

- **Type**: Narrative review + theoretical framework proposal (S2D2 = "Start and Stick to Desirable Difficulties")
- **Framework basis**: Builds on the Effort Monitoring and Regulation (EMR) Model (de Bruin et al., 2020), which itself builds on Nelson & Narens' (1994) metacognitive monitoring/regulation model.
- **Intervention taxonomy proposed** (Table 1):
  - **Accept** effort: Strategy-level education; correct misconceptions; show evidence for effort-learning link
  - **Reduce** effort: Task-level segmentation; spaced experience with strategies; app scaffolding (e.g., Anki)
  - **Silence** effort: High-importance framing; flow-state cultivation; deadline pressure; social study conditions
- **"Start" vs. "Stick" distinction**: Strategy-level interventions (education, training) help learners START. Task-level and learner-level interventions help learners STICK once they have begun.
- **No experimental data reported in this paper itself.**

## Limitations

- **No original empirical data**: This is a framework paper only. All effect claims are citations to other studies; none are verified or synthesized quantitatively here.
- **Student population skew**: The empirical base reviewed is heavily weighted toward higher-education students (university/college age). Generalizability to adult self-directed learners (e.g., NotionRPG users doing habit-based learning) is plausible but unverified.
- **Mostly short-term study designs in cited literature**: Authors explicitly note that most studies use single or dual-session designs. Effects across weeks or months of strategy adoption are largely unknown.
- **Limited moderator data**: Characteristics of the learner (e.g., age, prior knowledge, personality, metacognitive skill) that predict who responds to which intervention route are largely unstudied.
- **Framework is not empirically validated as a whole**: The S2D2 framework is proposed as a research agenda, not as a validated model. Its specific predictions have not been tested as a package.
- **Potential demand characteristics**: Much of the cited evidence uses self-report effort and learning ratings, which are known to be biased by many factors (acknowledged explicitly in the paper).
- **No conflict of interest statement** reviewed in full text.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Primarily older adolescents and adults in self-study or higher education contexts (explicitly stated)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium-High
  - The desirable difficulties reviewed (retrieval practice, spaced practice, interleaving) map directly onto app-based learning features
  - The effort-cost model and the "start vs. stick" distinction apply broadly to any self-improvement behavior, not just academic study
  - The mechanisms (fluency bias, data-driven effort interpretation, feedback-driven reframing) are domain-general and translate well to NotionRPG habit contexts
- **Red flags**: None. This is not a clinical population. The framework explicitly targets self-regulated, agentic learners — the closest match to NotionRPG's casual adult user.
- **Caution**: The review does not address populations with learning disabilities, ADHD, or other attention/effort regulation disorders. Effects in those groups may differ.

## Conclusions

"Students' perceptions of effort and learning interact dynamically in mostly unknown ways, and tackling these often erroneous perceptions is necessary to enhance students' willingness to start and stick to desirable difficulties." The authors argue that effort reinterpretation — not just effort reduction — is the core mechanism needed to sustain engagement with effective but effortful learning strategies. Sustainable adoption requires simultaneously addressing perceived effort, perceived learning, and actual learning outcomes, and requires both strategy-level education (to get learners to start) and task/learner-level support (to help them persist). Doing only one is insufficient.

## Named Frameworks (Applicable)

- **Framework**: Start and Stick to Desirable Difficulties (S2D2)
  - Components:
    1. Perceived effort (metacognitive monitoring signal, often biased)
    2. Perceived learning (monitoring judgment, often inversely biased from effort)
    3. Use of desirable difficulties (the regulated behavior)
    4. Three intervention routes: Accept / Reduce / Silence effort
    5. Two phase distinction: Start (strategy education) vs. Stick (task + learner level supports)
  - Research alignment:
    - Perceived effort → effort monitoring (metacognition literature)
    - Perceived learning → Judgments of Learning / Feeling of Knowing (Nelson & Narens model)
    - Accept effort → growth mindset / reframing interventions
    - Reduce effort → scaffolding, segmentation, habit automation (spaced repetition apps)
    - Silence effort → flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi), deadline-driven motivation
    - Start vs. Stick distinction → initiation vs. maintenance distinction in behavior change

- **Framework**: Effort Monitoring and Regulation (EMR) Model (de Bruin et al., 2020)
  - Components: Monitoring loop (perceive effort) → metalevel evaluation → regulation (invest/stop effort)
  - S2D2 is built on top of EMR; EMR is the mechanistic backbone.

## Quotable Passages

> "Learners often prefer restudying when in fact testing typically has a stronger long-term learning effect... Even when learners are aware that interleaved practice and retrieval practice are effective, they are often unwilling to invest the necessary effort because the effort induced by the more effective strategies is notoriously higher." (Introduction)

> "To improve effort regulation towards desirable difficulties, the typically high experienced effort should be accepted, reduced, or silenced... interventions at the strategy level are mostly effective to get learners to *start* using desirable difficulties, whereas interventions at the task and learner level are relevant when learners have started using desirable difficulties and need to *stick to* using them. For sustainable internalization of desirably difficult learning strategies, a combined implementation of both start- and stick to-interventions is needed." (Improving Effort Monitoring and Regulation section)

> "What is sometimes seen as 'lazy' is often not knowing how to approach a task effectively or manage the high load of the task... a lack of learning strategies is the unknown cause of motivational problems." (Discussion)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: Adult and older adolescent self-directed learners; self-study contexts; learning tasks where desirable difficulties (retrieval practice, spaced practice, interleaving, self-explanation) are appropriate
- **Does NOT apply to**: Learners for whom task difficulty is genuinely too high (difficulty becomes undesirable, not desirable); clinical populations; very young learners (metacognitive capacity for effort monitoring is developmentally limited)
- **Unknown/untested**:
  - Whether S2D2 interventions hold over weeks or months (most studies are 1-2 sessions)
  - Whether individual differences in personality, prior metacognitive skill, or trait motivation moderate the "accept / reduce / silence" routes
  - Whether effort-reframing interventions transfer across domains (e.g., math study → professional learning → habit formation)
  - Whether app-based scaffolding (e.g., Anki) reduces effort sufficiently to sustain long-term engagement without also educating users about the why
  - Whether the "silence" route (deadline/importance) can be deliberately induced in low-stakes self-improvement contexts

## Extraction Notes

This is a framework paper, not a data paper. The primary value for NotionRPG is conceptual:

1. **The effort-cost problem is the central design challenge for any difficult-but-effective feature.** Users will experience desirable difficulty (e.g., spaced review, interleaved habit challenges) as demotivating unless they understand why it feels hard. This is the metacognitive illusion problem.

2. **The "start vs. stick" distinction maps cleanly onto onboarding vs. retention.** Onboarding should address the WHY (education/framing), while ongoing design should reduce or reframe perceived effort (scaffolding, feedback, segmentation).

3. **Individual performance feedback is the single most actionable intervention.** Showing users their actual results (e.g., retention test scores, streak data, habit completion rates) after engaging in a harder strategy shifts effort interpretation from "this is costly" to "this is working." The feedback must be individualized and well-timed (not too immediate — some desirable difficulties have delayed effects).

4. **The r = -0.355 correlation between effort and perceived learning is the core diagnostic.** Any app feature that makes users feel they are doing poorly or working too hard without visible progress will be abandoned — even if it is actually the most effective intervention. This is a UX-critical finding.

5. **Segmentation reduces perceived effort without compromising the learning mechanism.** Breaking long or hard tasks into small units (the Pomodoro structure, Anki cards per session, micro-habit challenges) addresses effort perception without undermining the difficult-but-effective quality of the strategy. This is the cleanest design application.

6. **No effect sizes are original to this paper.** Do not cite de Bruin (2023) as a source for specific effect sizes — trace claims back to Baars et al. (2020) for the r = -0.355, and to cited individual studies for others.
