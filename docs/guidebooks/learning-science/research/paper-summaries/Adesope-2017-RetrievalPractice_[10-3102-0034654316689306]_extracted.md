# Rethinking the Use of Tests: A Meta-Analysis of Practice Testing

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., & Sundararajan, N. (2017)
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=118 articles, 272 independent effect sizes, N=15,427 participants total
- **Population**: Predominantly postsecondary students (~83%); also primary and secondary students; cognitively healthy populations
- **DOI**: 10.3102/0034654316689306
- **Search period**: Studies located in 2014; 84 studies published 2011–2015 included
- **Comparison scope**: Low-stakes practice tests only (not high-stakes summative testing)

## Key Findings

- **Overall testing effect**: Practice testing outperforms non-testing learning conditions with a moderately large overall weighted mean effect size (g=0.61, fixed-effects; g=0.70, random-effects), p<.001, I²=81%.

- **Comparison condition matters critically**: The "true" retrieval benefit (against active restudy/rereading) is g=0.51. The larger effect when compared to no-activity or filler conditions (g=0.93) includes the advantage of an additional learning opportunity, not just retrieval per se. The authors explicitly flag g=0.51 as the most accurate indicator of retrieval practice benefits for everyday application.

- **Practice test format moderates outcomes**: Mixed-format practice tests (g=0.80) and multiple-choice practice tests (g=0.70) outperformed short-answer (g=0.48), free-recall, and cued-recall formats. Between-levels difference significant: QB(7)=27.02, p<.001.

- **Transfer-appropriate processing (TAP) matters**: Testing effects were stronger when practice and final test formats were identical (g=0.63) versus dissimilar (g=0.53); QB(3)=15.73, p<.001. Mixed TAP conditions (partial format overlap) yielded g=0.75.

- **Feedback does not significantly moderate the testing effect**: Practice tests with feedback (g=0.63, k=119) and without feedback (g=0.60, k=153) were not significantly different. Testing effects occur regardless of whether corrective feedback is provided.

- **Setting (lab vs. classroom) does not significantly moderate effects**: Classroom studies: g=0.67, k=30. Laboratory studies: g=0.62, k=223. Effects are robust across both settings, though the authors caution about methodological confounds (longer retention intervals in classroom studies).

- **Education level**: Secondary students showed the strongest effects (g=0.83), followed by primary (g=0.64) and postsecondary (g=0.60). Effects are present at all levels.

- **Retention interval**: Intervals of 1–6 days between practice and final test produced larger effects (g=0.82) than intervals of less than 1 day (g=0.56). Timing matters.

- **Number of practice tests**: A single practice test prior to the final test produced larger effects (g=0.70, k=165) than two or more practice tests (g=0.51, k=100).

- **Experimental design**: Randomized designs (g=0.64) outperformed quasi-experimental designs (g=0.47). Effects persist under stringent designs.

- **Publication bias**: Fail-safe N=3,608 (classic) and 2,604 (Orwin's), both well above the 5k+10 threshold. Publication bias unlikely to invalidate findings.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Retrieval effort mechanism**: The cognitive demand of retrieving information from long-term memory strengthens memory traces beyond passive re-exposure. Harder retrieval practice (even without feedback) produces deeper encoding.

- **Desirable difficulty**: Consistent with Bjork's desirable difficulties framework — the effortful act of retrieval itself (not just additional exposure to material) drives retention gains. Multiple choice may outperform short-answer not because it is harder, but because lower-demand retrieval lets learners focus cognitive energy efficiently (Morris et al., 1977 levels-of-processing logic).

- **Transfer-appropriate processing (TAP)**: Memory is most accessible when retrieval conditions match encoding conditions. Matched practice/final test formats amplify the effect, though this may partially reflect familiarity inflation rather than genuine learning.

- **Interleaving (mixed formats)**: Mixed practice test formats may work via interleaving, requiring learners to reload different cognitive processes, forcing interference resolution that aids long-term retention.

- **Retention interval moderates timing effects**: Spacing between practice test and final test matters. Less than 1 day intervals show smaller effects than 1–6 day gaps, consistent with spacing effect literature.

- **Setting confound**: Classroom studies have longer retention intervals and different test formats than lab studies — this partially conflates setting with spacing, limiting clean interpretation of setting as a standalone moderator.

- **Feedback null finding**: Feedback may not moderate testing effects because the benefit comes from retrieval attempt itself, not from error correction. Feedback redundancy is plausible when retrieval success is already high.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Low-stakes practice tests (quizzes, free recall, cued recall, multiple-choice, short-answer) taken before a final criterion test
- **Control/Comparison**: Two categories: (a) restudy/rereading (matched for additional exposure); (b) filler/no-activity conditions (no additional exposure)
- **Outcome measures**: Primarily retention; 11 studies used transfer outcomes
- **Interrater reliability**: k=.96 for coding
- **Effect size metric**: Hedges's g (bias-corrected Cohen's d)
- **Statistical model**: Fixed-effects for primary analyses (justified by within-study sampling variance); random-effects also reported
- **Outliers**: 5 extreme effect sizes adjusted (not excluded) toward nearest distribution value

## Limitations

**Stated limitations:**
- Dissertation Abstracts database not searched (though PsycINFO includes ~12% dissertations from that database); unpublished studies remain underrepresented
- Cannot cleanly separate setting (classroom vs. lab) from confounds like retention interval, test format distribution, and student age
- Feedback coding was coarse — could not examine specific feedback type (immediate vs. delayed, type of feedback), due to insufficient study subgroups
- Only 11 studies measured transfer outcomes; transferability of testing effects remains understudied

**Obvious unstated limitations:**
- I²=81% is very high, indicating the "overall" effect size is an average of highly heterogeneous studies — any single-number headline (g=0.61) masks enormous variation
- ~83% postsecondary samples (predominantly college undergraduates in lab settings) limits generalizability to older or non-student adults
- Most studies were conducted over short timeframes (lab sessions, within-semester), limiting conclusions about very long-term retention (months to years)
- Lab-to-applied gap: the headline g=0.61 (fixed-effects) includes many short-interval lab studies; the applied baseline of g=0.51 (vs. restudy) is more appropriate for real-world use cases
- No funding source conflicts identified, but heavy reliance on existing literature that may itself carry methodological limitations
- Outlier adjustment procedure (adjusting rather than excluding) may inflate effect sizes

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Primarily college undergraduates (postsecondary, ~83%), plus K–12 students; almost entirely cognitively healthy populations
- **Relevance to casual adult behavior change**: Medium. The mechanism (retrieval practice enhances retention) is broadly applicable to any adult learner using self-testing tools (flashcards, quizzes, self-generated questions). Does NOT require supervised classroom settings.
- **Red flags**: None — this is not a clinical population study. However, virtually all data come from educational/academic learning material, not from self-directed real-world skill acquisition or habit-relevant knowledge. The adult non-student population is essentially absent from the evidence base.

## Conclusions

"An overwhelming amount of evidence reviewed in this meta-analysis suggests that retrieval practice increases achievement. The benefits of retrieval practice persist across a wide array of educational levels, settings, and testing formats and procedures." The authors advocate for low-stakes practice testing (quizzes, flashcards, self-generated questions) as superior to rereading and other passive study strategies. The effect against active restudy (g=0.51) is the authors' preferred metric for practical educational application. Testing effects occur with or without feedback, suggesting self-testing without a teacher or grader is still effective.

## Extraction Notes

**Lab-to-applied gap flag (domain note applied):** The headline effect (g=0.61) is a fixed-effects aggregate across highly heterogeneous conditions (I²=81%). The authors themselves identify g=0.51 (vs. active restudy) as the more conservative and practically relevant figure for everyday learners. This is already a corrected-downward estimate compared to many citations of this paper that quote the ~0.6–0.7 range without context.

**Table rendering failure:** The markdown conversion from PDF rendered all tables as vertical character streams (one character per line). All numerical values in this extraction were recovered from the prose Results and Discussion sections, not from tables. Some table-specific values (e.g., exact CIs for moderator subgroups, free-recall practice test effect size, and specific N per subgroup) could not be recovered and are marked where absent. The primary effect sizes (overall, comparison type, format, feedback, setting, education level, retention interval) are all reported in the prose and fully captured here.

**Study count discrepancy:** The abstract and methods mention 282 independent effect sizes at one point and 272 at another. The final analysis used 272 (after outlier identification from the original 272 coded, with 5 adjusted).
