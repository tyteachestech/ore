# Highlighting and Highlighted Information in Text Comprehension and Learning from Digital Reading

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Mason, L., Ronconi, A., Carretti, B., Nardin, S., & Tarchi, C., 2024 (accepted 2023)
- **Study Type**: Experimental (RCT, between-subjects design with immediate and delayed post-tests)
- **Sample**: n=192 (immediate post-test); n=170 (both post-tests), Italian university students (psychology and teacher education)
- **DOI**: 10.1111/jcal.12903
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — No main effect of highlighting condition**: Neither learner-generated nor experimenter-provided highlighting produced significantly better literal comprehension, inferential comprehension, transfer of knowledge, or metacognitive calibration compared to a no-highlighting control when analyzed across conditions. No main effect of condition emerged for any outcome.

- **Finding 2 — Experimenter-provided highlighting × cognitive reflection interaction (literal comprehension)**: Readers with higher cognitive reflection (ability to override automatic thinking) showed significantly better literal comprehension in the experimenter-provided highlighting condition than those with lower cognitive reflection (β = 0.58, SE = 0.19, 95% CI [0.21, 0.95]). This interaction did not emerge for the learner-generated highlighting condition (β = −0.18, SE = 0.20, 95% CI [−0.57, 0.21]) or control (β = 0.34, SE = 0.21). Condition × cognitive reflection interaction: χ²(2) = 7.99, p = 0.018.

- **Finding 3 — Cognitive reflection predicts inferential comprehension independently**: Cognitive reflection significantly predicted inferential comprehension regardless of highlighting condition (β = 0.25, 95% CI [0.14, 0.36], p < 0.001). No condition effect or condition × cognitive reflection interaction emerged for inferential comprehension.

- **Finding 4 — Transfer of knowledge: condition × time interaction**: The experimenter-provided highlighting condition showed significantly greater decline in transfer of knowledge from immediate to delayed post-test than the other two conditions (β = −1.03, SE = 0.24, p < 0.001). Learner-generated highlighting (β = −0.04, p = 0.847) and control (β = −0.10, p = 0.719) conditions showed no significant decline. Condition × time interaction: χ²(1) = 12.33, p = 0.002.

- **Finding 5 — Quality of learner-generated highlighting predicts literal comprehension and transfer**: Among students in the learner-generated highlighting condition (n=68), higher quality of highlighting (proportion of expert-identified key information highlighted) significantly predicted literal comprehension (β = 5.42, 95% CI [1.88, 8.95], p = 0.003) and transfer of knowledge (β = 3.17, 95% CI [0.46, 5.89], p = 0.022).

- **Finding 6 — Reading self-efficacy predicts metacognitive calibration**: Only reading self-efficacy predicted calibration bias (β = 0.49, 95% CI [0.15, 0.83], p = 0.005). Highlighting condition had no effect on calibration.

- **Finding 7 — Active highlighting increased reading time**: Learner-generated highlighters spent 36% more time reading than experimenter-provided highlighting readers and 32% more than control readers (both p < 0.01), confirming the manipulation increased engagement.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

- **Condition × cognitive reflection interaction (literal comprehension)**: χ²(2) = 7.99, p = 0.018; model R² marginal/conditional = 0.21/0.66
- **Cognitive reflection → inferential comprehension**: β = 0.25, 95% CI [0.14, 0.36], p < 0.001; model R² = 0.12/0.52
- **Condition × time interaction (transfer)**: χ²(1) = 12.33, p = 0.002; model R² = 0.07/0.54
- **Experimenter-provided highlighting time decline (transfer)**: β = −1.03, SE = 0.24, p < 0.001
- **Quality → literal comprehension (active highlighting only)**: β = 5.42, 95% CI [1.88, 8.95], p = 0.003; model R² = 0.29/0.64
- **Quality → transfer (active highlighting only)**: β = 3.17, 95% CI [0.46, 5.89], p = 0.022; model R² = 0.07/0.52
- **Reading self-efficacy → calibration**: β = 0.49, 95% CI [0.15, 0.83], p = 0.005; model R² (adjusted) = 0.027

Note: No standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d, etc.) reported for primary outcomes. All primary analyses use LMM with unstandardized regression coefficients.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — "Amplification" effect**: Experimenter-provided highlighting directs attention to key content; readers who engage more deeply (higher cognitive reflection) benefit more because they actually process the highlighted segments instead of skimming.
- **Mechanism — Selection quality determines benefit**: Active highlighting is not effective per se, but *what* is highlighted matters. Higher-quality selection (closer to the expert model) predicts better outcomes, supporting generative learning theory.
- **Moderator — Cognitive reflection**: Moderated the relationship between experimenter-provided highlighting and literal comprehension. Did not moderate outcomes for learner-generated highlighting.
- **Moderator — Reading self-efficacy**: Predicted metacognitive calibration accuracy independently; did not moderate highlighting effects.
- **Moderator — Prior knowledge**: Not a significant moderator for any outcome, likely because the text topic (stem cells) was uniformly unfamiliar.
- **Transfer of knowledge paradox**: Experimenter-provided highlighting produced better immediate transfer but showed significantly greater forgetting by the delayed post-test — suggesting that passive cueing may not support durable learning.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Three-arm online study (Qualtrics). Learner-generated highlighting (yellow mouse-highlight), experimenter-provided highlighting (expert-marked text at 3 relevance levels), or control (read-only). All read a 1,028-word Italian text on stem cells.
- **Outcomes**: Literal comprehension (17 MC questions), inferential comprehension (8 MC questions), transfer of knowledge (4 open-ended, 0–3 pts each), metacognitive calibration (slider 0–100)
- **Follow-up period**: 2 weeks (immediate post-test → delayed post-test)
- **Attrition**: 23/215 excluded for protocol violations; 22/192 did not complete delayed post-test. Final n=170 for longitudinal models.
- **Hypotheses not pre-registered** (explicitly noted by authors)
- **Analysis**: Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with restricted maximum likelihood; Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons

## Limitations

- **No pre-registration**: Authors explicitly acknowledge this; risk of confirmation bias in hypothesis framing
- **Single text, single topic**: Stem cells text only; may not generalize to different content domains or text types
- **University students only**: Italian university students with prior entrance-exam reading screening; may not generalize to other populations or K-12 settings
- **Online administration**: Participants could take notes on paper; 23 control participants excluded for doing so, suggesting real contamination risk
- **Digital reading only**: Cannot directly compare to paper-based highlighting effects; findings are specific to screen-based reading
- **No manipulation of highlighting quality before exposure**: Highlighting quality is a post-hoc correlational measure, not experimentally manipulated; causal direction unclear
- **Low test reliability for some measures**: McDonald's ω for prior knowledge = 0.26 (very low); literal comprehension ω = 0.68, inferential ω = 0.54–0.60 (modest)
- **Unstandardized effects**: No Cohen's d or other standardized effect sizes reported, making comparisons to other literature difficult

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (Italian; psychology and teacher education programs)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Low — findings are specific to academic reading strategy research; highlights the *null* main effect of highlighting
- **Red flags**: None clinical; population is students engaged in structured academic tasks, not general adult self-directed learners

## Conclusions

The study's primary takeaway is that active highlighting is not effective per se during digital reading — no main effect of condition emerged for any outcome. The "amplification" effect found for experimenter-provided highlighting suggests that readers with higher cognitive reflection may extract more benefit from pre-marked text because they process it more deeply. When students highlight, quality of selection matters more than the act of highlighting itself; students who select key information show better literal comprehension and knowledge transfer.

## Extraction Notes

This paper is notable for its *null main effect* finding on highlighting — a widely used but often critiqued strategy. The cognitive reflection moderator is interesting but comes from a non-preregistered interaction test, which warrants skepticism. The transfer of knowledge finding (experimenter-provided highlighting leads to more forgetting at 2 weeks) is potentially counterintuitive and practically important. For NotionRPG, the direct applicability is low (student reading strategy), but the underlying principle — that passive cueing without active processing may not produce durable retention — has moderate relevance to how study features should be designed.
