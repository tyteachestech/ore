# Habit and Behavioural Complexity: Habitual Instigation and Execution as Predictors of Simple and Complex Behaviours

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Benjamin Gardner, 2022
- **Study Type**: Cross-sectional prospective (questionnaire baseline + 1-week follow-up)
- **Sample**: n=118 (completed both timepoints; 173 baseline); UK university students; 70% female, 96% undergraduate, mean age 22.7 years
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.crbeha.2022.100081
- **PMID**: Not provided

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1: Instigation habit predicts behaviour frequency across all complexity levels**
  - Across all four behaviours (phone-checking, sugary drinks, social media, public transport), instigation habit alone significantly predicted behaviour frequency (all p<.001)
  - Effect persisted after adding execution habit at subsequent step; instigation remained predictive (all p<.02)
  - Standardized betas: phone-checking β=.31 to .34; sugary drinks β=.47; social media β=.51 to .48; public transport β=.56 to .45
  - Sample: n=118
  - **Critical implication**: Habit determines behaviour frequency through automatic action *selection* (instigation), not automatic action *performance* (execution), regardless of behavioural complexity

- **Finding 2: Execution habit does NOT predict behaviour frequency, even for complex behaviours**
  - Adding execution habit at step 2 did not improve models for any behaviour (minimum p=.24)
  - Execution habit failed to predict behaviour frequency independently (minimum p=.24 across all behaviours)
  - This contradicted the hypothesis that execution habit might facilitate complex behaviour performance via skill/self-efficacy mechanisms
  - Sample: n=118

- **Finding 3: Instigation-behaviour correlations significantly stronger than execution-behaviour correlations**
  - For drinking sugary drinks: instigation r=.47 vs execution r=.22, p=.02
  - For using social media: instigation r=.51 vs execution r=.38, p=.02
  - For phone-checking and public transport: same trend but not statistically significant (p=.07 and p=.10 respectively)
  - Evidence: Meng et al.'s formula applied to compare correlated correlations
  - Sample: n=118

- **Finding 4: Perceived complexity ratings replicate prior research ranking but differ in magnitude**
  - Behaviours ranked (least to most complex): drinking sugary drinks (mean=2.32, POMP 38.72%) < phone-checking (mean=2.50, POMP 41.68%) < using social media (mean=2.73, POMP 45.58%) < public transport (mean=2.98, POMP 49.80%)
  - All pairwise differences significant at p<.03
  - Sample: n=160 (complexity ratings); mean scores and ranges differ significantly from McCloskey & Johnson (2019) sample (all p<.001)
  - This finding highlights reliability concerns about relying on subjective complexity ratings across different samples

- **Finding 5: Instigation and execution habit are strongly intercorrelated but distinct constructs**
  - Intercorrelations across all four behaviours: r range .69-.79 (all p<.001)
  - Despite high correlation, collinearity not problematic (maximum VIF 2.67, minimum tolerance .37)
  - Participants successfully differentiated between instigation and execution even for simple behaviours, challenging the theoretical assumption that the distinction is irrelevant for simple actions

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Mechanism 1: Instigation habit operates via action selection (pre-action phase)**
- Habitual instigation prompts automatic selection of action when encountering associated cues, bypassing conscious decision-making
- This process determines *whether* someone performs the action (frequency)
- Operates identically across simple and complex behaviours because complexity pertains only to action execution (post-decision phase), not selection

**Mechanism 2: Execution habit regulates action performance quality, not frequency**
- Habitual execution automates the steps within an action sequence
- Reduces cognitive load and increases speed, but does NOT increase action frequency or initiation
- For complex behaviours, over-reliance on execution habit may reduce enjoyment and motivation

**Moderator analysis tested: Behavioural complexity**
- Explicit hypothesis that execution might strengthen relationships for complex behaviours
- **Result**: No interaction detected; execution habit failed to predict even for highly complex behaviour (public transport)
- Suggests complexity is not a moderator of habit-frequency relationships

**Not explicitly tested but discussed:**
- Self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation: theorized to potentially amplify complex behaviour via execution habit, but no empirical support found
- Sample context (university students) may limit generalizability to other populations and consequences

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: None (observational study)
- **Control/Comparison**: N/A
- **Follow-up period**: 1 week (baseline questionnaire to follow-up behaviour report)
- **Attrition**: 68% follow-up rate (118 of 173); no significant differences in demographics, habit, or complexity scores between responders and non-responders (minimum p=.19)
- **Blinding**: N/A (questionnaire study)
- **Measurement**: Self-report habit measured using 4-item Self-Report Behavioural Automaticity Index adapted separately for instigation and execution; complexity measured via 6-item scale (Boynton, 2005); behaviour frequency via single item (0-7 days over past week)
- **Data transformation**: Due to non-normal distributions, behaviour frequency scores transformed (phone-checking squared; sugary drinks and public transport log₁₀; social media squared) for regression; analyses also reported with raw scores in supplementary materials with identical conclusions

## Limitations

**Stated limitations:**
- Instigation-execution distinction may have been artificially magnified by questionnaire prompting participants to reflect on two phases that naturally occur in close temporal proximity (especially for simple behaviours)
- Self-report habit assessment subject to debate regarding accuracy of reflecting on automatic processes; however, measure targets characteristic features (automaticity, lack of conscious deliberation) rather than requiring insight into habit mechanism itself
- Sample of four behaviours selected for student relevance; sugary drinks and public transport performed relatively infrequently while phone-checking and social media highly frequent, limiting variability and potentially suppressing true effects
- Phone-checking performed daily by >75% of sample; heavily skewed distribution remained even after transformation, restricting range and weakening instigation-behaviour relationship
- UK university student sample (96% undergraduates, 70% female, highly educated) may not generalise to general population or more impactful behaviours (e.g., climate-relevant actions)

**Unstated limitations:**
- One-week follow-up too short to assess habit stability; behaviour frequency self-reported and subject to recall bias
- Cross-sectional design with temporal separation (baseline measures → follow-up behaviour) lacks experimental manipulation; cannot rule out reverse causality or common cause explaining habit-frequency correlation
- No objective observation of behaviour; all measurement via self-report
- Complexity operationalized as perceived (subjective) rather than objective; significant disagreement found between current sample and prior research (McCloskey & Johnson, 2019) complexity ratings for identical behaviours, raising questions about validity

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Student (UK university, full-time undergraduates in majority)
- **Relevance to casual behaviour change**: High. Findings directly address everyday habits (phone-checking, snacking, social media, commuting) relevant to casual self-management and habit formation
- **Red flags**:
  - Educated, young adult population; generalizability to older or less educated samples unknown
  - Behaviours studied are voluntary and low-stakes; unclear if findings apply to more consequential health or environmental behaviours
  - High prevalence of certain behaviours (phone-checking 76% daily) may suppress variation and weaken effects

## Conclusions

From the authors:

"This study tested this assumption and found that instigation habit typically predicted behaviour frequency, but execution did not, across four behaviours ranging in complexity. This confirms that, even for complex behaviours, the characteristic effect of habit in generating repetition can be attributed to habitual selection, rather than habitual performance. Behaviour change interventions should target the formation of instigation habits to promote maintenance, for simple and complex behaviours alike."

Further: "Our findings support recent suggestions that, for complex behaviours, interventions might most usefully promote 'higher-order' habits, which are characterised by rigid, habitual instigation but flexible, non-habitual execution."

**Key theoretical implication:** Complexity is irrelevant to habit-frequency relationships because complexity pertains to action execution (post-decisional), whereas habit's effect on frequency operates through action selection (pre-decisional via instigation). This distinction reconciles contradictory views about whether simple cue-behaviour associations can govern complex actions.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Framework: Pre-Action vs. Action Phase Model (Heckhausen & Kuhl, 1985; adapted by Gardner et al., 2016)**
  - Components:
    1. Pre-action phase: decisional processes up to and including selection of an action and commitment to perform it
    2. Action phase: post-decisional processes governing performance of action until termination

  - Research alignment (apparent from text):
    - Instigation habit → operates within pre-action phase, determines action selection and frequency
    - Execution habit → operates within action phase, determines quality/fluency of performance
    - Behavioural complexity → pertains to action phase only (number of sub-steps), so orthogonal to frequency prediction
    - This framework directly explains why complexity fails to moderate habit-frequency relationships

- **Framework: Higher-Order Habits (Phillips, 2020; Phillips & Mullan, 2022)**
  - Components:
    1. Rigid habitual instigation: automatic action selection in response to cues
    2. Flexible non-habitual execution: conscious, deliberative performance of chosen action

  - Research alignment (apparent from text):
    - Instigation predicts frequency (rigidity needed for maintenance) ✓
    - Execution does NOT predict frequency (flexibility preferred for complex behaviours to maintain motivation) ✓
    - Example cited: vegetable plate habit maintained with rigid instigation but flexible vegetable choice
    - Study provides empirical support for optimizing complex behaviour maintenance through instigation-only targeting

## Extraction Notes

This is a well-designed, focused study directly testing the instigation-execution distinction across complexity levels. Key strength: clear operationalization of two habit components via adapted SRBI, direct measurement of perceived complexity, and transparent reporting of data transformations. The finding that execution habit adds zero explanatory power across all behaviours (R² change always .00-.01) is unusually clean and supports the theoretical prediction precisely.

Primary limitation for guidebook application: narrow sample (UK students) and limited scope of behaviours tested (all voluntary, low-commitment). However, the instigation-execution distinction is theoretically important for breaking habits as well—the inverse question would be whether breaking an instigation habit is necessary and sufficient, or whether breaking execution habits also matters. This paper suggests instigation is the lever.

Reliability concern flagged by authors themselves: subjective complexity ratings showed significant disagreement between samples, suggesting that predicting when complexity "matters" may be harder than this study implies.

**Usefulness for Breaking Habits guidebook**: HIGH. Core finding—instigation habits drive frequency; execution habits do not—directly informs intervention design. Suggests that breaking a habit may require disrupting instigation (cue-response associations) rather than execution routines. Also provides theoretical framework for understanding why some habit-breaking attempts fail (targeting execution/willpower rather than instigation/cue management).
