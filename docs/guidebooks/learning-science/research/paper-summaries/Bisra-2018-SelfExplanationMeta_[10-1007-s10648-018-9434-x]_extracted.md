# Inducing Self-Explanation: A Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H., 2018
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=64 studies, 69 independent effect sizes, N=5,917 participants
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x
- **PMID**: Not reported
- **Scope**: Experimental and quasi-experimental studies comparing learning outcomes of participants prompted or directed to self-explain vs. those who were not
- **Search sources**: ERIC, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Education Source, Academic Search Elite, Dissertation Abstracts (1306 initial hits)

---

## Key Findings

- **Overall effect**: Self-explanation prompts produce a moderate-to-large benefit on learning outcomes: g=0.55 (95% CI [0.45, 0.65]), p<.001, across 69 effect sizes. Substantial heterogeneity: I²=65.42%, Q=196.63, p<.001.

- **Self-explanation vs. instructional explanation**: Self-explanation outperforms instructor-provided explanations by g=0.35 (post-hoc comparison). Self-explanation vs. no additional explanation: g=0.67. Self-explanation vs. instructional explanation: g=0.35. The "coverage hypothesis" (that effects are due to content added, not the generative process) is NOT supported.

- **Type of self-explanation elicited is the most practically important moderator**: Conceptual self-explanations (g=0.87) and anticipatory self-explanations (g=1.37, k=1, interpret with caution) significantly outperform metacognitive self-explanations (g=0.19, not statistically significant). Multiple-choice-prompted self-explanations (g=0.24, k=2) were also not statistically detectable.

- **Prompt format matters at the extremes**: Fill-in-the-blank prompts (g=0.90) and predirected prompts (g=0.70) were effective. Multiple-choice format (g=0.24, NS) was not. Overall format differences reached p=.048 but no specific post-hoc contrasts were significant after correction.

- **Effect is robust across task types, subject areas, knowledge types, and education levels**: No statistically detectable differences among levels of: task type (problem solving, text study, worked examples, etc.), knowledge type (conceptual vs. procedural), subject area (science, math, social sciences), or education level (elementary through professional programs). All individual subgroups showed detectable positive effects.

- **Time on task is not the explanation**: Studies where self-explanation took more time (g=0.72) vs. equal time (g=0.41) did not differ statistically (p=.255). Self-explanation appears time-efficient, though time reporting in primary studies is poor.

- **Region moderates effect**: East Asian studies showed substantially higher effects (g=1.19, k=6, 95% CI [0.79, 1.59]) compared to North America (g=0.53), Europe (g=0.48). Post-hoc: East Asia > North America (z=3.11, p=.002), East Asia > Europe (z=3.28, p=.001). Authors attribute to task/comparison condition confounds and possibly reproduction-oriented studying norms.

- **Publication bias**: Fail-safe N = 6,028 (Rosenthal threshold: ≥355); Orwin's fail-safe N = 274. Authors conclude publication bias is not a plausible threat.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Generative processing mechanism**: Self-explanation works because learners actively retrieve prior knowledge and elaborate it with new material, forming associations. The benefit is in constructing the explanation, not merely receiving content. This is why self-explanation outperforms instructional explanation (g advantage = 0.35) despite instructional explanations being more informationally complete.

- **Coverage hypothesis is disconfirmed**: Authors expected instructional explanations to match self-explanation effects (adding information is the mechanism). Data show otherwise — generating the explanation is the active ingredient.

- **Type of self-explanation is the key moderator**: Conceptual self-explanations (asking learners to define or elaborate meaning of named concepts) produce g=0.87 vs. metacognitive self-explanations (g=0.19, NS). Prompt type drives outcome type.

- **Multiple-choice format undermines the effect**: g=0.24 (NS, k=2). The "cue strength" of MC format likely circumvents the generative effort that makes self-explanation work. Fill-in-the-blank (g=0.90) and open-ended formats preserve the generative component.

- **Comparison condition matters substantially**: Effect vs. no control = g=0.67; effect vs. instructional explanation = g=0.35. Studies not equating comparison conditions inflate apparent effect of self-explanation.

- **Time on task is NOT the mechanism**: g is larger when SE takes more time (0.72 vs. 0.41) but the difference is not significant. The benefit is not merely additional study time.

- **East Asia anomaly**: Likely explained by confounds (text-study tasks + weak comparison conditions) plus possible cultural baseline — if learners rarely self-explain spontaneously, prompts provide greater marginal benefit.

- **Non-significant moderators**: Timing (concurrent/retrospective/beginning), content specificity, media type, interactivity level (ITS vs. CBI vs. none), diagrams in materials, test format (except essay/NS), education level, knowledge type (conceptual vs. procedural), subject area.

## Study Design Details

- **Inclusion criteria**: Between-subjects design; prompted or directed self-explanation treatment; no-SE comparison condition; cognitive outcome measured; sufficient statistics for Hedge's g computation
- **Excluded**: Think-aloud protocols with reminders to keep talking; self-explanation combined with confounded strategies (summarizing, feedback); within-subjects designs
- **Effect size method**: Hedge's g (unbiased), random effects model (CMA v3.3); combined scores for multiple outcomes; last measurement for repeated measures; weighted average of multiple comparison groups
- **Heterogeneity testing**: QB statistic with Holm-Bonferroni post-hoc correction
- **Publication bias**: Rosenthal fail-safe N = 6,028 (threshold = 355); Orwin fail-safe N = 274 (to reduce g to <0.10)
- **Coding reliability**: 86% agreement on fixed-choice items, 78% on free-comment items (3 coders, 10-study initial calibration phase)
- **Follow-up period**: Not systematically extracted; only last posttest measure used
- **No assessment of long-term retention** across studies

## Limitations

**Stated:**
- Most studies did not report separate time-on-task for each treatment group, limiting time-efficiency conclusions
- Instructor-scripted prompts dominate the literature; cannot generalize to adaptive or computer-generated prompts
- Anticipated that think-aloud studies may have been included inconsistently by primary researchers; authors applied additional criteria to exclude confounded protocols
- All included studies available in English only — likely excludes non-English research (especially East Asian studies)

**Unstated/obvious:**
- High heterogeneity (I²=65%) means the "overall g=0.55" masks wide variation; moderator analyses explain only a portion of this
- Reliance on Rosenthal fail-safe N is a weak publication bias test (known to be overly optimistic); no funnel plot, no Egger's test, no trim-and-fill reported
- Nearly all primary studies involve school/university populations performing academic learning tasks; generalizability to informal or self-directed adult learning is untested
- Many moderator cells have very small k (k=1-2), making subgroup estimates unreliable (e.g., fill-in-the-blank k=2, multiple choice k=2, anticipatory k=1)
- No assessment of long-term retention; all effects are immediate or short-term posttests
- 90% of studies used random assignment, but allocation concealment and blinding details not reported; risk of experimenter demand effects in unblinded educational experiments
- No quality scoring of primary studies; lower-quality studies could inflate the overall estimate

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Predominantly undergraduate students (k=42/69 effect sizes); also elementary, high school, professional program. Not clinical. Academic/educational settings only.
- **Task context**: Academic learning tasks (problem solving, text comprehension, worked examples). All studies set in formal or semi-formal educational environments.
- **Relevance to casual adult behavior change**: Medium-Low. The mechanism (generative explanation during learning) is broadly applicable, but all evidence comes from structured educational tasks. No studies of informal self-directed learning, professional development without course structures, or non-academic domains (e.g., behavior change, skill acquisition outside school).
- **Red flags for NotionRPG scope**: None (not clinical). However: effect may be attenuated when learners cannot generate accurate self-explanations due to low prior knowledge — a likely condition for adults learning new topics without instructor scaffolding.
- **Most directly applicable finding for behavior change/guidebook context**: Conceptual self-explanation prompts (g=0.87) outperform metacognitive self-explanations (g=0.19, NS) and justify-type prompts (g=0.42). Design implication: prompts that ask "what does this mean / how does this connect?" outperform prompts asking "how am I doing?" or "why did I choose X?"

---

## Conclusions

Authors conclude: "The foremost [practical implication] is that having learners generate an explanation is often more effective than presenting them with an explanation." Self-explanation prompts produce a mean weighted effect of g=0.55 comparable to mastery learning (d=0.50) and peer tutoring (d=0.55) in Hattie's (2009) review. Benefits were detected across subject areas, knowledge types, education levels, task types, and media. The effect is robust to publication bias. Key exceptions: multiple-choice prompts and metacognitive self-explanation prompts failed to show statistically detectable effects. Authors recommend future research on computer-generated adaptive prompts and on fading prompts so self-explanation becomes self-regulated.
