# Can a Brief, Digital Skill Training Intervention Help Undergraduates "Learn to Learn" and Improve Their STEM Achievement?

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Bernacki, M. L., Vosicka, L., & Utz, J. C., 2020 (preprint labeled 2019)
- **Study Type**: RCT (randomized at section level, not individual level)
- **Sample**: n=224 focal sample (84 trained, 140 control); drawn from N=615 enrolled in 3 sections of an undergraduate Anatomy & Physiology course
- **DOI**: 10.1037/edu0000405
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Brief digital training shifts targeted learning behaviors**: A ~2-hour, 3-module self-guided digital training ("Science of Learning to Learn") significantly increased students' use of planning resources (study guides, exam blueprints), self-assessment quizzes (retrieval practice), and learning objective checklists (monitoring progress toward mastery) compared to a time-matched control group.
  - Overall MANCOVA effect on behaviors: η² = .144 (large effect), F(2.48) = significant at p = .003
  - The effect was specific: trained students did NOT increase use of lecture notes (an unrecommended, low-value resource), confirming behavioral specificity rather than a general effort increase.

- **Finding 2 — Training improved quiz and exam performance**: Students who completed the full 3-module training outperformed control on subsequent course assessments.
  - Overall MANCOVA effect on performance: η² = .067 (medium effect)
  - Quiz average: trained M = 81.75% vs. control M = 74.00% (+7.75 percentage points)
  - Exam 3 (immediate, unit-level): trained 75.6% vs. control 71.9% (+3.5 pp), F(1,204) = 5.259, p = .023, η² = .025
  - Exam 4 (final, cumulative): trained 78.5% vs. control 76.6% (+1.9 pp), F(1,204) = 2.938, p = .088 (non-significant), η² = .014

- **Finding 3 — Dosage matters; partial training produces no benefit**: Students who completed only 1 or 2 of the 3 modules showed no significant performance advantage over control. Only full completion (all 3 modules) produced gains. This suggests interdependent module design is load-bearing for the effect.
  - Partial (1 module) Exam 3: M = 80.8; partial (2 modules): M = 80.1; full (3 modules): M = 83.3, F(2,247) = 4.013, p = .019

- **Finding 4 — Cumulative effect fades on summative assessment**: The training effect on the cumulative final exam (administered ~9 weeks post-training, covering all course units) was not statistically significant (p = .088). The training was conducted mid-semester, limiting its cumulative reach.

- **Finding 5 — Prior performance moderates practical (but not statistical) benefit**: The interaction of training × prior performance group was not statistically significant (p = .378), but descriptive data show differential practical benefit:
  - C-or-worse earners: +12% on quizzes (d = 0.46), +3.2% on Exam 3 (d = 0.13)
  - B earners: +6% on quizzes (d = 0.21), +6% on Exam 3 (d = 0.36)
  - A earners: +2% on quizzes (d = 0.12), +1.3% on Exam 3 (d = 0.06)

- **Finding 6 — Planning resource use transfers immediately but does not sustain**: Trained students used more planning resources in the first week of Unit 3 (the unit immediately after training), F(1,219) < .001. By Unit 4 (two units later), both groups converged and no planning difference was detected.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism 1 — Contextualized strategy instruction promotes transfer**: Training was embedded inside the actual course LMS where students later studied. Students were explicitly shown which specific course tools (practice quizzes, study guides, learning objective lists) corresponded to each recommended strategy. Proximity of training to authentic learning context is proposed as key to the transfer that prior face-to-face and digital programs failed to achieve.

- **Mechanism 2 — Full training completion required for interdependent strategy stack**: Module 1 taught strategies; Module 2 taught when to select them (via learning objectives analysis); Module 3 taught how to regulate behavior and environment for their use. Partial completion produced no benefit because the three modules were mutually reinforcing.

- **Mechanism 3 — Behavioral specificity confirms strategic rather than motivational effect**: Training increased use of recommended tools only (quizzes, planning docs, checklists) — not generic engagement (lecture notes unchanged). This rules out a simple effort/engagement explanation for the performance gains.

- **Moderator 1 — Prior performance level**: Descriptively (not inferentially), lower-performing students gained more from training (+12% quiz improvement for C earners vs. +2% for A earners). High performers appear to need training less and may be better off spending that time on content study.

- **Moderator 2 — Timing in semester**: Training was delivered mid-semester. Effects were strongest on the immediately following unit exam. Effects on the cumulative final (9 weeks later) were non-significant. Earlier training (beginning of semester) would theoretically amplify cumulative benefit.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: "Science of Learning to Learn" — 3 self-guided online modules hosted on the course LMS (Blackboard). Each module ~40-50 min. Total: ~90-120 minutes. Topics: Module 1 (retrieval practice, spacing, self-explanation), Module 2 (SRL overview, goal-setting, planning, strategy selection, monitoring), Module 3 (implementation intentions, mental contrasting, distraction management).
- **Control/Comparison**: 3 independent anatomy case study activities, time-matched (~45 min each), designed to provide no effect on learning skills. No significant dosage effect found within control group.
- **Follow-up period**: ~9 weeks post-training (to the cumulative final exam)
- **Randomization**: At section level (not individual level) — 2 sections assigned to training, 1 to control. A baseline Exam 1 advantage for trained group was detected post-randomization (t[222] = 2.19, p = .004); controlled for via covariate.
- **Attrition**: Of ~200 students/section, 63% consented. Focal sample retained only the 84 students completing all 3 training modules (vs. 140 control completers). This selectivity may introduce self-selection bias.
- **Blinding**: Instructor was blind to section-condition assignment.

## Limitations

- **Section-level randomization**: Two sections assigned to training vs. one to control increases confounding risk. Students who self-select different course meeting times may differ on unmeasured variables. The authors partially mitigated this by using two training sections with different meeting times.
- **Baseline performance imbalance**: Despite randomization, trained students outperformed control students on Exam 1 (p = .004). Covariate adjustment was applied but cannot fully eliminate this confound.
- **Focal sample includes only full-training completers**: 84 of 249 initial training condition participants completed all 3 modules. This ~34% completion rate creates a highly selected sample — results apply to people who complete the training, not to all who begin it. The effect on the "intent-to-treat" population is unknown and almost certainly smaller.
- **Training delivered mid-semester**: Limits cumulative effect accumulation. Final exam results are consequently weaker. Authors recommend beginning-of-semester deployment.
- **LMS trace data are inferred**: Accessing a planning document ≠ actually planning. Behavioral measures rely on reasonable but unverified inferences about how resources were used.
- **Print textbook is unmeasured**: Students also used offline materials; digital traces capture only part of actual study behavior.
- **Single course/institution**: Anatomy & Physiology at one university (high proportion of underrepresented minority and first-generation college students). Generalizability to other courses, disciplines, or institutions is untested.
- **No long-term follow-up**: No data beyond the same semester. Persistence of behavioral change beyond the course is unknown.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Undergraduate STEM students (Anatomy & Physiology course); median age 20; 74% female; 56% first-generation college students; 47% Title 1 high school graduates; diverse ethnic composition
- **Relevance to casual behavior change (NotionRPG)**: Medium. The core finding — that a brief, digital, contextualized onboarding module can shift specific behavioral patterns (tool usage, planning, self-monitoring) in a target environment — is directly analogous to onboarding design for a productivity app. However, the population and stakes (STEM retention, GPA) are quite different from adult casual users of a habit-tracking system.
- **Red flags**: Student population only; stakes-driven motivation (extra credit, course grade) may not transfer to voluntary-use tool context; module completion rate was ~34%, suggesting the "true" population-level effect is weaker than within-completers numbers suggest; no adult general population data

## Conclusions

"This study confirmed that brief digital training that exposes undergraduates to learning strategies and provides opportunities to practice selecting, applying, and monitoring the effect of learning strategies can promote desired behavior and improve course performance. Further, results indicated that these effects can be achieved when students invest just a few hours in a self-contained training that requires no additional investment of instructor effort or class time."

Authors frame the key insight as: time spent "learning to learn" yields better returns than equivalent time spent studying content — but only when training is (a) brief, (b) contextualized to the actual learning environment, and (c) fully completed.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Framework**: Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) — Winne & Hadwin (1998, 2008); Zimmerman (2000, 2011)
  - Components: Task definition → Goal-setting → Planning → Strategy selection → Monitoring → Adaptation
  - Research alignment: Module 2 directly maps to this cycle; behavioral outcomes tracked via LMS match each SRL phase
- **Framework**: Hattie & Donoghue (2016) Skill Acquisition Model
  - Components: Surface acquisition (encoding) → Surface consolidation (retrieval practice) → Deep learning (metacognitive self-regulation) → Transfer
  - Research alignment: Module 1 = surface consolidation (retrieval practice, spacing); Module 2 = deep learning (SRL); Module 3 = transfer (implementation intentions, environment regulation)

## Extraction Notes

**Usefulness for NotionRPG**: High, with important caveats. The core design principle — embed a brief strategy-training module inside the tool where the behavior will actually occur, teach specific tool affordances explicitly, and require full completion for it to work — is directly applicable to app onboarding design. The finding that effects are behavior-specific (not general engagement boost) is especially useful: it suggests that onboarding should teach targeted tool behaviors, not just motivation.

**Critical caveat**: The 34% within-condition completion rate means the η² = .067 performance effect is a within-completer estimate. For a tool where completion is voluntary and stakes are low (no extra credit, no grade), the real-world effect at the population level would likely be substantially smaller. This is the most important limitation for any application to NotionRPG onboarding ROI estimates.

**Quality note**: The baseline exam performance difference despite randomization (p = .004) is a meaningful methodological concern. ANCOVA correction helps but does not eliminate the confound. Effect sizes should be treated as upper-bound estimates.

**Particularly quotable for guidebook**: The dosage finding — partial completion = zero benefit — is a strong design implication for any multi-part onboarding sequence.
