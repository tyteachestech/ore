# Test-Enhanced Learning: Taking Memory Tests Improves Long-Term Retention

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Roediger & Karpicke, 2006
- **Study Type**: Experimental (two experiments)
- **Sample**: Experiment 1: n=120 undergraduates, ages 18-24; Experiment 2: n=180 undergraduates, ages 18-24
- **DOI**: 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Testing vs. Restudying on Immediate Tests (Exp 1, 5-min delay)**: Restudying produced better recall than testing (81% vs. 75%)
  - Effect size: d = 0.52, t(39) = 3.22
  - Sample: n per group not explicitly reported

- **Testing vs. Restudying After 2 Days (Exp 1)**: Initially tested group recalled more than additional-study group (68% vs. 54%)
  - Effect size: d = 0.95, t(39) = 6.97
  - Sample: n per group not explicitly reported

- **Testing vs. Restudying After 1 Week (Exp 1)**: Tested group recalled 56% of material, restudy group recalled 42%
  - Effect size: d = 0.83, t(39) = 6.41
  - Sample: n per group not explicitly reported

- **Repeated Testing vs. Repeated Studying After 1 Week (Exp 2)**: STTT group (study once, test three times) recalled 61%, SSSS group (study four times) recalled 40%
  - Effect size: Not explicitly reported
  - Sample: n=30 per condition

- **Proportional Forgetting (Exp 2)**: SSSS condition forgot 52%, SSST condition forgot 28%, STTT condition forgot 14%
  - Effect size: Not explicitly reported
  - Sample: Not explicitly reported

- **Confidence Ratings vs. Actual Performance (Exp 2)**: SSSS group predicted better memory than SSST and STTT groups, but actually performed worst on delayed test
  - Effect size: d = 0.54 (SSSS vs. SSST prediction), t(118) = 2.95; d = 0.61 (SSSS vs. STTT prediction), t(118) = 3.35
  - Sample: Not explicitly reported

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Experiment 1 (n=120 total, 40 per retention interval group):**
- Restudying vs. Testing at 5-min: d = 0.52, t(39) = 3.22
- Testing vs. Restudying at 2 days: d = 0.95, t(39) = 6.97
- Testing vs. Restudying at 1 week: d = 0.83, t(39) = 6.41
- Main effect of testing vs. restudying: F(1, 117) = 36.39, eta-p-squared = .24
- Main effect of retention interval: F(2, 117) = 50.34, eta-p-squared = .46
- Learning Condition x Retention Interval interaction: F(2, 117) = 32.10, eta-p-squared = .35

**Experiment 2 (n=180 total, 30 per condition):**
- Main effect of retention interval: F(1, 174) = 122.53, eta-p-squared = .41
- Effect of learning condition: F(2, 174) = 2.32, eta-p-squared = .03 (marginally significant)
- Learning Condition x Retention Interval interaction: F(2, 174) = 18.48, eta-p-squared = .18
- Confidence predictions (SSSS rated passage as less interesting): F(2, 177) = 3.88, eta-squared = .04
- Confidence predictions (SSSS more confident of memory): F(2, 177) = 6.09, eta-squared = .06
- SSSS vs. SSST prediction: t(118) = 2.95, d = 0.54
- SSSS vs. STTT prediction: t(118) = 3.35, d = 0.61

**Interrater reliability:** r = .95 (for scoring recall tests)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Transfer-appropriate processing**: Testing during learning permits practice of the skill required on future tests, enhancing performance after a delay
- **Desirable difficulties**: Testing introduces a desirable difficulty during learning - conditions that hinder short-term performance but enhance long-term retention
- **Elaboration and retrieval routes**: Testing may enhance learning by producing elaboration of existing memory traces and multiplying "retrieval routes" to stored events
- **Not reexposure**: The testing effect is not simply due to reexposure during tests - restudying allowed 100% reexposure but produced poor long-term retention

**Moderator tested:**
- **Retention interval**: Testing produced worse performance at 5-min but superior performance at 2 days and 1 week (crossover interaction)

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**:
  - Exp 1: Two conditions: (1) study once + restudy once, or (2) study once + take test
  - Exp 2: Three conditions: (1) SSSS (study four times), (2) SSST (study three times + test once), or (3) STTT (study once + test three times)
  - Tests were free-recall tests without feedback (10-min duration for final tests, 7-min for initial test in Exp 1, 10-min for initial tests in Exp 2)
  - Study periods: 7 min (Exp 1), 5 min (Exp 2)
  - Materials: Prose passages from TOEFL prep book (256 and 275 words, divided into 30 idea units each)

- **Control/Comparison**: Restudy condition (students restudied entire passage, allowing 100% reexposure)

- **Follow-up period**: 5 min, 2 days, or 1 week (Exp 1); 5 min or 1 week (Exp 2)

- **Attrition**: Not reported

- **Blinding**: Not reported

- **Initial recall performance**: Subjects recalled approximately 70% of passage on initial tests (20.9 out of 30 idea units)

- **Reading frequency**: In Exp 2, subjects read passage approximately 3.5 times during each 5-min study period. SSSS subjects read passage mean of 14.2 times total, STTT subjects read it 3.4 times total.

## Limitations

- **Student population only**: Both experiments used undergraduate students ages 18-24 from Washington University; generalizability to other populations unknown

- **Short prose passages**: Materials were relatively brief (256-275 words); effects with longer texts or other materials unknown

- **No feedback on tests**: Tests provided no feedback; testing with feedback might produce even stronger effects (as noted by authors)

- **Limited retention intervals**: Only tested up to 1 week; longer-term retention effects unknown

- **Free recall only**: Only used free-recall tests; effects may differ for recognition tests or other test formats

- **Boundary conditions not established**: Authors note "boundary conditions for the testing effect are not yet known"

- **Controlled laboratory setting**: Effects in actual classroom settings with authentic educational materials and stakes unknown

- **No measurement of encoding quality**: Did not assess what students were doing during restudy periods vs. test periods

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General adult (undergraduate students, though specific to one university)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: High - testing effect applies to self-directed learning and skill acquisition
- **Red flags**: None - healthy undergraduate population without clinical conditions

## Conclusions

"Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it." Taking tests soon after learning promoted superior long-term retention compared to repeated studying, even though repeated studying produced better immediate performance. The testing effect is not simply due to reexposure to material during tests, but rather due to processes like transfer-appropriate processing and desirable difficulties. Repeated studying inflated students' confidence in their future memory but actually produced worse long-term retention than testing. "Judicious use of testing may improve performance in educational settings at all levels from elementary through university education."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Transfer-appropriate processing** (Morris, Bransford, & Franks, 1977)
  - Components: Compatibility between operations engaged during learning and testing phases
  - Research alignment: Practicing retrieval during learning → better retrieval performance at test

- **Desirable difficulties** (Bjork, 1994)
  - Components: Study conditions that hinder short-term performance but enhance long-term retention
  - Research alignment: Testing introduces difficulty during learning → superior long-term retention

## Extraction Notes

This is a foundational paper demonstrating the testing effect with educationally relevant materials. Notable strengths: (1) directly compared testing to restudying (not just testing to no exposure), (2) crossover interaction shows testing harms immediate performance but helps delayed performance, (3) demonstrated metacognitive illusion where repeated studying increases confidence but decreases actual performance.

Sample sizes per condition are not explicitly stated for Experiment 1 (text only says "40 were scored by two raters" for reliability check, and reports t(39) suggesting 40 per retention interval group, but does not state how many were in restudy vs. test within each interval). Experiment 2 clearly states 30 per condition.

The effect sizes for delayed testing are large (d = 0.83 to 0.95), making this a robust finding. The practical implications are clear: frequent low-stakes testing should be incorporated into learning routines.
