# Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013)
- **Study Type**: Systematic Review
- **Focus**: Comprehensive literature review evaluating 10 learning techniques across four categories of variables: learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, and criterion tasks
- **Sample**: Review of 100+ years of research; primarily studies involving K-12 and college students
- **DOI**: 10.1177/1529100612453266
- **Journal**: Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4–58

## Key Findings

This monograph evaluates 10 learning techniques and assigns utility ratings based on evidence of generalizability:

### High Utility Techniques (2)

- **Practice Testing**: Rated HIGH utility. Testing effects demonstrated across impressive range of practice-test formats, materials, learner ages, outcome measures, and retention intervals. Comparative advantage: Karpicke & Roediger (2008) found performance 1 week later was 80% after continued testing vs. 36% after continued study. Broad applicability; not time intensive; minimal training required. Evidence in representative educational contexts supports efficacy.

- **Distributed Practice**: Rated HIGH utility. Spacing effects robust across 254 studies (14,000+ participants). Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis: students recalled 47% after spaced study vs. 37% after massed study. Key finding: optimal lag between sessions is approximately 10-20% of desired retention interval (e.g., 12-24 hours apart to remember 1 week; 6-12 months apart for 5-year retention). Generalizes across many variables; moderate effect sizes for recall of verbal stimuli.

### Moderate Utility Techniques (3)

- **Elaborative Interrogation**: Rated MODERATE utility. Seifert (1993) reported average effect sizes ranging from 0.85 to 2.57 for cued recall (Pressley et al. 1987: elaborative group 72% accuracy vs. 37% in control groups). Effects shown across broad range of factual topics. Limitations: applicability unclear for lengthier/complex material beyond fact lists; few studies examined educational contexts; limited research on retention intervals beyond 2 weeks.

- **Self-Explanation**: Rated MODERATE utility. Effects shown across impressive range of learning outcomes (memory, comprehension, transfer). Benefits demonstrated across age range from kindergarten to college; works for problem-solving and text learning. Limitations: durability across longer retention intervals woefully underexplored (only 5 studies); time demands substantial (30-100% increase in study time); minimal research in educational contexts.

- **Interleaved Practice**: Rated MODERATE utility. Taylor & Rohrer (2010) with fourth graders: interleaved practice yielded 77% accuracy on delayed criterion test vs. 38% after blocked practice, despite lower accuracy during practice (79% vs. 98%). Benefits shown for problem-solving and concept formation. Limitations: evidence just beginning to be systematically explored; interaction with skill level and task difficulty requires more research; mixed results in classroom contexts.

### Low Utility Techniques (5)

- **Summarization**: Rated LOW utility. Can be effective for skilled summarizers but requires extensive training for younger students and learners with disabilities (6.5-11 hours training for learning-disabled students). Benefits limited to certain criterion tasks; mixed findings for application/synthesis questions. In head-to-head comparisons, self-questioning outperformed summarization on standardized tests.

- **Highlighting/Underlining**: Rated LOW utility. Despite popularity, highlighting did not boost performance across variety of criterion tasks (received "N" rating for criterion tasks). Students report heavy reliance on this technique, but evidence does not support effectiveness as study strategy relative to alternatives.

- **Keyword Mnemonic**: Rated LOW utility. Benefits limited to keyword-friendly materials (concrete nouns). Key concern: Wang et al. (1992) found keyword benefit vanished on delayed test—when performance equated at immediate test, rote repetition group outperformed keyword group on delayed test, suggesting accelerated forgetting. Requires extensive training and keyword generation; less efficient than practice testing.

- **Imagery Use for Text Learning**: Rated LOW utility. Helps some students on some criterion tasks, but conditions for benefit limited and research insufficient. Benefits depend heavily on prior domain knowledge; limited evidence across criterion tasks.

- **Rereading**: Rated LOW utility. Although benefits shown across relatively wide range of text materials, generality across other variables not well established. Most research limited to college-age students; effects on comprehension unclear (benefits mainly for recall-based memory measures). Much less effective than elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and practice testing in direct comparisons.

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

### Practice Testing
- Runquist (1983): pairs tested vs. untested—53% vs. 36% at 10 minutes; 35% vs. 4% at 1 week
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): practice test vs. restudy on free recall after 1 week—56% vs. 42%
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008): continued testing vs. continued study after 1 week—80% vs. 36%
- Testing effects generalize across formats: cued recall, free recall, multiple-choice, problem-solving

### Elaborative Interrogation
- Pressley et al. (1987) meta-analysis: effect sizes d = 0.85 to 2.57
- Smith et al. (2010) educational context: elaborative interrogation group 76% vs. control 69% on true/false questions

### Distributed Practice
- Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis (254 studies, 14,000+ participants): 47% recall after spacing vs. 37% massed
- Bahrick (1979): performance on final test 30 days later—30-day spacing superior to 1-day spacing, which superior to massed (0-day)

### Self-Explanation
- Berry (1983): logical reasoning transfer test—substantially higher accuracy in self-explanation groups
- Effects shown for near-transfer and far-transfer tasks

### Interleaved Practice
- Taylor & Rohrer (2010): delayed test (1 day)—77% accuracy for interleaved vs. 38% for blocked practice
- Kang & Pashler (2012): concept learning—68% interleaved vs. 58% blocked practice

### Keyword Mnemonic
- Wang et al. (1992) Experiment 3: immediate test advantage disappeared at delayed test; when equated at immediate, rote repetition superior at delay

### Summarization
- Bretzing & Kulhavy (1979): high school students summarizing vs. control—both immediate and 1-week delayed tests favored summarization and note-taking groups

## Mechanisms & Moderators

### How Techniques Work

**Elaborative Interrogation & Self-Explanation**: Integration of new information with existing prior knowledge; activation of schemata that organize information; processing of similarities and differences between related entities (distinctive processing).

**Practice Testing**: Retrieval practice enhances memory; direct retrieval from memory strengthens target information. Correct answer feedback prevents perseveration errors.

**Distributed Practice**: Multiple mechanisms likely: (1) deficient processing—second learning episode suffers if massed, learner may misjudge fluency; (2) reminding—second presentation retrieves first episode, enhancing memory; (3) consolidation—second episode benefits from consolidation of first trace. These mechanisms not mutually exclusive.

**Interleaved Practice**: Two competing explanations supported by evidence: (1) discriminative-contrast hypothesis—interleaving promotes comparison of different problem types, helping students discriminate and identify critical differences; (2) retrieval-practice hypothesis—distributed retrieval from long-term memory required for interleaved practice boosts memory for retrieved information.

### Key Moderators

**Elaborative Interrogation**:
- Prompt quality: benefits larger when elaborations precise vs. imprecise
- Prior knowledge: effects larger with higher prior knowledge
- Self-generation: benefits larger when elaborations self-generated vs. provided
- Dosage: effects diluted or reversed when elaborative prompts administered infrequently (< 1 per page)

**Self-Explanation**:
- Timing: concurrent self-explanation more effective than retrospective
- Access to provided explanations: effects diminished when learners can access experimenter-provided explanations
- Domain knowledge: mixed evidence; effects sometimes only for lower-skill students
- Time demands: substantial time increases (30-100%) may confound effects

**Practice Testing**:
- Feedback: crucial; testing + feedback vastly superior to testing alone or restudy. Delayed feedback as effective as immediate for correcting errors
- Practice test format: benefits shown across multiple formats; some evidence for free recall showing slightly larger effects

**Distributed Practice**:
- Retention interval: optimal spacing is ~10-20% of desired retention interval
- Lag between sessions: longer lags better than shorter for long-term retention, but answer depends on desired retention interval
- Type of practice: distributed practice testing better than distributed study

**Interleaved Practice**:
- Skill level/task difficulty: more pretraining required for younger, less skilled students, and complex tasks
- Prior knowledge: benefits may depend on achieving baseline competency first (Rau et al. 2010: benefits mainly for students with low prior knowledge)
- Initial instruction: amount of initial blocked practice before interleaving may influence effectiveness

**Keyword Mnemonic**:
- Material type: limited to keyword-friendly materials (concrete nouns); fails for abstract terms
- Keyword provision: student-generated keywords show mixed results vs. experimenter-provided
- Retention interval: evidence for accelerated forgetting at longer delays

**Summarization**:
- Writing skill: linked to summarization ability in seventh graders
- Domain knowledge: students with more domain-relevant knowledge better at summarizing; unclear if benefits transfer to domain experts
- Text presence: unclear whether text should be present during summarization

**Highlighting**:
- No significant moderators identified; broadly ineffective across conditions

**Rereading**:
- Rereading lag: spaced rereading outperforms massed; optimal lag ~4 days in one study
- Retention interval: effects may not persist beyond short delays for massed rereading
- Criterion task type: strong for free recall and cued recall; weak/nonexistent for recognition and comprehension

**Imagery for Text Learning**:
- Prior domain knowledge: prerequisite for effectiveness; students must know objects/ideas words refer to

## Study Design Details

**Evaluation Framework**: Reviewed literature on 10 techniques across four variable categories:
1. Learning conditions (dosage, individual vs. group, reading vs. listening, etc.)
2. Student characteristics (age, ability, prior knowledge, working memory, interests, motivation)
3. Materials (vocabulary, definitions, narrative, expository texts, math problems, diagrams, maps)
4. Criterion tasks (cued recall, free recall, recognition, problem-solving, essay, application, comprehension)

**Analysis Approach**: For each technique, authors (1) described mechanism and theoretical rationale; (2) evaluated generalizability across four variable categories; (3) assessed evidence in representative educational contexts; (4) identified implementation issues; (5) assigned overall utility rating (High/Moderate/Low).

**Data Sources**: Relied on prior reviews for heavily researched areas (e.g., distributed practice) and supplemented with recent studies; cited subset of relevant articles rather than exhaustive list due to volume.

**Methodology Notes**: Authors examined coverage in educational-psychology textbooks and found most techniques received minimal coverage, influencing teacher training.

## Limitations

### Stated Limitations

1. **Insufficient research on long-term retention**: Almost all self-explanation studies administered tests within minutes of learning; only 5 studies used longer retention intervals (1-2 weeks maximum).

2. **Limited research in educational contexts**: Few techniques evaluated in actual classroom settings with course-relevant content and high-stakes assessments; most evidence from laboratory studies.

3. **Unresolved questions on time on task**: For self-explanation and other techniques, studies often did not equate time on task across conditions; increased study time may confound effects.

4. **Mixed findings on moderating variables**: Limited systematic exploration of how individual differences (ability, prior knowledge, age) moderate technique effects; existing evidence often inconsistent.

5. **Keyword mnemonic durability**: Evidence for long-term benefit compromised by experimental designs that tested all items on both immediate and delayed tests, allowing retrieval practice to inflate delayed performance.

6. **Interleaving**: Evidence "just beginning to be systematically explored"; ultimate effectiveness unknown. Interaction with skill level/task difficulty requires further investigation.

7. **Educational-psychology textbook coverage**: Survey of six textbooks found most techniques received insufficient coverage, limiting teacher awareness and training.

### Obvious Unstated Limitations

1. **Publication bias**: Review relies on published literature; unknown whether unpublished null results would change conclusions.

2. **Heterogeneity in technique implementation**: Wide variability in how techniques operationalized across studies (e.g., self-explanation prompts vary from content-free to content-specific); limits generalization of mechanisms.

3. **Age range gaps**: Most elaborate techniques (e.g., distributed practice) researched primarily with college students; generalizability to younger learners limited.

4. **Task specificity**: Effects highly dependent on specific criterion tasks used; transfer to real-world achievement tests unclear for most techniques.

5. **Training effects confound**: Techniques requiring training (summarization, keyword mnemonic) difficult to disentangle from effects of training itself vs. technique intrinsically.

6. **Interaction with motivation**: Little research on how student motivation moderates technique effectiveness; applicability to unmotivated learners unclear.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Mixed—primarily K-12 students and college undergraduates; some research with graduate students and adults in online studies
- **Age coverage**: Elaboration techniques tested from kindergarten to college; rereading limited to mostly college students; distributed practice broad; practice testing excellent age range coverage
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: HIGH relevance—all techniques directly applicable to voluntary study and learning regulation by students and lifelong learners. Emphasis on student-controllable techniques requiring minimal external support.
- **Clinical/comorbidity concerns**: None identified; review focuses on learning in normative student populations (though some techniques studied with learning-disabled students)
- **Red flags**: None; paper explicitly designed to evaluate techniques students can self-regulate without advanced technologies or teacher preparation

## Conclusions

"This monograph will foster improvements in student learning, not only by showcasing which learning techniques are likely to have the most generalizable effects but also by encouraging researchers to continue investigating the most promising techniques" (p. 81).

Two techniques received high utility: practice testing and distributed practice, showing robust benefits across learner ages, abilities, and criterion tasks with applicability in educational contexts. Three techniques (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice) show promise but fall short of high ratings due to limited evidence in educational contexts and/or insufficient research on long-term retention. Five techniques (summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading) received low utility due to limited generalizability, high training demands, or poor comparative effectiveness. Authors recommend students adopt effective techniques while research continues on moderators and application in real classroom settings.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

**Jenkins' (1979) Model**: Four-category framework for evaluating learning technique generalizability (Learning conditions, Student characteristics, Materials, Criterion tasks)—foundational model guiding the review structure.

**Bloom's Taxonomy (1956, updated 2001)**: Six levels of learning objectives from knowledge/memory through comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation—used to categorize criterion tasks and learning outcomes throughout review.

**Good-Strategy-User Model (Pressley, 1986; Pressley, Goodchild, et al., 1989)**: Defines sophisticated learner as one who knows effective techniques, knows when/how to use them, and uses them in combination with domain knowledge—theoretical foundation for evaluating technique utility for student learning regulation.

## Extraction Notes

This is a landmark monograph providing evidence-based recommendations for learning technique selection. Strengths include systematic literature review spanning 100+ years of research, clear evaluation criteria, and practical utility ratings. The paper directly addresses widespread student reliance on ineffective techniques (highlighting, rereading) while many effective techniques remain underused.

Key utility for NotionRPG: The high-utility techniques (practice testing, distributed practice) and moderate-utility techniques (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice) directly map to evidence-based study strategies that could enhance gamified learning systems. Moderator analyses provide detailed guidance on when techniques work best for different learners/materials. The emphasis on educational context validation highlights gap between laboratory evidence and real-world implementation.

Notable: Paper critiques textbook coverage of learning techniques and calls for improved teacher training, suggesting institutional barriers to adoption of effective techniques. Also addresses the "time-on-task" confound that affects interpretation of self-paced learning interventions.
