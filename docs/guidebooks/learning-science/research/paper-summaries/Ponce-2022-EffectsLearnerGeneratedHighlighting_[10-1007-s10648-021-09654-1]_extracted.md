# Effects of Learner-Generated Highlighting and Instructor-Provided Highlighting on Learning from Text: A Meta-Analysis

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Ponce, H. R., Mayer, R. E., & Méndez, E. E., 2022
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis
- **Sample**: k=36 published articles (1938–2019), generating 85 effect sizes; populations = college students and K-12 students (school = grades 5–6 in most studies)
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1
- **PMID**: Not reported

---

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Learner-generated highlighting improves memory but not comprehension**: Students who highlighted on their own showed a significant improvement in memory tests (g=0.36, p=0.013) but a non-significant improvement in comprehension tests (g=0.20, p=0.155). The overall effect across both outcome types was g=0.30, p=0.003.

- **Finding 2 — Learner-generated highlighting works for college students but not K-12 students**: College students showed significant improvement (g=0.39, p=0.001); K-12 students did not (g=0.24, p=0.278). The between-group difference was not statistically significant (QB(1)=0.4, p=0.517), meaning the directional pattern existed but the moderation test was underpowered.

- **Finding 3 — Training on how to highlight dramatically boosts effectiveness**: Students who received structured training before highlighting showed g=1.02 (p=0.002). Even brief training (~1–2 hours) produced g=0.86 in one study. Only k=4 studies; interpret with caution.

- **Finding 4 — Combining highlighting with notetaking or graphic organizers nearly doubles effectiveness**: Highlighting + complementary strategy (notetaking, graphic organizer) yielded g=0.82, p<0.001, k=3. This is more than twice the effect of highlighting alone for college students.

- **Finding 5 — Reviewing previously highlighted material does not improve learning**: Highlighting + review later (one week) showed g=-0.06, p=0.692, k=5. No significant benefit over reading-only control.

- **Finding 6 — Instructor-provided highlighting improves both memory and comprehension equally**: Pre-highlighted text produced g=0.44 for memory (p<0.001) and g=0.44 for comprehension (p=0.006). Overall g=0.44, p<0.001.

- **Finding 7 — Instructor-provided highlighting works for both college and K-12 students**: College students: g=0.41, p<0.001; K-12 students: g=0.48, p=0.002. Combined overall g=0.42, p<0.001. Both educational levels benefit.

- **Finding 8 — Inappropriate highlighting is actively harmful**: Texts with wrong/irrelevant information highlighted produced g=-0.70, p=0.047, k=6, compared to reading-only controls.

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Mechanism — Selecting vs. deeper processing**: Based on generative learning theory (Fiorella & Mayer, 2015) and CTML (Mayer, 2021). Learner-generated highlighting primarily activates the "selecting" cognitive process (attending to relevant material) but consumes enough working memory capacity that little remains for "organizing" and "integrating" — hence memory gains but not comprehension gains.

- **Mechanism — Instructor highlighting frees working memory**: Pre-highlighted text efficiently guides selecting, freeing cognitive capacity for organizing and integrating, which is why it improves both memory AND comprehension — including for younger learners who lack the skill to select appropriately themselves.

- **Moderator — Educational level for learner-generated highlighting**: College students benefit (g=0.39); K-12 students do not (g=0.24, ns). Explanation: younger learners highlight secondary details rather than main ideas; they have less developed meta-comprehension skills and less familiarity with expository text structure. Note: the moderation test was not statistically significant (QB=0.4, p=0.517), so this is a directional pattern, not a statistically confirmed moderation.

- **Moderator — Training**: Providing structured instruction on how to highlight (what counts as a main idea, examples of good/bad highlighting) dramatically improves outcomes (g=1.02 vs. g=0.36 without training). Training teaches learners to activate all three cognitive processes (selecting + organizing + integrating), not just selecting.

- **Moderator — Complementary strategy**: Adding notetaking or graphic organizers to highlighting (g=0.82) roughly doubles the effect vs. highlighting alone, because the second activity provides structure for organizing and integrating.

- **Moderator — Reviewing**: Reviewing previously highlighted material (one week later) shows no benefit (g=-0.06), likely because learners who highlighted poorly are reviewing poor material.

- **Moderator — Quality of highlighting**: Inappropriate highlighting (wrong content highlighted) is actively harmful (g=-0.70). Quality of what is highlighted matters as much as whether highlighting occurs.

- **Moderator — Education level for instructor-provided highlighting**: NOT a meaningful moderator — both college (g=0.41) and K-12 (g=0.48) students benefit equally from high-quality pre-highlighted text.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Learner-generated highlighting (students highlight important text using markers, underlines, computer tools) vs. control (read-only); OR instructor-provided highlighting (experimenter/instructor pre-highlights key material) vs. control (read-only)
- **Control/Comparison**: Read-only or study-only condition; no alternative study strategy
- **Outcomes measured**: Memory tests (recall of main ideas, selected statements) and/or comprehension tests (inference, transfer)
- **Study period**: 1938–2019; laboratory and classroom experiments
- **Attrition**: Not applicable to meta-analysis
- **Outliers**: Windsorized (one case)
- **Effect size metric**: Hedge's g (corrected from Cohen's d for small-sample bias)
- **Analysis**: Random-effects model (REML); moderator analyses via mixed-effects model; publication bias via Trim-and-Fill

## Limitations

- **Small k for some analyses**: Training (k=4) and complementary strategies (k=3) analyses rely on very few studies; prediction intervals are wide. Results directionally consistent but not robust.
- **Heterogeneity is high and unexplained**: I²=60–89% for most learner-generated highlighting analyses. Many sources of variation are unexamined (text length, topic difficulty, highlighting medium paper vs. computer, proportion of text highlighted, time on task).
- **K-12 studies narrow in grade range**: Most school studies used only 5th and 6th graders. Generalizability to younger or older school students is unknown.
- **No gray literature or theses**: Only published peer-reviewed articles were included. Potential upward bias if file-drawer problem exists (Trim-and-Fill was reassuring, but the test is imperfect).
- **Moderator testing underpowered**: The QB tests for educational level moderation were non-significant despite meaningful g differences (e.g., 0.39 vs. 0.24), suggesting insufficient studies to detect the moderation statistically.
- **Correlation assumed**: Assumed r=0.50 between memory and comprehension for combined-score computation; this is an unverified assumption across all studies.
- **Text type confound**: Nearly all studies used expository texts. Results may not generalize to narrative, procedural, or digital text formats.
- **No long-term retention data**: Outcomes are immediate posttests in most cases. Long-term effects of highlighting are unknown.
- **Interaction effects untested**: Whether training moderates the college vs. K-12 gap is not examined; small k prohibits this.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Students (college and K-12), predominantly laboratory and classroom settings with structured text reading tasks
- **Relevance to casual adult behavior change**: Medium. The instructor-provided highlighting and training findings are directly applicable to instructional design of digital learning content (e.g., a gamified guidebook, educational app, learning modules). The learner-generated finding (works for adults, not kids) is relevant for understanding when self-annotation is worth building into a system.
- **Red flags**: None — this is not a clinical population. No hard addiction or clinical disorder involvement.
- **Scope note**: Findings apply most strongly to academic text learning in structured settings. Applicability to informal reading (e.g., reading a guidebook without formal study intent) is an extrapolation; however, the instructor-provided highlighting results are the most externally valid, since they concern passive exposure to pre-formatted material — which is analogous to well-designed formatted content in any medium.

---

## Conclusions

"The results from the meta-analyses show that, on average, when learners generated their own highlighting, they improved on tests of memory but not on tests of comprehension, with average effect sizes of 0.36 and 0.20, respectively... When learners received high-quality highlighting, they improved on tests of memory and comprehension, both with an average effect size of 0.44." The key practical implication is that learner-generated highlighting alone is a weak strategy — effective only for surface memorization and only for adults — but instructor-provided highlighting (i.e., good content design) and highlighting-plus-training both produce above-average educational impacts by Hattie's (2009) benchmark of d=0.40.
