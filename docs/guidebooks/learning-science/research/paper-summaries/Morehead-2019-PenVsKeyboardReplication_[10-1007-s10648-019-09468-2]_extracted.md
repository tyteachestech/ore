# How Much Mightier Is the Pen than the Keyboard for Note-Taking? A Replication and Extension of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014)

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K.A., 2019
- **Study Type**: Direct replication + extension (2 experiments); also includes a continuously cumulating meta-analysis (CCMA) combining results with Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014)
- **Sample**: Experiment 1: n=188 undergraduates; Experiment 2: n=217 undergraduates; both at a large Midwestern US university
- **DOI**: 10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Longhand superiority did not consistently replicate**: Experiment 1 showed a significant longhand advantage for factual immediate test (d=0.45, p=0.04) but NOT for conceptual questions (d=0.13, p=0.31). Experiment 2 showed no significant difference on either factual (d=0.28, p=0.15) or conceptual (d=0.35, p=0.09, trend toward laptop superiority) questions. Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) had found the opposite pattern — longhand superiority for conceptual but not factual.

- **Finding 2 — CCMA (combined meta-analysis) shows small, non-significant effects**: Combining all four direct replications (2 from Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014, 2 from present study), the longhand advantage was small and non-significant for both factual questions (d=0.22, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.46]) and conceptual questions (d=0.17, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.40]).

- **Finding 3 — Note-taking itself may not improve encoding**: In Experiment 2, a no-notes group performed as well as (and sometimes numerically better than) all three note-taking groups on the immediate test. Note-taking did not consistently benefit encoding, regardless of method.

- **Finding 4 — No note-taking method differences for storage function (Experiment 2)**: After studying notes for 7 minutes before the delayed test, factual and conceptual test performance did not significantly differ among longhand, laptop, and eWriter groups (all method effects p > 0.14).

- **Finding 5 — eWriter performs similarly to longhand**: eWriter note-takers performed no differently from longhand on test performance in either experiment. Word count and note characteristics of eWriter users closely mirrored longhand users.

- **Finding 6 — Laptop users produce more words and higher verbatim overlap**: Word count was significantly higher for laptop than longhand (Exp 1: d=0.44; Exp 2: d=0.58). Verbatim overlap was significantly higher for laptop in Experiment 1 (d=0.44) but not significant in Experiment 2 (d=0.20). CCMA confirmed large, consistent laptop advantage on word count (d=0.77, 95% CI [0.57, 0.98]) and verbatim overlap (d=0.59, 95% CI [0.39, 0.79]).

- **Finding 7 — Note content (test relevance) predicts performance more than method**: Across both experiments, whether students captured test-relevant content in their notes was a better predictor of performance than note-taking method. Factual test relevance correlated with factual performance; conceptual test relevance correlated with conceptual performance.

|---------|---------|---------|--------|-------------|
| Factual immediate | 0.45* | 0.28 | 0.22 | [−0.01, 0.46] |
| Conceptual immediate | 0.13 | −0.35 (laptop) | 0.17 | [−0.07, 0.40] |

**Note characteristics (laptop vs. longhand; negative = laptop higher):**

| Outcome | CCMA d | CCMA 95% CI |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| Word count | −0.77 | [−0.98, −0.57] |
| Verbatim overlap | −0.59 | [−0.79, −0.39] |

**Delayed test (Experiment 1, no study session):** Method not significant for factual (F(2,151)=2.55, p=0.08, η²=0.01) or conceptual (F(2,151)=0.19, p=0.83, η²<0.01)

**Delayed test (Experiment 2, with note study session):** Method not significant for factual (F(2,168)=1.62, p=0.20, η²<0.01) or conceptual (F(2,168)=1.17, p=0.31, η²<0.01)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Verbatim overlap hypothesis (not supported)**: Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) hypothesized that higher verbatim overlap for laptop users explained their poorer performance. The CCMA shows verbatim overlap differences are large and consistent, but performance differences are small and non-significant — undermining the verbatim overlap mechanism.
- **Encoding function of note-taking is weak overall**: The no-notes group in Experiment 2 performed as well as note-takers, consistent with prior literature showing that notes primarily help via the storage function (reviewing before tests), not encoding.
- **Content quality as mechanism**: The best predictor of performance was whether the correct content appeared in notes (test relevance), not the modality of note-taking. This suggests note quality, not method, is the active ingredient.
- **Potential moderators noted but not tested**: Criterion format (handwritten vs. computer-based tests), whether materials include images (favoring longhand), student typing fluency, and distraction availability (all laptops in the study were distraction-free).

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Three note-taking methods (longhand on paper, laptop in MS Word, eWriter [Boogie Board Sync with stylus]) + no-notes group (Experiment 2 only). Notes taken away after lecture.
- **Materials**: TED Talk videos (~17 min each; same 5 videos as Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014). Mean speaking rate 164.80 words/min.
- **Test types**: Short-answer factual-recall and conceptual-application questions (20 points per video)
- **Follow-up period**: Experiment 1: immediate (same session) + delayed (2 days later, no note study). Experiment 2: immediate + delayed (2 days later, with 7-min note study session).
- **Attrition**: Experiment 1: 5 excluded (programming errors); 7 did not return for session 2. Experiment 2: 5 excluded (programming errors); 2 did not complete a session.
- **Power**: Planned for d=0.76 (Mueller & Oppenheimer's effect) with power=0.90

## Limitations

- **TED Talk materials differ from typical academic lectures**: TED talks are not explicitly organized to highlight key testable content; findings may not generalize to structured classroom lectures with emphasized main points
- **Distraction-free laptops**: Participants could use all MS Word functions but were in cubicles without internet. Ecological validity is limited — in real classrooms, laptop distraction is a significant confound
- **One-session note-taking**: Notes taken away immediately; students couldn't review until Experiment 2's delayed session. Real-world note-taking involves ongoing access
- **Near-floor performance**: Authors note performance was near floor for some videos — this compresses effect sizes and reduces power to detect differences
- **Student population only**: Undergraduate students at one Midwestern university; largely female (84% Exp 1, 90% Exp 2 under age 22)
- **Small effects even when significant**: The d=0.45 significant result in Experiment 1 for factual questions did not replicate in Experiment 2 (d=0.28, ns); the significant finding was likely sampling error

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: University students (US undergraduates, predominantly female, young adults 18–44)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Low-moderate — specific to academic note-taking context; but the underlying message (method matters less than content quality) has some broader applicability
- **Red flags**: None clinical; narrow academic population

## Conclusions

The authors conclude that "concluding which method is superior for improving the functions of note-taking seems premature." The CCMA reveals small, non-significant longhand advantages on test performance, while large laptop advantages on word count and verbatim overlap do not translate into performance differences. No note-taking method was significantly better than no notes at all on the immediate test. The best predictor of performance is whether students captured the relevant content in their notes, regardless of method.

## Extraction Notes

This paper is a high-quality direct replication that substantially weakens the "pen is mightier than the keyboard" claim popularized by Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014). The CCMA's 95% CI for conceptual questions crosses zero (d=0.17, CI [−0.07, 0.40]), and for factual questions barely excludes zero (d=0.22, CI [−0.01, 0.46]). For NotionRPG, the key takeaway is: note-taking modality is a weaker lever than note quality and content capture. Relevant if the guidebook addresses study technique recommendations — the evidence base for prescribing handwriting over typing is much weaker than commonly represented.
