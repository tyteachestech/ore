# Classroom Testing Meta-Analysis (Yang et al., 2021)

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Yang, C., Luo, L., Vadillo, M. A., Yu, R., & Shanks, D. R. (2021)
- **Study Type**: Meta-analysis (multilevel random-effects models)
- **Sample**: k=222 studies, n=48,478 students, 573 effect sizes
- **DOI**: 10.1037/bul0000309
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Overall testing effect**: Classroom quizzing significantly boosts student academic achievement to a medium extent.
  - Effect size: g=0.499 (95% CI [0.442, 0.557])
  - Heterogeneity: Q(572)=4,816, p<.001; substantial heterogeneity present
  - Publication bias: 5 methods used (PET-PEESE, trim-and-fill, 3PSM, publication year, publication status) — none revealed noteworthy bias; p-curve showed strong evidential value

- **Finding 2 — Comparison conditions**: Testing superiority differs by comparison group.
  - vs. No/filler activity: g=0.610 (95% CI [0.547, 0.673])
  - vs. Fewer questions: g=0.465 (95% CI [0.218, 0.711])
  - vs. Restudying: g=0.330 (95% CI [0.256, 0.404])
  - vs. Other elaborative strategies (concept mapping, note-taking, etc.): g=0.095 (95% CI [-0.005, 0.194]), p=.062 (marginal)

- **Finding 3 — Quiz format**: Test format significantly modulates learning gains; Q(8)=61.333, p<.001.
  - Matching: g=0.913; Fill-in-blank: g=0.773; Short answer: g=0.638; Multiple choice: g=0.567; Cued recall: g=0.316; Free recall: g=0.238

- **Finding 4 — Retrieval effort theory NOT supported**: Recognition tests (g=0.518) and recall tests (g=0.520) produced statistically equivalent enhancement, even when controlling for feedback provision (recall with FB: g=0.541; recognition with FB: g=0.607). Retrieval difficulty does not drive the classroom testing effect.

- **Finding 5 — Corrective feedback**: Providing feedback after quizzing significantly increases learning gains.
  - With feedback: g=0.537 (95% CI [0.469, 0.604])
  - Without feedback: g=0.374 (95% CI [0.278, 0.471])

- **Finding 6 — Dose-response for repetitions**: Positive linear relationship between number of test repetitions and learning gains. β=0.083 per additional repetition (95% CI [0.044, 0.122], p<.001).
  - 1 repetition: g=0.444; 2 repetitions: g=0.601; ≥3 repetitions: g=0.642; unlimited: g=0.762

- **Finding 7 — Treatment duration**: Learning gains increase linearly with treatment length. β=0.008 per class meeting (95% CI [0.002, 0.014], p<.001).
  - Single class: g=0.385; <1 semester: g=0.521; 1 semester: g=0.547; >1 semester: g=0.624

- **Finding 8 — Transfer to untested material**: Testing significantly benefits untested content, though to a smaller extent than tested content.
  - Tested material: g=0.512; Untested material: g=0.321

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Additional exposure** (SUPPORTED): Testing creates extra re-exposure to material. Evidence: larger benefit vs. no/filler (g=0.610) than vs. restudying (g=0.330); feedback enhances benefit (g=0.537 vs. g=0.374 without). However, cannot be the sole explanation because testing still outperforms restudying.

- **Transfer-appropriate processing** (SUPPORTED): Learning is more durable when acquisition and retrieval contexts match. Evidence: consistent formats (g=0.531) significantly outperform inconsistent (g=0.399).

- **Motivation** (SUPPORTED): Testing boosts motivation for subsequent learning. Evidence: significant linear increase with treatment duration (β=0.008/class meeting) and repetitions (β=0.083); multi-class effects (g=0.541) larger than single-class (g=0.389).

- **Retrieval effort** (NOT SUPPORTED): Difficult tests should outperform easy tests. Evidence against: recall (g=0.520) ≈ recognition (g=0.518); recall with feedback (g=0.541) vs. recognition with feedback (g=0.607) — if anything, in the wrong direction.

- **Key moderators**:
  - Feedback provision: significant (QB p<.001)
  - Format consistency (quiz ↔ exam): significant (QB p<.001)
  - Number of repetitions: significant positive linear trend (p<.001)
  - Treatment duration: significant positive linear trend (p<.001)
  - Quiz format (matching > MC > free recall): significant (QB p<.001)
  - Timepoint (postclass > preclass): significant (QB p<.001)
  - Experimental design (within > between): significant (QB p<.001)

- **Non-significant moderators**: Stake level, administration mode, gender, academic subject, instructor matching

---

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Class quizzing (retrieval practice) across any academic subject and education level
- **Control/Comparison**: No/filler activity, restudying, fewer questions, other elaborative strategies
- **Follow-up period**: Varied; most studies measured final exam performance at end of course/unit
- **Analysis model**: Multilevel random-effects meta-analysis using RVE (Robust Variance Estimation) to handle non-independent effects within studies
- **Publication bias tools**: 5 independent methods employed (PET-PEESE, 3PSM, trim-and-fill, publication year regression, published vs. unpublished comparison) — none indicated material bias
- **Blinding**: Not applicable (classroom intervention design)
- **Attrition**: Not applicable at meta-analytic level; study-level dropout not aggregated

---

## Limitations

- Majority of included studies used within-subjects designs (k=227 vs. 346 between-subjects); within-subjects produces inflated estimates (g=0.674 vs. g=0.415). The overall g=0.499 reflects this mix.
- Studies with continuing education populations are severely underrepresented (k=6); effect non-significant for this group.
- Collaborative testing vastly understudied (k=21 vs. 552 independent) — no firm conclusions possible.
- Subject coverage uneven (k=144 Psychology; k=1 Physics) — generalizability to some fields untested.
- Elaborative strategy comparisons (k=25) are too sparse to draw firm conclusions about testing vs. concept mapping or summarizing specifically.
- Moderator analyses cannot establish causality; confounds between moderators (e.g., format and level of education) are possible.
- Only 7 of 90+ reviews had conducted formal meta-analyses before this one — field may have residual researcher degrees of freedom in how outcomes are operationalized.

---

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Student populations (elementary through university); mostly Western samples
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium — mechanisms (retrieval practice, spaced repetition, feedback) are directly transferable to self-directed learning tools and habit trackers. Does not directly address adult behavior change outside education.
- **Red flags**: Entirely educational population; generalization to non-academic skill acquisition is inferential

---

## Conclusions

The meta-analysis synthesized 573 effects from 222 studies (n=48,478 students) and found that classroom quizzing reliably boosts academic achievement to a medium extent (g=0.499), with strong evidential value and minimal publication bias. Key practical takeaways: provide corrective feedback, increase quiz frequency, extend treatment duration across the semester, administer quizzes after rather than before instruction. Retrieval effort (difficulty) does not drive the effect — even easy recognition formats (MCQ) produce substantial gains (g=0.567). The effect applies across 18 academic subject categories, all school levels, and benefits both factual recall and higher-order conceptual learning.

---

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Retrieval Effort Theory**: Predicts harder tests (recall > recognition) should produce larger gains. NOT supported by this data. Recognition ≈ recall in effect size.
- **Transfer-Appropriate Processing**: Predicts larger gains when acquisition and test formats match. SUPPORTED: consistent > inconsistent format (g=0.531 vs. g=0.399).
- **Additional Exposure Account**: Testing as a form of re-exposure. PARTIALLY SUPPORTED: explains some variance but not all (testing > restudying, which also provides exposure).
- **Motivation Theory**: Testing motivates subsequent study. SUPPORTED: linear dose-response with treatment duration and repetitions.

## Quotable Passages

> "The meta-analysis found that testing (class quizzing) reliably enhances academic performance on criterion achievement assessments, and assessment scores are raised to a medium extent (g = 0.499). A positive effect of testing was detected in a majority (82.9%) of the included studies. The p-curve analysis demonstrated strong evidence supporting the existence of the overall effect." (Discussion, Summary of Research Findings)

> "Although g = 0.499 is only a medium-sized effect according to conventional descriptors, it is a notably large effect by the standards of educational interventions. An analysis of more than 800 educational meta-analyses found an average effect size of 0.40 (Hattie, 2009), and a meta-analysis of 141 high-quality randomized control trials... found a mean effect size of 0.06, with only one trial obtaining an effect size larger than 0.5." (Discussion, p. 22)

> "The current analysis observed that recognition tests (g = 0.518) produced equivalent enhancement as recall tests (g = 0.520). This finding... [fails] to support the retrieval effort explanation." (Discussion, Theoretical Implications, p. 24–25)

## Boundary Conditions & Population Caveats

- **Applies to**: K-12 and university students; both STEM and humanities subjects; in-classroom and online/digital quiz delivery; low- and high-stake contexts; male and female students equivalently
- **Does NOT apply to**: Continuing education professionals (insufficient evidence, k=6); clinical populations; non-academic skill learning
- **Unknown/untested**: Individual differences in working memory, learning disabilities, cultural contexts outside Western academia; additive effects of combining testing with elaborative strategies; optimal spacing between quizzes; long-term retention beyond course end; effects on intrinsic motivation longitudinally

---

## Extraction Notes

This is the most comprehensive meta-analysis on classroom testing effects to date and is unusually rigorous: 5 independent publication bias assessments, multilevel RVE to handle non-independent effects, 573 effects across 222 studies. The g=0.499 estimate is probably the best available for "testing vs. any control" in classroom contexts.

Key design note: within-subjects estimates (g=0.674) are substantially larger than between-subjects (g=0.415). The overall g=0.499 blends these, so it understates between-subjects effects but overstates them for some applications. For tool design purposes (e.g., gamified quiz features), the between-subjects estimate g=0.415 may be more conservative and appropriate.

The null result for stake level (high-stake g=0.441 vs. low-stake g=0.477) is practically important: low-stakes quizzing works as well as graded quizzes. This directly supports no-pressure "practice mode" quiz design.
