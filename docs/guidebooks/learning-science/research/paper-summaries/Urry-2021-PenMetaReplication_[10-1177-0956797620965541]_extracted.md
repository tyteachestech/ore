# Don't Ditch the Laptop Just Yet: A Direct Replication of Mueller and Oppenheimer's (2014) Study 1 Plus Mini Meta-Analyses Across Similar Studies

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Urry et al., 2021
- **Study Type**: Preregistered direct replication + Mini meta-analyses
- **Sample**: n=142 undergraduate students (replication); 8 studies total in meta-analyses
- **DOI**: 10.1177/0956797620965541
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Factual recall performance (replication)**: Laptop vs. longhand showed negligible effect in opposite direction to original
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.41, 0.25]
  - Sample: n=142 (68 laptop, 74 longhand)
  - Not significant (no p-value reported for replication)

- **Conceptual application performance (replication)**: Laptop vs. longhand showed negligible effect in opposite direction to original
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = -0.13, 95% CI [-0.45, 0.20]
  - Sample: n=142
  - Not significant (no p-value reported for replication)

- **Word count**: Laptop notes contained more words than longhand notes
  - Laptop: M = 230.69, SD = 133.87
  - Longhand: M = 136.16, SD = 66.26
  - t(94.63) = -5.22, p < .001

- **Verbatim overlap**: Laptop notes had more verbatim overlap with lecture than longhand
  - Laptop: M = 12.97%, SD = 6.53
  - Longhand: M = 8.13%, SD = 4.73
  - t(119.46) = -4.98, p < .001

- **Total quiz performance (meta-analysis)**: Longhand vs. laptop showed negligible boost
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.20]
  - z = 0.46, p = .645
  - Equivalent to -0.38 to 0.38 (z = -4.10, p <= .001)

- **Factual recall (meta-analysis)**: Negligible effect
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.19]

- **Conceptual application (meta-analysis)**: Negligible to very small effect
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.35]

- **Word count (meta-analysis)**: Laptop boosted word count to a large degree
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = -0.91, 95% CI [-1.18, -0.65]

- **Verbatim overlap (meta-analysis)**: Laptop boosted verbatim overlap to a large degree
  - Effect size: Hedges's g = -0.78, 95% CI [-1.03, -0.54]

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Replication Study (n=142):**
- Factual recall: Hedges's g = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.41, 0.25]
- Conceptual application: Hedges's g = -0.13, 95% CI [-0.45, 0.20]
- Word count: t(94.63) = -5.22, p < .001
- Verbatim overlap: t(119.46) = -4.98, p < .001

**Original Study (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014, Study 1; n=65):**
- Factual recall: Hedges's g = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.48, 0.50]
- Conceptual application: Hedges's g = 0.34, 95% CI [-0.16, 0.83]

**Mini Meta-Analyses (8 studies):**
- Total quiz performance: Hedges's g = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.20], z = 0.46, p = .645
- Factual recall: Hedges's g = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.19]
- Conceptual application: Hedges's g = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.35]
- Word count: Hedges's g = -0.91, 95% CI [-1.18, -0.65]
- Verbatim overlap: Hedges's g = -0.78, 95% CI [-1.03, -0.54]

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Original hypothesis (not supported in replication):**
- Mueller & Oppenheimer proposed that laptop note taking leads to more verbatim transcription, which impairs encoding and learning
- Replication found laptop notes had more verbatim overlap (consistent with original), but this did not translate to worse quiz performance

**Associations within studies:**
- Higher word count was associated with better quiz performance (mentioned in abstract but specific values not reported)
- Higher verbatim overlap was associated with worse quiz performance in original study, but "not robust" in replication (specific values not reported)

**No explicit moderator analyses reported**

## Study Design Details

**Replication:**
- **Intervention**: Participants randomly assigned to take notes on laptop or with pen and paper while watching one of five TED Talks (~15 min each)
- **Control/Comparison**: Between-subjects design (laptop vs. longhand)
- **Follow-up period**: Immediate quiz after brief distraction; no study opportunity
- **Attrition**: 3/145 excluded (2 provided no quiz responses, 1 unclear condition assignment)
- **Blinding**: Not reported
- **Power**: 80% power to detect Cohen's d of ±0.47 or larger

**Meta-analyses:**
- **Included studies**: 8 studies total (Mueller & Oppenheimer Studies 1 & 2, Morehead et al. 2019 [2 studies], current replication, Kirkland 2016, Luo et al. 2018, Mitchell & Zheng 2017)
- **Inclusion criteria**: "Very similar studies" (specific criteria not detailed)

## Limitations

- **Failed to replicate original finding**: Conceptual application advantage for longhand not found
- **Population**: Undergraduate students at single institution (Tufts University) — generalizability to other populations unknown
- **Immediate testing only**: No study opportunity, no delayed retention test (unlike Mueller & Oppenheimer Study 3)
- **No intervention fidelity check**: No verification that laptop users actually engaged in more verbatim transcription vs. other laptop activities
- **Meta-analysis heterogeneity**: Not reported (no I², τ², or Q statistics provided)
- **Brief lectures**: TED Talks (~15 min) may not generalize to longer classroom lectures
- **Artificial setting**: Lab study with pre-recorded lectures, not authentic classroom environment

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: General undergraduate students (college-age)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Low — addresses learning strategy choice in controlled academic context, not self-directed habit formation or behavior change
- **Red flags**: None — healthy undergraduate population, non-clinical

## Conclusions

"Until future research determines whether and when note-taking media influence academic performance, we conclude that students and professors who are concerned about detrimental effects of computer note taking on encoding information to be learned in lectures may not need to ditch the laptop just yet."

Key takeaways:
1. Replication failed to find advantage for longhand note taking on quiz performance (both factual and conceptual items)
2. Laptop notes consistently contain more words and more verbatim overlap with lecture content
3. Meta-analysis of 8 similar studies shows near-zero effect of note-taking medium on learning outcomes
4. Despite media attention and educator adoption of the original findings, the evidence does not robustly support banning laptops for note taking

## Extraction Notes

This is a high-quality preregistered replication that failed to replicate the key finding from an influential study. The meta-analyses strengthen the conclusion by showing the pattern holds across multiple studies. The paper is transparent about methods and provides full data/materials on OSF.

Notable: The original study's conceptual-application effect (g=0.34) had a confidence interval that crossed zero [-0.16, 0.83], indicating it was not statistically significant despite being described as a key finding. The replication's opposite-direction effect (g=-0.13) also crosses zero.

The paper does not report heterogeneity statistics (I², τ²) for the meta-analyses, which limits assessment of consistency across studies.

**STRICT EXTRACTION NOTES**: This extraction contains ONLY values stated verbatim in the source text. No effect sizes were calculated or inferred. Where the paper states "large degree" or "negligible" without providing numeric interpretation, that language is preserved. The t-statistics for word count and verbatim overlap are reported without converting to Cohen's d, as no d values were provided in the text.
