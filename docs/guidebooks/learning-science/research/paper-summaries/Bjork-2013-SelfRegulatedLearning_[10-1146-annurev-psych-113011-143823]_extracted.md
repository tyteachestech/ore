# Self-Regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions

## Study Overview

- **Citation**: Bjork, R.A., Dunlosky, J., & Kornell, N., 2013
- **Study Type**: Narrative review (empirical review article)
- **Sample**: No original sample — synthesizes findings across dozens of primary studies, several involving hundreds to thousands of college students (e.g., Kornell & Bjork 2007: n=472 UCLA undergrads; Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012: n=324 Kent State undergrads; Kornell 2009: n=120 college students; Crede & Phillips 2011 meta-analysis: k=67 samples, n>19,000 college students)
- **DOI**: 10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1 — Students use ineffective strategies and believe they work**: Surveys of college students consistently show rereading is the dominant study strategy (76% of students at UCLA; nearly identical rates at Kent State). When asked why they self-test, only 18% said it was to enhance learning; ~70% said it was to evaluate current knowledge. Most students believe rereading is more effective than self-testing — which is the reverse of the actual evidence.
  - Effect size: No original effect sizes. Cited: Crede & Phillips (2011) meta-analysis — MSLQ strategy subscales correlated only weakly and sometimes non-significantly with student GPA across 67 samples (n>19,000).
  - Only 11% of college students spontaneously reported using retrieval practice when asked in open-ended surveys (Karpicke et al. 2009).

- **Finding 2 — Metacognitive illusion of massed practice**: Across multiple experiments, 90% of participants showed better recall after spaced practice than massed practice, yet 72% reported afterward that massing was more effective (Kornell 2009). This is a documented metacognitive illusion driven by processing fluency: massed study feels easier, and people interpret ease as evidence of learning.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically; finding is directional from within-subject comparisons.

- **Finding 3 — Students systematically prefer blocked/massed over interleaved study**: When given free choice of study schedule for learning bird families (n=120), 75% of participants chose to block (mass) their study rather than interleave. Interleaved study produced objectively superior classification performance on the final test (Kornell & Bjork 2008a).
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically; finding is categorical preference.

- **Finding 4 — Fluency illusions corrupt judgments of learning (JOLs)**: Font size, volume, prior exposure (priming), and ease of processing reliably inflate learners' confidence that material is learned, even though these factors have no effect on actual retention. Specifically: words in larger fonts were rated as more memorable, words at louder volumes were rated as more memorable, and pre-exposed words were judged more confidently — none of which corresponded to actual recall differences (Kornell et al. 2011; Rhodes & Castel 2008, 2009).
  - Effect size: Kornell et al. (2011) — font size affected JOLs consistently; additional study trials (which actually drove learning) had no effect on JOLs.

- **Finding 5 — Delayed self-testing produces highly accurate metacognitive monitoring**: When JOLs were made after a delay using cue-only testing, metacognitive accuracy reached gamma >0.9. Immediate JOLs with full pairs present yielded gamma <0.6 — less than half as accurate (Nelson & Dunlosky 1991; Dunlosky & Nelson 1992).
  - Effect size: gamma correlation: delayed cue-only = >0.9 vs. other conditions = <0.6.

- **Finding 6 — Stability bias leads to systematic miscalibration**: Learners act as if their memory state is static — underestimating how much they can improve with future study (underconfidence about learning potential) while simultaneously overestimating current knowledge (overconfidence). This bias also applies to forgetting: participants' JOL predictions were insensitive to retention interval even when told they would be tested after a week or a year (Koriat et al. 2004).
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically; described as "striking" and replicated across conditions.

- **Finding 7 — Pre-test errors enhance learning more than pure study**: Generating a wrong answer (8 seconds) before studying the correct answer (5 seconds) produced better cued recall than studying the correct answer alone for 13 seconds (Kornell et al. 2009). Despite this, participants attributed their learning to the study condition, not the test condition.
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically; finding is directional from within-subject comparison. Replicated across multiple labs (Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012; Hays et al. 2012; Huelser & Metcalfe 2012).

- **Finding 8 — Hypercorrection effect: high-confidence errors are corrected best**: Feedback was most effective following errors made with high confidence vs. low confidence (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001). Effect replicated including at educationally realistic retention intervals (Butler et al. 2011; Metcalfe & Finn 2011).
  - Effect size: Not reported numerically; replicated across multiple studies.

## Effect Sizes

No original data — narrative review. Effect sizes cited from primary sources:

- **JOL accuracy (gamma correlation)**: delayed cue-only condition = >0.9; other conditions = <0.6 (Nelson & Dunlosky 1991; Dunlosky & Nelson 1992)
- **MSLQ strategy subscales vs. GPA**: low, sometimes non-significant (Crede & Phillips 2011 meta-analysis, k=67, n>19,000)
- **Massing vs. spacing recall advantage**: 90% of participants recalled more after spacing than massing (Kornell 2009, n=120), but 72% believed massing was more effective
- **Interleaving vs. blocking preference**: 75% of participants chose blocked study despite interleaved producing superior test performance (Kornell & Bjork 2008a, n=120)
- **Pre-error generation advantage**: 8 sec wrong + 5 sec correct > 13 sec correct alone (Kornell et al. 2009; no d reported)
- **Font size on JOLs**: reliable effect on JOL; no effect on actual recall (Kornell et al. 2011)
- **Retrieval practice use (self-report)**: 11% spontaneously report using it; ~42% endorse it when given forced-choice (Karpicke et al. 2009)
- **Dissonance in interleaving study (Kornell & Bjork 2008a, Figure 5)**: majority of n=120 judged massing as more effective, even though majority showed objectively better interleaved performance

## Mechanisms & Moderators

- **Fluency heuristic**: Learners rely on the subjective ease of processing (encoding fluency, retrieval fluency, perceptual fluency) as a proxy for learning. Conditions that feel easy — massed practice, re-reading, large fonts — inflate JOLs without improving actual learning. Conversely, desirable difficulties feel hard but enhance long-term retention and transfer.
  - Specific fluency subtypes: retrieval fluency, encoding fluency, perceptual fluency, fluency of induction (for category learning).

- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork 1994)**: Spacing, interleaving, generation, variation, and self-testing all reduce current performance (introduce errors, slow apparent acquisition) while enhancing long-term retention and transfer. The mechanism is that these conditions require the learner to reconstruct or retrieve — strengthening memory traces — rather than simply re-process familiar material.

- **Stability bias**: Learners treat current memory state as static, failing to account for: (a) future improvement from additional study, (b) forgetting over time. This produces dual miscalibration — underconfidence about future learning potential and overconfidence about what is retained right now.

- **Hindsight and foresight biases**: Hindsight bias inflates perceived familiarity when material is at hand (reading a textbook chapter); foresight bias leads learners to assume they will recall an answer on a test because it seems natural when the answer is visible during study.

- **Belief-based vs. experience-based cues**: Metacognitive judgments are dominated by experience-based cues (what is immediately felt) over belief-based cues (what is known abstractly). Even when learners consciously know that forgetting happens over time, this belief does not influence JOLs unless made explicitly salient.

- **Study agenda setting**: Students develop agendas to guide study allocation. These can include discrepancy reduction (study the hardest items) or region of proximal learning (study the easiest items first). Habitual biases — such as reading left-to-right through a list — can override strategic allocation entirely.

- **Moderator — retrieval-induced forgetting**: Retrieval practice enhances recall of retrieved items but can reduce accessibility of competing items associated with the same cues. The net effect depends on the structure of the test and the relationship between practiced and unpracticed items. Multiple-choice formats may mitigate this by exposing all alternatives.

- **Moderator — interleaving benefits for inductive vs. rote learning**: For category induction (e.g., learning artists' styles), interleaving forces comparison across categories, highlighting discriminating features. For rote associations, spacing benefits appear driven mainly by temporal spacing per se.

## Limitations

- **No original data**: The review synthesizes published research but reports no new experiments. All specific effect sizes are borrowed from cited primary sources; the paper itself cannot be evaluated for internal validity as an empirical study.
- **Predominantly college student samples**: Nearly all cited studies use undergraduate participants in controlled lab settings. Generalizability to non-student adults, older learners, workplace learning, or informal self-directed contexts is assumed but not tested.
- **Ecological validity**: Lab paradigms (word pairs, paired associates, flashcard simulations) may not translate to the complexity of real-world skill or knowledge acquisition.
- **Publication bias in cited literature**: As a narrative review, the paper selects and synthesizes findings. No systematic literature search or quality appraisal was conducted; findings supporting "desirable difficulties" may be overrepresented relative to null results.
- **Self-report limitations**: Survey-based evidence of student beliefs relies on student recall and honesty; actual study behavior may differ from reported behavior (and the paper itself documents this discrepancy).
- **Causal claims from correlational survey data**: Cited correlations between strategy use and GPA (e.g., Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012) are observational; the paper appropriately does not claim that teaching better strategies directly raises GPA, but this nuance is easy to miss.
- **No conflict of interest stated**: Authors declare no affiliations or financial holdings that could bias the review.

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Primarily college students (traditional undergrads). Secondary: general learner (the review's framing is explicitly lifespan and self-directed learning)
- **Relevance to casual adult behavior change**: High. The central principles — that fluency is a misleading cue, that retrieval practice beats re-reading, that desirable difficulties improve long-term retention — apply to any self-directed learner, not only students. The paper explicitly argues for lifespan applicability.
- **Red flags**: None. This is not a clinical population. No addiction, compulsive disorder, or therapeutic context. Directly applicable to casual adult skill/knowledge acquisition.
- **Caveat**: Most effect sizes are from constrained lab tasks (word pairs, foreign vocabulary, bird classification). Transfer to open-ended learning goals (e.g., learning a skill, understanding a complex domain) is extrapolated rather than demonstrated.

## Conclusions

"Research on learning, memory, and metacognitive processes has provided evidence that people often have a faulty mental model of how they learn and remember, making them prone to both misassessing and mismanaging their own learning."

The authors conclude that learners consistently prefer and believe in strategies (re-reading, massed study) that feel effective but produce inferior long-term retention compared to desirable difficulties (spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice, generation). The gap between perceived and actual effectiveness is not random noise but a systematic bias driven by fluency: easier feels better, but harder is better for durable learning. Correcting this requires explicit metacognitive education, not just experience, since everyday learning experience does not naturally teach these distinctions.

## Named Frameworks

- **Desirable Difficulties (Bjork 1994)**
  - Components: Spacing, interleaving, generation, variation in conditions of learning, self-testing
  - Research alignment:
    - Spacing → spacing effect (well-established across memory literature)
    - Interleaving → contextual interference / category induction advantage
    - Generation → generation effect; errorful generation enhancing learning
    - Self-testing → retrieval practice / testing effect (Roediger & Butler 2011)

- **Nelson & Narens (1990) Metacognitive Framework**
  - Components: Monitoring (JOLs, ease-of-learning judgments, feeling-of-knowing, confidence in retrieved answers, source monitoring) and Control (item selection, termination of study, selection of processing kind, retrieval practice)
  - Research alignment: Framework organizes the paper's review; monitoring inaccuracies drive control failures
