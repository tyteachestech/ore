# The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard: Advantages of Longhand Over Laptop Note Taking

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Mueller, P. A., & Oppenheimer, D. M., 2014
- **Study Type**: Experimental (3 studies)
- **Sample**: Study 1: n=67 (2 excluded, final analyzed unclear from text), Study 2: n=151, Study 3: n=109
- **DOI**: 10.1177/0956797614524581
- **PMID**: Not reported

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1: Conceptual performance advantage for longhand (Study 1)**
  - On conceptual-application questions, laptop participants performed significantly worse (M = -0.156, SD = 0.915) than longhand participants (M = 0.154, SD = 1.08)
  - Effect size: F(1, 55) = 9.99, p = .03, eta-p-squared = .13
  - Sample: F-test indicates df suggests n analyzed, not explicitly stated

- **Finding 2: No difference on factual recall (Study 1)**
  - On factual-recall questions, participants performed equally well across conditions (laptop: M = 0.021, SD = 1.31; longhand: M = 0.009, SD = 1.02)
  - Effect size: F(1, 55) = 0.014, p = .91
  - Sample: F-test df

- **Finding 3: Longhand notes contain less verbatim transcription (Study 1)**
  - Laptop notes contained an average of 14.6% verbatim overlap with the lecture (SD = 7.3%), whereas longhand notes averaged only 8.8% (SD = 4.8%)
  - Effect size: t(63) = -3.77, p < .001, d = 0.94
  - Sample: n=65 (inferred from t-test df of 63)

- **Finding 4: Longhand notes are shorter (Study 1)**
  - Participants who took longhand notes wrote significantly fewer words (M = 173.4, SD = 70.7) than those who typed (M = 309.6, SD = 116.5)
  - Effect size: t(48.58) = -5.63, p < .001, d = 1.4, corrected for unequal variances
  - Sample: n not explicitly stated for this analysis

- **Finding 5: More notes help, but verbatim overlap hurts (Study 1)**
  - Overall, participants who took more notes performed better, beta = 0.34, p = .023, partial R2 = .08
  - Those whose notes had less verbatim overlap with the lecture also performed better, beta = -0.43, p = .005, partial R2 = .12
  - Effect size: Full model F(3, 61) = 4.25, p = .009, R2 = .17
  - Sample: n inferred from df

- **Finding 6: Conceptual performance advantage replicated (Study 2)**
  - On conceptual-application questions, longhand participants performed better (z-score M = 0.28, SD = 1.04) than laptop-nonintervention participants (z-score M = -0.15, SD = 0.85)
  - Effect size: F(1, 89) = 11.98, p = .017, eta-p-squared = .12
  - Sample: n=151 total, F-test df suggests subset analyzed

- **Finding 7: Instructional intervention ineffective (Study 2)**
  - Scores for laptop-intervention participants (z-score M = -0.11, SD = 1.02) did not significantly differ from those for either laptop-nonintervention (p = .91) or longhand (p = .29) participants
  - The instruction to not take verbatim notes was completely ineffective at reducing verbatim content (p = .97)
  - Sample: n=151

- **Finding 8: Interaction between note-taking medium and study opportunity (Study 3)**
  - Significant interaction between note-taking medium and opportunity to study
  - Effect size: F(1, 105) = 5.63, p = .019, eta-p-squared = .05
  - Sample: n=109

- **Finding 9: Longhand + study condition superior (Study 3)**
  - Participants who took longhand notes and were able to study them performed significantly better (z-score M = 0.19) than participants in any of the other conditions (z-score Ms = -0.10, -0.02, -0.08)
  - Effect size: t(105) = 3.11, p = .002, d = 0.64
  - Sample: n=109

- **Finding 10: Indirect effects on conceptual questions (Study 3)**
  - For conceptual questions, there were significant indirect effects on performance via both word count (-0.17, 95% CI = [-0.29, -0.08]) and verbatim overlap (0.13, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.15])
  - Sample: n=109

## Effect Sizes (Raw Values)

**Study 1:**
- **Conceptual questions**: F(1, 55) = 9.99, p = .03, η²p = .13
- **Factual recall**: F(1, 55) = 0.014, p = .91
- **Verbatim overlap**: t(63) = -3.77, p < .001, d = 0.94
- **Word count**: t(48.58) = -5.63, p < .001, d = 1.4
- **Word count predicting performance**: β = 0.34, p = .023, partial R² = .08
- **Verbatim overlap predicting performance**: β = -0.43, p = .005, partial R² = .12
- **Full mediation model**: F(3, 61) = 4.25, p = .009, R² = .17
- **Interrater reliability**: alpha = .89

**Study 2:**
- **Conceptual questions (longhand vs laptop-nonintervention)**: F(1, 89) = 11.98, p = .017, η²p = .12
- **Laptop-intervention vs laptop-nonintervention**: p = .91
- **Laptop-intervention vs longhand**: p = .29
- **Verbatim content reduction by instruction**: p = .97

**Study 3:**
- **Interaction (medium × study opportunity)**: F(1, 105) = 5.63, p = .019, η²p = .05
- **Longhand + study vs other conditions**: t(105) = 3.11, p = .002, d = 0.64
- **Indirect effect via word count (conceptual)**: -0.17, 95% CI [-0.29, -0.08]
- **Indirect effect via verbatim overlap (conceptual)**: 0.13, 95% CI [0.02, 0.15]
- **Main effect of note-taking medium**: Not reported as significant
- **Main effect of opportunity to study**: Not reported as significant

## Mechanisms & Moderators

**Mechanism 1: Verbatim transcription reduces depth of processing**
- Laptop use facilitates verbatim transcription because most students can type significantly faster than they can write
- Typing may impair the encoding benefits seen in past note-taking studies
- Verbatim note taking has generally been seen to indicate relatively shallow cognitive processing
- Evidence: Those whose notes had less verbatim overlap performed better (β = -0.43, p = .005)

**Mechanism 2: Encoding vs. external storage**
- The encoding hypothesis suggests that the processing that occurs during the act of note taking improves learning and retention
- The external-storage hypothesis touts the benefits of the ability to review material
- These two theories are not incompatible; students who both take and review their notes likely profit from both approaches
- Evidence: Study 3 found interaction suggesting longhand notes benefit more from review opportunity

**Moderator 1: Question type**
- Detriments due to verbatim note taking are more prominent for conceptual than for factual items
- Evidence: Study 1 showed significant difference on conceptual questions (F = 9.99, p = .03) but not factual (F = 0.014, p = .91)

**Moderator 2: Study opportunity**
- Study opportunity interacts with note-taking medium
- Evidence: Study 3 found significant interaction F(1, 105) = 5.63, p = .019
- Longhand notes showed greater benefit from review opportunity

**Non-moderator: Instructional intervention**
- Telling students not to take notes verbatim did not prevent this deleterious behavior
- Evidence: Study 2 instruction had no effect on verbatim content (p = .97) or performance (p = .91 vs nonintervention, p = .29 vs longhand)

## Study Design Details

**Study 1:**
- **Intervention**: Students watched TED Talks (slightly over 15 min) and took notes either on laptops or in longhand notebooks
- **Control/Comparison**: Laptop vs. longhand note taking
- **Follow-up period**: Approximately 30 min had elapsed since the end of the lecture (filled with two 5-min distractor tasks and a reading span task)
- **Materials**: 5 TED Talks selected for length and to cover topics that would be interesting but not common knowledge
- **Setting**: Princeton University, laptops disconnected from Internet, full-size keyboards
- **Participants**: 67 students (33 male, 33 female, 1 unknown), 2 excluded
- **Scoring**: First author scored all responses blind to condition; independent rater also scored (initial interrater reliability alpha = .89)

**Study 2:**
- **Intervention**: Added laptop-intervention condition with explicit instruction not to take verbatim notes
- **Conditions**: Longhand, laptop-nonintervention, laptop-intervention
- **Setting**: University of California, Los Angeles Anderson Behavioral Lab
- **Participants**: Final N = 151 (35 male)

**Study 3:**
- **Design**: 2 (laptop, longhand) × 2 (study, no study) design
- **Intervention**: Half of participants given opportunity to review notes before testing
- **Follow-up**: Text states "after a week's delay" in General Discussion
- **Setting**: University of California, Los Angeles Anderson Behavioral Lab
- **Participants**: Final N = 109 (27 male)

## Limitations

**Stated limitations:**
- None explicitly discussed in a dedicated limitations section

**Obvious unstated limitations:**
- Study 1 had only ~30 min delay between lecture and test, limiting generalizability to longer retention intervals
- All studies used TED Talks as stimuli, which may not generalize to actual college lectures in various disciplines or to more technical content
- Study populations limited to Princeton and UCLA students
- Internet was disabled on laptops, which doesn't reflect typical laptop use in real classrooms
- No measurement of individual typing speed or handwriting speed differences
- Short-term experimental setting doesn't capture cumulative effects over a semester
- Study 2 and 3 don't report separate breakdowns for factual vs. conceptual questions in results
- No exploration of why the instructional intervention failed in Study 2
- Limited detail on exactly which participants were analyzed in each study (some df don't match total n)
- No correction for multiple comparisons despite multiple outcome measures

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: College students (Princeton University, UCLA)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: Medium - note-taking is a voluntary study behavior, but this is an academic performance context rather than general habit formation
- **Red flags**: None - general student population studying normal academic material, not clinical or addiction context

## Conclusions

"The studies we report here show that laptop use can negatively affect performance on educational assessments, even -- or perhaps especially -- when the computer is used for its intended function of easier note taking. Although more notes are beneficial, at least to a point, if the notes are taken indiscriminately or by mindlessly transcribing content, as is more likely the case on a laptop than when notes are taken longhand, the benefit disappears. Indeed, synthesizing and summarizing content rather than verbatim transcription can serve as a desirable difficulty toward improved educational outcomes."

"Even when allowed to review notes after a week's delay, participants who had taken notes with laptops performed worse on tests of both factual content and conceptual understanding, relative to participants who had taken notes longhand."

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

**Framework**: Encoding-Storage Paradigm (DiVesta & Gray, 1972; Kiewra, 1989)
- Components:
  - Encoding (processing during note taking improves learning and retention)
  - External storage (ability to review material)
- Research alignment (apparent from text):
  - Encoding → depth of processing, generative vs. nongenerative note taking
  - External storage → review opportunity, Study 3 study condition

**Framework**: Levels of Processing (Craik & Lockhart, 1972)
- Components:
  - Shallow processing (verbatim copying)
  - Deep processing (summarizing, paraphrasing, concept mapping)
- Research alignment (apparent from text):
  - Shallow processing → verbatim note taking, laptop use
  - Deep processing → generative note taking, longhand use

## Extraction Notes

This is a well-executed series of three experiments with clear statistical reporting. The mechanism (verbatim transcription) is well-tested through mediation analyses. Study 2's finding that instructing students not to transcribe verbatim is completely ineffective (p = .97) is particularly valuable for practical application. Study 3 extends to a week delay and includes review opportunity, strengthening ecological validity. Effect sizes are moderate (η²p around .12-.13 for main effects). The paper reports all statistics clearly and includes process measures (word count, verbatim overlap) that illuminate the mechanism. Note that some sample sizes must be inferred from degrees of freedom as they aren't always explicitly stated in results sections.
