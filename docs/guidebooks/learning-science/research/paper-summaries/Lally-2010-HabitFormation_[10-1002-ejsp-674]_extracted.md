# How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world

## Study Overview
- **Citation**: Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C.H., Potts, H.W., Wardle, J. (2010)
- **Study Type**: Longitudinal, prospective cohort
- **Sample**: n=96 adults (82 with sufficient data; 39 with good model fit), predominantly postgraduate students (mean age 27, range 21-45), 66 women/30 men, 65% White, UK/European
- **DOI**: 10.1002/ejsp.674
- **PMID**: Not provided (published in European Journal of Social Psychology)

## Key Findings

- **Finding 1: Asymptotic curve models habit formation across individuals**
  - 62 of 82 participants' automaticity scores fit a nonlinear asymptotic model (Mitscherlich's law: y = a - be^(-cx))
  - Asymptotic model (median R² = 0.88, Q1:Q3 = 0.84:0.94) provided significantly better fit than linear model (median R² = 0.72, Q1:Q3 = 0.55:0.80) among good-fit participants (Z = -5.765, p < 0.001)
  - Across all 82 participants, nonlinear (median R² = 0.74) significantly outperformed linear (median R² = 0.66), Z = -3.019, p = 0.003
  - Effect: Early repetitions produce larger gains in automaticity; gains diminish as behavior approaches plateau

- **Finding 2: Median 66 days to reach 95% of asymptote (plateau), with substantial individual variation**
  - Among n=39 with good model fit: median = 66 days, IQR = 39-102 days, range = 18-254 days
  - Asymptote values (automaticity at plateau): median = 35, IQR = 29-43, range = 21-48 on 42-point SRHI automaticity subscale
  - Correlation between time-to-95%-asymptote and asymptote level: r = 0.38, p = 0.02 (reaching higher plateaus takes longer)

- **Finding 3: Consistency of behavior performance predicts model fit and asymptote trajectory**
  - Among good-fit participants (n=39): median compliance = 88%, IQR = 80-94%
  - Good-fit participants performed behavior more consistently than poor-fit groups (e.g., R² < 0.7: median compliance = 60%, p < 0.05 via Mann-Whitney)
  - Correlation between percent compliance and R²: r = 0.34, p = 0.035 (stronger relationship between consistency and asymptotic shape)
  - Those with asymptote < 21 (non-habit threshold): median compliance = 61%, IQR = 37-73%

- **Finding 4: Approximately 50% of motivated participants did not achieve habit formation**
  - Of initial 96: 82 provided sufficient data; 62 could be modeled; 39 achieved good fit (48% of those with adequate data)
  - Approximately half of participants with daily choice to perform behavior did not perform consistently enough to form habit (asymptote ≥ 21)
  - 14 participants dropped out before day 60

- **Finding 5: Single missed opportunities have minimal impact on habit formation**
  - 140 missed opportunities identified across 55 participants
  - Automaticity decrease immediately after missed opportunity: 0.29 points on 42-point scale (negligible)
  - Increase from before-omission to after-reperformance (when data available, n=67): 0.55 points (not significant via Wilcoxon signed-rank test)
  - Comparison: Two-day increase with consistent performance = 0.79 points; with one missed day = 0.55 points (comparable, nonsignificant difference)
  - Timing of omission (early vs. late in habit formation): no correlation with automaticity change, r = 0.099, p = 0.246, n=140

- **Finding 6: No significant differences in habit formation parameters across behavior type (eating, drinking, exercise)**
  - Asymptote (median): Eating = 34 (IQR 27-44), Drinking = 35 (IQR 29-43), Exercise = 35 (IQR 31-44); Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.708
  - Time to 95% asymptote (median): Eating = 65 days (IQR 35-106), Drinking = 59 days (IQR 39-75), Exercise = 91 days (IQR 44-118); Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.328
  - Compliance differed significantly: Drinking = 93% (IQR 86-96) vs. Eating = 80% (IQR 77-90), Exercise = 86% (IQR 79-92); p = 0.046
  - Exercise group took 1.5x longer median time despite no statistical significance (trends support complexity hypothesis)

- **Finding 7: Measure reliability and repeated-measures validity**
  - Cronbach's α for SRHI automaticity subscale at baseline = 0.90
  - No correlation between number of questionnaire completions and any curve parameter, suggesting no response inflation from daily completion
  - Median questionnaire completions: 50 per participant (IQR 35-64)

## Mechanisms & Moderators

### Mechanisms Proposed by Authors
- **Context stability + repetition → automaticity transfer**: When a behavior is repeated in a consistent environmental context (cue), the association between that cue and the behavior strengthens incrementally. Control gradually transfers from conscious planning and intention to environmental cues triggering automatic response.
- **Asymptotic learning curve**: Early repetitions produce large gains in automaticity due to initial cue-response link formation; subsequent repetitions produce smaller gains as the system approaches its asymptotic ceiling (diminishing returns).
- **Intrinsic reward sufficiency**: No extrinsic reinforcement necessary when behaviors are self-selected (intrinsically rewarding). Participants were offered fixed payment (£30, independent of performance), suggesting behavior choice itself suffices for habit formation.

### Key Moderator Insights
- **Consistency matters more than complexity**: The strongest predictor of whether a participant's data fit the asymptotic model (r = 0.34, p = 0.035) was behavioral consistency, not behavior type. Those with "good fit" maintained 88% compliance; those with poor fit ~60%.
- **Complexity trend without significance**: Exercise behaviors showed a 1.5x longer median time-to-plateau (91 vs. 59-65 days) and lower compliance, but Kruskal-Wallis was not significant (p = 0.328). Authors acknowledge "not powered for sub-group analyses."
- **Omission resilience is behavior-dependent**: Authors note "missing one opportunity does not preclude habit formation, but missing a week's worth of opportunities reduces likelihood of future performance." Single-day omissions benign; sustained gaps detrimental.

## Study Design Details

- **Intervention**: Participants self-selected one eating (n=27), drinking (n=31), or exercise (n=34) behavior to perform daily in response to a salient, fixed daily cue (e.g., "after breakfast") for 84 days. Examples: "eating a piece of fruit with lunch," "drinking a bottle of water with lunch," "running for 15 minutes before dinner."
- **Control/Comparison**: No formal control group. Internal comparison: participants' own baseline automaticity (median = 3/42 at start) vs. plateau automaticity (median = 35/42).
- **Measurement**: Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI), 12-item Likert scale (1-7 per item). Automaticity sub-scale: 7 items only (excluded frequency/history and identity items), scored 0-42. Completed daily online. Behavioral report (yes/no performed behavior) also daily.
- **Follow-up period**: 84 days (12 weeks). Participants attempted daily performance; data recorded only on days they logged in.
- **Attrition**: 14/96 dropped out before day 60; 82 provided adequate data; 62 could be modeled; 39 had good model fit (48%). Completeness: median 47/84 days logged (56% direct; 76 days total with retrospective 3-day lookback).
- **Blinding**: Not applicable (open-label behavior change study).
- **Payment**: £30 fixed payment (not contingent on performance), offered to offset time burden.

## Limitations

**Stated Limitations:**
- Small sample with good model fit (n=39) limits power for subgroup analyses (e.g., behavior type comparison shows trends but not significance)
- Incomplete daily logging: participants did not consistently enter data (median 47/84 days logged); authors unable to directly assess repetitions vs. automaticity relationship, substituting "days of study" as proxy
- SRHI never previously used as daily repeated measure; uncertain whether daily questionnaire completion affects response patterns (authors found no correlation with compliance, suggesting minimal bias, but limitation remains)
- Potential ceiling effects: Although disputed by authors (only 7/39 participants reached max score of 42), asymptote values as high as 48 estimated for some individuals exceed scale maximum
- Measure validity: SRHI automaticity subscale developed ad-hoc for this study (excluding frequency and identity items); unknown whether subset captures automaticity as rigorously as full SRHI

**Unstated but Apparent Limitations:**
- **Sample composition bias**: 82% postgraduate students, 65% White, 69% female. Findings may not generalize to diverse populations, younger ages, or those with lower education/motivation
- **Intrinsic motivation assumption**: Participants self-selected behaviors they "wanted to perform." Results may not apply to behaviors people are forced or externally incentivized to adopt (different reward structure)
- **Relatively short follow-up**: Some participants reached plateau within study window, but others did not (range 18-254 days). 84-day study window insufficient for 254-day trajectories
- **Real-world validity concerns**: Structured daily logging requirement may artificially inflate consistency (demand characteristics; awareness of being monitored often increases behavior adherence)
- **No objective automaticity measures**: Relying entirely on self-report. No behavioral markers (reaction time, cognitive load, eye-tracking, neural imaging) to validate subjective automaticity claims
- **Missing moderator data**: No assessment of motivation level, personality, prior habit strength, or environmental barriers—all potentially important moderators

## Population Scope Assessment

- **Population**: Adults (primarily postgraduate students; 96% age 21-45)
- **Relevance to casual behavior change**: **High** — Study directly addresses how new healthy habits are acquired in everyday life with minimal external structure. Generalizable to habit-formation contexts in personal health management, productivity, and lifestyle change.
- **Scope for NotionRPG**: **Applicable** — The 66-day median to automaticity (95% of asymptote) directly informs gamified habit-tracking systems. Findings suggest that:
  - Habit automaticity develops gradually over weeks-to-months, not days (unlike popular "21-day myth")
  - Consistency matters more than daily perfection (single missed days tolerable)
  - ~50% of even motivated users fail to maintain consistency—critical for designing support/reminders
  - Simple (drinking) vs. complex (exercise) behaviors follow same trajectory (no differential intervention needed by complexity)
- **Red flags**: None for NotionRPG application. Population is healthy, general adults with intrinsic motivation (not clinical, not addiction/compulsion). Findings are robust.

## Conclusions

"Repeating a behaviour in response to a cue appeared to be enough for many people to develop automaticity for that behaviour. Although consistency in repetition is required, the degree of consistency is not yet known. There was variation both in the maximum automaticity reached and the time taken to plateau." (p. 215)

"The range of times to reach a plateau shows that it can take a large number of repetitions for an individual to reach their highest level of automaticity for some behaviours, and therefore creating new habits will require self-control to be maintained for a significant period before the desired behaviours acquire the necessary automaticity to be performed without self-control." (p. 215)

Key takeaway: Habit formation is nonlinear, takes weeks-to-months of consistent repetition, tolerates occasional lapses, and shows extreme individual variation in timeline. The asymptotic model (early gains, diminishing returns, plateau) better describes real-world habit development than linear frequency assumptions.

## Named Frameworks (if applicable)

- **Hull's Asymptotic Model** (Hull, 1943, 1951; adapted/tested in this paper)
  - Components: Automaticity increases via asymptotic curve: y = a - be^(-cx)
    - a = asymptote (maximum automaticity reachable)
    - b = initial gap (difference between asymptote and starting point)
    - c = rate constant (speed of approach to asymptote)
    - x = repetitions (or days in this study)
  - Research alignment:
    - Asymptote (a) → behavioral ceiling / maximum habit strength
    - Rate constant (c) → learning speed (fast for simple, slower for complex)
    - Consistency moderator → "context stability" prerequisite for cue-response linkage
    - Single-omission tolerance → demonstrates nonlinear learning, not catastrophic loss

- **Cue-Response Association (Verplanken, 2006; Wood & Neal, 2007)**
  - Components: Repeated behavior in consistent context strengthens cue→action link; control transfers from prefrontal planning to automatic response
  - Research alignment:
    - "Same situation each day" instruction → cue consistency operationalized
    - Automaticity increase → gradual transfer of behavioral control
    - Exercise (complex) trend toward longer plateau → supports Wood & Neal proposition that complex behaviors may develop goal-directed automaticity (flexible, intention-sensitive) rather than pure habit (inflexible)

## Quotable Passages

> "On average, across all participants with sufficient data... the fit of the asymptotic curve was superior to the linear model. We are therefore reasonably confident that the asymptotic curve reflects a generalized habit formation process. Early repetitions result in larger increases in automaticity than those later in the habit formation process, and there is a point at which the behaviour cannot become more automatic even with further repetition." (Results, p. 1005-1006)

**Relevance**: Directly validates nonlinear habituation model over frequency-based assumptions.

---

> "The average modelled time to plateau in this sample was 66 days, but the range was from 18 to 254 days. We were only able to find one statement in the literature discussing how long it takes to for a habit. This was from Ronis, Yates, and Kirscht (1988), who argued that a behaviour is habitual once it has been 'performed frequently (at least twice a month) and extensively (at least 10 times)' (p. 213). Our study has shown that it is likely to take much longer than this for a repeated behaviour to reach its maximum level of automaticity." (Discussion, p. 1006)

**Relevance**: Challenges the folk-science "21-day habit" myth with empirical evidence.

---

> "Immediately following a missed opportunity, automaticity decreased by an average of 0.29 points on the SRHI automaticity subscale; a very small decrease... These results suggest that missing a day resulted in a nonsignificantly lower SRHI score the following day, but there were no longer-term costs associated with a single omission." (Results, p. 1006)

**Relevance**: Boundary condition for habit formation—short lapses are tolerable; supports resilience in behavioral interventions.

---

> "It is interesting to note that even in this study where the participants were motivated to create habits, approximately half did not perform the behaviour consistently enough to achieve habit status." (Discussion, p. 1006)

**Relevance**: Success rate ceiling for habit formation (~50% in motivated sample) suggests consistency is a bottleneck, not behavior choice or motivation alone.

## Extraction Notes

**Quality Assessment**: This is a foundational, well-designed study with clear methodology, transparent analysis, and appropriate caveats. Nonlinear modeling approach is novel and well-executed. Primary strength: idiographic (individual-level) modeling of real-world habit formation. Primary weakness: modest sample size for subgroup comparisons and reliance on self-report automaticity (SRHI). The asymptotic curve finding is robust; subgroup differences (behavior type) are trends only.

**Generalizability**: Findings apply well to health behavior habit formation in motivated adults. UK university student sample limits generalization to diverse populations and lower-motivation contexts. However, the asymptotic model itself (early gains, diminishing returns) is likely universal.

**Relevance to NotionRPG**: Exceptionally high. The 66-day median and 39-102-day IQR directly inform gamification timelines (long-term engagement, progressive challenge, milestone-based rewards). The ~50% success rate on consistency suggests need for reminders, social accountability, or reward structures to sustain daily repetition. The finding that single lapses are tolerable supports "forgiving" streak-break mechanics in game design.

**Cross-domain applicability**: Beyond habit formation, findings apply to learning science (automaticity = procedural fluency in motor/cognitive tasks follows similar curves), behavior change psychology, and gamification design (progression curves, engagement thresholds).
