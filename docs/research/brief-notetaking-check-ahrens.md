## 1. Ahrens's method in 10 lines

1. Writing is the medium of thinking. Work as if nothing counts except writing. Reading and note-taking are steps toward a written piece (ch05; ch02 intro).
2. **Fleeting notes** are quick reminders. Process them within a day or two, then throw them away. Underlining and margin comments are fleeting notes too: useless unless turned into proper notes soon (ch02 §2.1 step 1; ch06).
3. **Literature notes** are written while reading with a pen in hand. They are very short, extremely selective and in your own words, roughly a note that page x says y. They are kept with the bibliographic data in a reference system. Length depends on how hard the text is (ch02 step 2; ch10 §10.1; ch12 §12.7).
4. **Permanent notes** are written about once a day from the literature notes. Ask what the idea means for your own questions, and whether it contradicts, corrects, supports or adds to what you already have (ch02 step 3; ch11 §11.2).
5. Permanent notes hold one idea each, in full sentences, with sources. They are written for a reader who lacks the original context, which will soon include your future self. Each should fit on one screen (ch02 step 3; ch10 §10.4; ch12 §12.7).
6. File each note behind related notes, link it to others, and add a few index entry points. Choose keywords by the situation in which you would want to stumble on the note again, not by where it belongs (ch02 step 4; ch11 §11.4; ch12 §12.1–12.2).
7. Topics and questions emerge bottom-up from clusters of linked notes. Comparing a new note with old ones exposes duplicates, contradictions and fine distinctions (ch02 step 5; ch12 §12.3).
8. Copy quotes rarely. A quote stripped of its context changes meaning; translate it instead, keeping the original meaning (ch02 step 2; ch10 §10.1; ch11 intro).
9. The learning claim: elaboration, meaning connecting new information to many meaningful contexts, beats rereading and cramming. Flashcards beat cramming but stay isolated (ch10 §10.5; ch11 §11.3; ch12 §12.4).
10. Habit, not willpower. Set up an environment where the right step is the easy one, and build the habit of reading with a pen (ch01 §1.2, where Luhmann says he only does what comes easily; ch14).

### 2a. The six focus questions

1. **Permanent, standalone, linked notes vs per-source My notes.** This is the one real gap.
   - Ahrens's method stands on the permanent-note step: ideas leave their source and get linked to other notes. My notes stops at the literature-note stage.
   - Ore replaces his re-encounter mechanism (old notes resurfacing while linking) with scheduled retrieval. For retention that is the better-evidenced choice [E].
   - What is lost is the *cross-source elaboration act*: comparing two authors' versions of an idea, noticing a contradiction, connecting to prior knowledge. Elaboration with prior knowledge has moderate evidence (Stein 1984, which Ahrens cites; self-explanation, Bisra) [E]. The slip-box as a package has none.
   - The "no archive" guardrail is **not** in tension with Ahrens. He rejects archives too.
   - The tension is narrower. D34.1 plus per-source scoping leaves no place where an idea from source A meets one from source B in Tyler's own words. Search is the only bridge.
   - Smallest fix: changes 1–3 below. None of them needs a notes screen.
2. **"Short, selective, own words" vs D15 one-liners.** These match. Ahrens's literature notes vary with difficulty: sometimes keywords, sometimes a whole book in one sentence, longer for unfamiliar material (ch10 §10.1). One line per highlight plus a longer My notes covers that range.
3. **Rereading notes.**
   - He dismisses rereading *sources*, and re-viewing for memory (ch10 §10.4–10.5). Luhmann almost never read a text twice.
   - He treats revisiting *notes* as central, but as writing work: daily processing of fleeting notes, and comparing and correcting while linking. It is never a review session.
   - D34.1 matches him on "no review screen". It does not provide his writing-time re-encounter, except through search.
   - The summary brief's table row "Rereading notes or highlights adds little" is internally inconsistent. The evidence brief §1.6 says reviewing *your own notes* helped (Mueller Study 3, d = 0.64; Jansen) and only reviewing *highlights* did not (g = −0.06). See change 4.
4. **Highlighting and copying quotes.**
   - Highlights are fleeting notes, nearly worthless unless processed within a day or so (ch06). Underlining plus margin comments is close to taking no notes at all (ch10 §10.1).
   - Collecting de-contextualised quotes is about the worst research approach there is (ch11 intro, citing Lonka).
   - This strongly backs D34.2 and D29. It is mild pressure on Keep-only highlights, which D15 already handles by making the note one key away.
5. **Notes into questions or flashcards; spaced repetition.**
   - He never recommends spaced repetition software.
   - He grants that flashcards beat cramming, but calls them isolated (ch12 §12.4).
   - He claims the slip-box itself delivers spacing, variation, contextual interference and retrieval through ordinary use. That is an overclaim (§3a, item 5).
   - Questions, in his workflow, are prompts for further inquiry and further notes (ch11 §11.2), not review items. Ore's design (D1 prompts, D19, D34.9) meets his objection better than plain flashcards would.
6. **Habit, effort and optional prompts.**
   - He argues environment over willpower: make the right action the easy default and standardise the format so there are no decisions (ch01 §1.2; ch09; ch12 §12.7; ch14).
   - One-key-dismiss prompts, no nags and a fixed note format fit that.
   - Where he differs: he wants writing on *every* reading as the habit, and he would count notes per day. Ore keeps writing optional except the finish recall, and it bans counts (D23). Keep Ore's position. His own habit argument cuts against forcing.

## 3. What Ahrens cites, and how he uses it

### 3a. Overstated, or contradicted by the evidence briefs

1. **Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014** (ch10 §10.1).
   - What he says: handwriting gives much better understanding, still measurable after a week. He also says several independent studies show this, but cites only one.
   - Problems:
     - The outcome difference does not replicate. Urry 2021: g = 0.04 over 8 studies. Morehead 2019: both ns (evidence brief §3.1) [E].
     - In Mueller's own Study 3, the one-week advantage appeared only when students could study their notes (evidence brief §1.6).
     - The *process* claim does hold: typing produces more verbatim notes (evidence brief §3.2).
     - Telling people not to copy did nothing (Mueller Study 2).
   - **Contradicted.**
2. **Elaboration called the best-researched, most successful learning method** (ch10 §10.5) and proven better than any other approach (ch11 §11.3, McDaniel & Donnelly 1996).
   - Dunlosky 2013 rates elaborative interrogation *moderate* utility. Practice testing and distributed practice are rated *high* (evidence brief §2.5).
   - **Overstated.**
3. **Rereading called nearly useless**, with ping-pong said to help more (ch10 §10.4–10.5).
   - Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009 is a survey of student study habits, not a test of rereading.
   - Dunlosky rates rereading *low*, not zero.
   - Donoghue & Hattie report d ≈ 0.47 on mostly surface, immediate outcomes (evidence brief, line 225).
   - The exercise claim rests on Ratey, a popular book.
   - **Overstated.** The direction is right; the size is not.
4. **Bjork's storage vs retrieval strength** (ch11 §11.3).
   - Ahrens says storage strength can't be improved and repetition is pointless.
   - In Bjork's theory, storage strength accumulates with study and especially with retrieval. Ahrens lumps all repetition in with cramming.
   - **Misread.** This matters because it can read as a case against spaced retrieval. In ch12 §12.4 he endorses spacing and retrieval.
5. **The slip-box said to force spacing, variation, contextual interference and retrieval** (ch12 §12.4).
   - Meeting an old note while linking is re-exposure with the note in view. It is not effortful recall, and the intervals are uncontrolled. No study tests it.
   - **Overstated, and the most important one for Ore**: this is why FSRS retrieval is still needed on top of good notes.
6. **Flashcards called isolated, citing Birnbaum et al. 2013** (ch12 §12.4).
   - As far as I know, that paper is about why interleaving helps category learning, not about flashcards lacking context.
   - **Citation stretched** (verify).
7. **Kornell & Bjork 2008, interleaving art styles** (ch11 §11.3).
   - The finding is real.
   - The leap to the claim that not sorting notes by topic improves learning and classification is his.
   - **Extrapolated.**
8. **Zeigarnik** (ch09).
   - He credits Zeigarnik's follow-up research with showing that writing a task down frees the mind as if it were done.
   - The writing-down result is Masicampo & Baumeister 2011, and it requires a specific *plan*, not just a note.
   - The Zeigarnik memory effect itself replicates poorly (a 2023 meta-analysis found no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks; outside the library, verify).
   - **Misattributed and overstated.** Not load-bearing for Ore.
9. **Willpower and success.**
   - ch01 §1.2: studies are said to have repeatedly proven that success comes from environments, not willpower (Neal 2012 etc.).
   - ch14: researchers are said to find hardly any measurable link between long-run intentions and behaviour.
   - Both are overstated. Neal is about habits. Intention–behaviour links are real but modest.
   - He *does* flag the ego-depletion replication problems himself (endnote 2; ch09). That is a fair hedge.
10. **Offloading is free** (ch11 §11.3; ch10 §10.5).
    - He claims moving ideas to external memory lets you forget them without cost and even helps learning.
    - This is contradicted by the offloading studies in the practice brief §2.5 (Grinschgl 2020; Eskritt & Ma 2013) unless there is an explicit goal to remember plus closed-book recall [E].

### 3b. Fairly used

- Arnold & McDermott 2013 (pretesting), Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (testing effect), Bjork 2011's desirable-difficulties quote, and the mere-exposure point (Bornstein 1989) (ch10 §10.4–10.5).
- His endnote 29 admits a quote he attributes to Jang 2012 isn't in that paper. That is honest, but it shows second-hand sourcing.
