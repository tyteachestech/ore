# Brief: learner-made diagrams (concept maps, matrices, drawings) (2026-10-23)

Input to deciding whether Ore gets a diagramming or canvas feature, and what shape it takes. D34.1 currently bans "a canvas". That ban was written against note-organizing busywork, not against drawing as a learning act; this brief tests the second.

## Answer

Making a diagram helps when it makes Tyler **select, organize and retrieve** ideas himself, and it stops helping the moment the work shifts to arranging boxes. The evidence supports three narrow forms, not a general canvas:
1. **A node-link map or sketch built from memory**, closed-book, as an alternative format for the finish recall. It works as well as written free recall; it does not beat it (Blunt & Karpicke 2014).
2. **A matrix for compare-and-contrast material**, with rows and columns given and cells filled by him (d = 1.07, 8 of 8 comparisons; F&M ch03).
3. **A quick sketch of a causal or spatial system** (how X produces Y), followed by comparison with a correct diagram (drawing d = 0.40 overall, up to 0.94 with comparison; F&M ch04).

What hurts or wastes time:
- Mapping with the text open, by a reader who already knows the domain (high-ability median d = −0.08, F&M ch03).
- Tool mechanics: an on-screen drag-and-drop drawing tool erased the benefit (d = −0.16 comprehension; F&M ch04 l184).
- Building a map before recalling (no gain, 10 extra minutes; O'Day & Karpicke 2021).
- Studying an AI- or expert-made map in place of making one (g = 0.43 vs 0.72 for making one; Schroeder 2018).

Cross-source "graph of my ideas" views (Obsidian graph, Ahrens-style link maps) have no learning evidence. They are practitioner opinion.

## Boundary conditions

**Prior knowledge and ability.** The strongest moderator in the book.
- Mapping: low-ability d = 0.45 vs high-ability d = −0.08 (4 comparisons each; F&M ch03 l274). The ch03 summary prints +0.08 (l5); Box 1 and the body say −0.08.
- Individual studies: good readers −0.09 (Liu 2010), high-ability −0.07 (Haugwitz 2010), high verbal −0.35 (Roberts & Dansereau 2008; ch03 l238–256).
- F&M's reading: "high-knowledge learners are more likely to engage spontaneously in useful learning strategies" (ch10 l44).
- Tyler reads mostly in known domains, so text-open mapping during reading should be expected to add little for him.
- Imagining runs the other way: it works better for experienced learners (ch10 Table 10.1 l36).

**Material structure.**
- Matrices worked on compare-and-contrast texts. All 8 studies used that structure (ch03 l270).
- Drawing worked on texts explaining "how a scientific system works" (ch04 l198); every Table 4.2 study is science.
- Mapping "presuppose[s] that the instructional material has a clear underlying structure" (ch03 l5).
- For non-spatial material (social science, humanities, narrative), verbal summaries may fit better (ch02 l206).
- Roelle & Nückles 2019, as summarized in `brief-notetaking-practice.md` §2.2: for well-written texts retrieval helped and generative tasks did not; for low-cohesion texts only generative tasks helped.

**Training.** The authors disagree.
- F&M: "effective mapping strategies require extensive training" (ch03 l5); several hours of training (l286).
- Karpicke & Blunt's reply to Mintzes et al. (*Science* 334:453, 2011): mapping advocates themselves say it "can be taught in 10–20 minutes". They also found "no evidence from randomized, controlled experiments that extensive training on concept mapping is necessary".
- In most of F&M's large concept-map effects, training, feedback and teacher help come with the mapping (Chularut 1.33/4.00; Guastello 6.02; Passmore 2.65; ch03 l230–242), so mapping's own effect is not separated.

**Spatial or drawing ability.**
- F&M treat difficulty with "the mechanics of drawing" as extraneous load (ch04 l120). They report no ability moderator.
- The word-level drawing effect did not depend on artistic skill (row 20, Secondary).
- No study isolates spatial ability for adults.

**Outcome measured.**
- Drawing gains are largest on transfer when supported (Van Meter 2006: transfer, not multiple-choice; ch04 l174).
- In Cromley's meta-analysis drawing gains are largest on factual items (g = 0.85) and smallest on transfer (0.22).
- Mapping effects hold on comprehension and transfer tests too (ch03 l280).

**Time.** Most positive drawing and mapping studies did not match time to the control.
- Van Meter: more than twice the time, and more than triple with comparison (ch04 l170–172).
- O'Day Exp 2: 25 vs 5 min.
- Time-matched comparisons are the ones where retrieval wins (rows 8, 10). The matrix studies are the exception: Jairam & Kiewra and Ponce & Mayer gave both groups the same time (ch03 l266–268).

**Publication bias.** Published mapping effects run about 3–4× unpublished ones (rows 6, 7).

**Book inconsistencies.**
- Schwamborn 2010b Exp 2: text says comprehension 0.40, transfer 0.81 (ch04 l180); Table 4.2 says transfer 0.40, comprehension 0.81 (l150–151).
- Chiou 2009 individual mapping is d = 0.90 in the text (l236) but blank in the table (l193).
- The GB conflict file gives Karpicke & Blunt Exp 2 as "d = 1.01–1.07"; the paper says 1.07 (short answer) and 1.01 (map test). The two agree.

## What this implies for Ore

Each principle is tied to rows above. "Inference" marks where I extend past the evidence.

1. **Diagramming is a retrieval format, not a workspace.** Offer "map or sketch it from memory" as an alternative to typed free recall at the finish and chapter recalls (D18), with the text, highlights and notes hidden (D34.4). Closed-book maps match written recall (row 9) and both beat text-open work (rows 8, 9). It must not be a third step after typed recall: stacking a map on recall bought nothing (row 10). He picks one format per recall.
2. **No text-open mapping canvas beside the reader.** Text-open mapping lost to recall by d = 1.07–1.50 (row 8), and for high-ability readers it is near zero (Boundary: prior knowledge). This keeps D34.1's intent: no organizing surface.
3. **Mechanics near zero.** The tool is the cost (rows 4, 12c, 12d; ch04 l120): an on-screen drag-and-drop tool turned d = 0.87 into −0.16. So: type a node, type a labeled arrow to another node, automatic layout. No styling, colors, shapes, free positioning or infinite canvas. Keyboard-first (D26). *Inference:* text entry like `A -causes-> B` with auto-layout comes closest to the paper conditions that worked; tldraw/Excalidraw-style freeform drawing is the condition that failed.
4. **Fit the form to the content, and keep it optional.**
   - Matrix for compare-and-contrast: the largest effect, with equal time (row 3).
   - Sketch for causal or spatial systems (rows 12, 19).
   - Plain written recall for everything else (ch02 l206).
   - AI may propose the *frame*: the row and column labels of a matrix, or "draw how X leads to Y". The Kauffman matrices supplied topic names and categories (ch03 l264). *Inference:* AI choosing the frame does some of the selecting; that is the tested condition, so accept it.
   - Never a filled map. Partial fill-in maps did not beat studying a complete map for undergraduates (Wong 2020, Wang 2020, Abstracts), so skip blanks-to-fill exercises.
5. **AI's diagram only after his attempt, as a comparison, with prompts.**
   - The comparison step is where drawing's large effects come from (rows 12a, 15, 16): "What does the AI version have that yours lacks? Which link differs? Change anything?"
   - Shown first or instead, it is studying a provided map: g = 0.43, or 0.32 for postsecondary learners (rows 5a, 5c). Copying a map is Active, not Constructive (ICAP, `brief-notetaking-practice.md` §2.1).
   - Showing it beside the work also risked less effort (row 13a).
   - This is D34.3 applied to diagrams.
6. **AI feedback on his map, same rules as text feedback.**
   - Feedback follows the attempt (D18). It names missing central ideas and wrong links, and never edits his map (D34.2).
   - It never sets a rating (D21).
   - Feedback did accompany several large classroom effects, but always confounded with training (Boundary: training).
   - No study tests LLM feedback on learner maps for learning outcomes. Reviews of LLM feedback on text find it beats no feedback but warn of hallucination and over-positivity (Maier et al. 2026, Abstract).
7. **Maps feed retrieval, not an archive.**
   - A labeled link he drew ("A inhibits B") can seed one `prompt` item ("Explain how A affects B"), credited "from your map" like "from your notes" (D19). *Inference*, consistent with retrieval > restudy.
   - No gallery or map-review screen (D34.1). The saved map is one more artifact on the source, shown only after an attempt (D34.4).
8. **Cost guard.** One diagram per finish or chapter recall, not per section (rows 4, 12d; F&M: "use mapping sparingly or in abbreviated form", ch03 l284).
9. **Offer "picture it" before "draw it".** For a system he can picture, a no-mechanics "imagine it, then type what you saw" costs nothing to try (row 13). Evidence is one comparison, and the benefit favours experienced learners.
10. **Judge it on retention (D34.8).** Report retention for items seeded by map recalls vs typed recalls, null below 10 reviews. Students systematically overrate mapping (row 8b), so how a session feels is no guide.

**Proposed decision changes** (for Tyler; not applied):
- **D34.1:** keep "no canvas" as an organizing surface. Add: "A closed-book map, matrix or sketch is allowed as a recall format at the finish and chapter recalls (D18). Minimal mechanics, AI comparison after the attempt."
- **D18:** the finish and chapter recalls accept typed text *or* a closed-book diagram, never both in one recall.
- Defer the matrix until the material type is known (the F&M check skipped it as #8 for its UI cost). It is the best-supported form, but only for compare-and-contrast material.

## Unsupported or practitioner-only

- **Cross-source idea graphs** (Obsidian graph view, Heptabase or Obsidian Canvas boards, Ahrens/Luhmann linking). No controlled study of learning outcomes.
  - The one nearby study (row 21, Abstract) found that mapping three documents improved structural integration over summaries. One study, not a graph tool.
  - An auto-drawn graph of his notes is an AI-made map he studies (row 5c), not generation.
  - Ahrens's value claim is about writing and linking notes, not viewing graphs (`brief-notetaking-check-ahrens.md`). Practitioner.
- **Freeform infinite canvases** (tldraw, Excalidraw, MarginNote, LiquidText) as learning tools: no learning-outcome evidence. Every user community reports the same failure, piles of cards never revisited (`brief-notetaking-practice.md` item 10). Practitioner.
- **"Visual learners" benefit more.** Not supported. Fit the form to the content, not the person ("A complex spatial relationship should be shown as a diagram for everyone"; GB `guidebook/audiobook-edition.md` l2042).
- **Extensive mapping training is required.** Asserted by F&M and Mintzes; Karpicke & Blunt found no controlled test of it.
- **AI-generated concept maps help learning.** Evidence so far is map quality and student perceptions (e.g. ChatGPT maps comparable to teacher maps, N = 83; Schicchi 2025, Abstract), not learning outcomes.
- **Collaborative mapping g = 0.91** (row 5d) is irrelevant to a single user and confounded.

## Open questions

1. Is a closed-book map worth its extra time over typed recall for Tyler? Blunt & Karpicke matched time at 10 min per period. With his typing speed and an unfamiliar tool, the map may take longer for the same result. n-of-1: alternate formats across sources and compare D34.8 retention.
2. Which works better after the attempt: comparison with a full AI map, or with a list of central ideas and links? Leutner 2025 favours integration support, but no study uses AI-made comparison maps.
3. Does seeing the AI comparison map later reduce effort on future maps? Row 13a suggests the risk; untested for adults with maps.
4. Do maps help *application* (D1) more than typed recall does? Mapping evidence leans on comprehension and recall tests. Drawing helps transfer only with support (rows 12a, 14).
5. Ortega-Tudela 2019: map from memory, then write a paragraph from memory, beat the reverse order at 2 weeks (Abstract). If confirmed in full text, a map-first finish could be justified. Read the paper before acting.
6. Not verified, worth fetching before a build decision:
   - Lechuga et al. 2015 full text.
   - Schroeder 2018 full text (moderators were read from a secondary table).
   - Cromley 2019's paper-vs-technology moderator (asked in its title; result not seen).
   - Nesbit & Adesope 2006's constructing vs studying numbers (0.82 / 0.37 are secondary).
