# Evidence brief: exercise types

Prepared 2026-11-10 for Tyler's question: which exercise types work, is fill in the blank one of them, and what is the science-backed way to write each, with what to avoid.

"Exercise" is the word that replaces "card" (Tyler, 2026-11-10): one thing Ore asks him to do with an idea from memory. He answers, sees how he did against the source, and it comes back later, often in a new form.

## How to read this

- It extends `brief-learning-science.md` §3, §4, §6 and §8, and uses the same grades (Well-established, Moderately supported, Emerging, Preliminary / Inference, Practitioner). Numbers marked "main brief" come from there.
- Sources were found with SciSpace and Consensus searches and read from their abstracts, not full texts. Check a number in the paper before quoting it in public.
- Lab effects shrink by about a third in real use (main brief). Plan with that.

## The short answer

1. **The format matters less than what the exercise makes him retrieve, and whether he is corrected.** Short answer and multiple choice land close together in classrooms once there is feedback (g = 0.64 and 0.57, main brief; [6]). Without feedback the results split: typed answers won in a university course [5] and lost in a lab study where few answers could be recalled [7].
2. **What he can do afterwards follows what the exercise asked.** Practising a fact strengthens that fact. It did not raise performance on questions that need the idea used [18][2]. Asking for use, on a different case each time, is what builds use [16][19][20][21].
3. **Fill in the blank: yes for exact things, no for understanding.** It helps the blanked item and little else [1][2]. Use it for a term, a number, a name, a piece of syntax. Never for a reason, a mechanism or steps.
4. **Feedback is half of every exercise.** For anything beyond a fact, feedback that explains beats feedback that only shows the answer, and the gain shows up on new questions [59][60].
5. **A hard question is fine when it is corrected.** A failed attempt followed by the answer still helps [61][62]. Without the answer, what he could not recall gains little [4][7].

## One process for every exercise

Each step names its evidence. Steps without a study behind them say so.

| Step | What to do | Why | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Outcome first | Decide what he should be able to do with the idea: recall it exactly, explain it, use it, tell it apart, carry it out. The type follows from that. | Fact practice did not lift higher-order performance; higher-order and mixed practice did [18][19]. Transfer is strongest to application and inference when practice asked for them [16]. | Moderately supported |
| 2. Key before question | Write the answer and its 2 to 4 key points from the source before the question is shown, keeping the source's hedges. Grade against that key. | AI grading is reliable on rubric items and unreliable on open judgement (main brief §3). Ore's D158 already does this for tutor practice. | Inference |
| 3. One target | One thing to retrieve. A list or a set of parts becomes an Explain exercise whose key points are the parts. | No controlled study. Ore's own question eval found two-part questions had key points nobody asked for (D147). | Practitioner |
| 4. New words, stands alone | Ask it in words the source did not use, with the context named. Never lift the source sentence. | Completion items helped only the repeated item [1]; transfer is weakest when the same content is merely rearranged [16]. | Inference |
| 5. Always correct | Show the answer after every attempt. For concepts, show why, and which key points he hit or missed. | Explanation feedback beat correct-answer feedback on new inference questions [59]. Elaborated 0.49, correct answer 0.32, right/wrong only 0.05 [60]. | Well-established |
| 6. Type it when it has parts | A one-word answer can be recalled in his head. An answer with several parts should be typed or said. | In-head recall equals typed recall for simple items [57], but typed recall was better for definitions (d = 0.43) because in-head recall stops early [58]. | Moderately supported |
| 7. Bring it back changed | Once he knows it, the next time asks the same idea on a case he has not seen. | A different example each time beat the same one on transfer [20][21][22]. | Moderately supported |

## The types

Each type lists what it is for, the evidence, how to write one, and what to avoid.

### 1. Recall (short answer)

- **For:** terms, definitions, names, numbers, single facts.
- **Evidence (Well-established):** classroom g = 0.64 (main brief). Helps the targeted information and makes him better at judging what he knows [3]. Its edge over multiple choice depends on him being able to recall the answer, or being shown it [4][5][7].
- **Write it:** a question in new words with one short answer; name the context; accept answers that mean the same.
- **Avoid:**
  - a list ("name the five…"): make it an Explain exercise;
  - a yes/no question (half right by guessing);
  - a question that contains its answer or a give-away cue;
  - a question with several right answers;
  - facts he has no use for: fact practice alone did not lift understanding [18].

### 2. Fill in the blank

- **For:** exact wording that matters. A term inside its definition, a number, a name, a command or piece of syntax, one part of a formula.
- **Evidence (Moderately supported for the item, against it for understanding):**
  - Fill-in practice improved only the repeated items, not related ones, while open paragraph recall carried over to new questions [1].
  - It improved recall of the premises but not the inferences that follow from them [2].
  - Producing a word beats reading it, d = 0.40 across 86 studies, mostly word lists in the lab [65].
  - The g = 0.90 for "fill-in" prompts in the main brief is for filling in an explanation, from two studies (Emerging) [27].
  - No study compares a blank against the same fact asked as a question (gap, main brief §4).
- **Write it:**
  1. Decide the single thing to recall.
  2. Write a fresh, complete statement around it. Do not reuse the source's sentence.
  3. Blank the one word or number that carries the meaning.
  4. Check that exactly one answer fits, and that the rest of the sentence does not give it away ("a/an", plurals, a rhyme).
  5. If the idea is a why or a how, stop: it is an Explain exercise.
- **Avoid:**
  - blanking a lifted sentence, which can be answered from the sentence's shape;
  - more than one blank;
  - blanking a word that doesn't matter;
  - a blank many words could fill;
  - blanks for reasons, mechanisms or steps: it looks learned and doesn't carry over [1][2];
  - the same fact as several different blanks, hoping they reinforce each other (limited transfer between them, main brief §4).

### 3. Explain (why, how it works)

- **For:** concepts, mechanisms, trade-offs.
- **Evidence (Well-established, with limits):** prompted self-explanation g = 0.55 over 69 effects [27]; small to moderate in maths, with less evidence on delayed retention [28]. A focused prompt beats an open "explain this" [29], but writing his own explanation beats completing a given one [30]. Why-questions work when he has background on the topic and fade when he has none [31][32].
- **Write it:** ask a specific why or how ("Why does X lead to Y?"). Key points first, each one something the question asks for. Feedback marks the points he hit and missed.
- **Avoid:**
  - "Explain X" or "What do you know about X";
  - two questions joined by "and";
  - key points the question never asked for;
  - a why-question on a topic that is brand new to him, before its definition has been asked;
  - asking why a doubtful claim "works so well".

### 4. Apply to a new case

- **For:** anything he wants to use.
- **Evidence (Moderately supported):** practice transfers at d = 0.40 overall, most to application and inference [16][17]. Higher-order practice raised higher-order performance and fact practice did not, so use does not have to wait until the facts are solid [18][19]. A different case each time beat the same case [20][21][22]. Gains on applying complex ideas needed several rounds and a delay of days [8].
- **Write it:**
  1. Start from the idea's key points.
  2. Invent a setting from his own work that the source did not use.
  3. Ask for a decision or a product in that setting.
  4. Map every key point to the original idea, with at least one tied to the new setting, so the memorised answer earns part credit at most.
  5. Check it can be answered from the source's idea alone.
  6. Use a new setting each time it returns.
- **Avoid:**
  - reusing the source's own example (that is recall);
  - a case that needs knowledge the source doesn't give;
  - a case so long that reading it takes longer than answering;
  - the same case every time.

### 5. Predict

- **For:** cause and effect, surprising results, "what happens if".
- **Evidence (Moderately supported for surprising material):** committing to a prediction before the answer raised memory for outcomes that broke expectations and raised curiosity [25][26]. Guessing before reading helps the questioned material, with little spread to the rest [24]; a pretest can match a posttest [23].
- **Write it:** a situation whose outcome the idea decides; ask for the outcome and the reason; he commits before the reveal; the feedback explains why.
- **Avoid:**
  - outcomes that are coin flips or can't be known;
  - a guess with no feedback after it;
  - a situation where something other than the idea decides the outcome;
  - material with nothing surprising in it (the gain is on what breaks his expectation [26]).

### 6. Find the mistake

- **For:** procedures, common misconceptions, code.
- **Evidence (Moderately supported, mixed):** finding, explaining and fixing an error did as well as or better than solving problems, mostly on delayed tests [33][34][35]. The written explanation is what works, not the spotting [36]. Some studies found nothing [37].
- **Write it:** start from a correct worked answer; plant one realistic mistake (a known misconception, not a typo); ask him to find it, say why it is wrong and fix it. The key holds the error, the reason and the fix.
- **Avoid:**
  - more than one error;
  - a silly error;
  - spotting with no explanation;
  - a wrong statement shown and never corrected, since wrong options can be remembered as true [9];
  - using it before he has met the correct version.

### 7. Compare, or choose and justify

- **For:** ideas he confuses, picking between methods.
- **Evidence (Moderately supported):** comparing cases beat studying them one at a time across 57 experiments [38], and carried a principle to new problems [39][40]. It works better when he is told what to compare [41].
- **Write it:** two cases that differ on the one feature that matters; ask which fits and why, or what the difference changes. The key names the principle.
- **Avoid:**
  - cases that differ in many ways;
  - "list the similarities" with no principle to find;
  - a choice where one option is plainly silly.

### 8. Name it from a case

- **For:** telling concepts apart when they show up unlabelled.
- **Evidence (Inference):** from comparison and category learning [40] and from interleaving confusable ideas (main brief §6).
- **Write it:** a short case that shows the concept without naming it; ask for the name and what gives it away.
- **Avoid:** the term or its root in the case; a case that fits two terms.

### 9. Recall it all, or teach it

- **For:** a whole section or source, straight after reading and at goal tries. Not a spaced exercise.
- **Evidence (Moderately supported, contested):** free recall beat concept mapping on later inference questions [46], though a 2023 paper argues that comparison was confounded [47], and combining the two added nothing [48]. Free recall helps the broad picture and motivation, short answer helps the targeted points [3]; the two together did best [49]. Explaining to an imagined learner gives small to moderate, inconsistent gains [52], and the gain seems to come from the retrieval in it [50]. One study found teaching with the text open beat teaching from memory and neither beat restudy [51].
- **Write it:** one prompt that names the scope ("everything you remember about X from this chapter") or the audience ("explain X to someone new"). Afterwards, show what he missed against the source's main ideas.
- **Avoid:** scheduling it as a spaced item (too long, no clean grade); no feedback on what was missed; a scope as wide as a whole book; using it instead of targeted questions, since they do different jobs [3].

### 10. Multiple choice and true/false

- **For:** quick checks, questions before reading, small screens.
- **Evidence (Well-established with feedback):** classroom g = 0.57, close to short answer (main brief), and as good as short answer in school classes with feedback [6]. Without feedback the comparison with typed answers goes both ways [5][7], and wrong options can be remembered as true [9][13]; feedback removes that [10]. Options that are all plausible make him retrieve why each is wrong, which helps related questions too [11][12]. True/false beats rereading [14], but multiple choice beat true/false for understanding [15].
- **Write it:** a stem that is a full question answerable without the options; 3 or 4 options, each wrong one a real confusion; feedback every time, saying why.
- **Avoid:** no feedback; filler options nobody would pick; "all of the above" and "none of the above"; cues (the longest option, grammar that fits one option); negative stems; true/false alone. Flawed items of these kinds are common and penalise some learners [66], though which flaw does how much harm is not predictable [67].

### 11. Order the steps, or complete the example

- **For:** procedures and code.
- **Evidence (Emerging to Moderately supported, mostly programming and maths):** putting shuffled lines of code in order taught novices as much as writing the code, in less time [44][45]. Showing a worked example with steps removed one by one helped near transfer [42]. The support should fade as he improves, because it slows people who can already do it [43].
- **Write it:** take a correct procedure. To order: shuffle the steps, with at most one extra step that is a common error. To complete: show it with one step missing, then more, then none.
- **Avoid:** keeping the support once he can do it; steps whose order doesn't matter; many distractors.

### 12. Label the picture

- **For:** figures, diagrams, maps, charts. Ore's figure exercises (D166).
- **Evidence:** retrieval works on pictures as on words (D166's source). Drawing from a text helps understanding against reading alone, but costs time and needs guidance [53].
- **Write it:** one box hides one label; the rest of the figure stays visible.
- **Avoid:** hiding everything at once; a label that the neighbouring labels give away.

### 13. Writing his own questions

- **Evidence (Moderately supported, against relying on it):** writing and answering his own questions did no better than answering given ones and took longer [54]; self-written questions were poorly aimed [55]; in a class, given questions beat generating them [56]. Against that, making his own flashcards beat premade ones (d ≈ 0.3 to 0.45, main brief §4).
- **So:** Ore writes the exercises from his highlights and notes, and he edits them. His own questions stay what they are today: things he wants answered.

## Hints

- When he can't start, fading cues (first letters, then fewer) rescued learning that plain testing lost [63].
- A hint after a wrong answer was no better than showing the answer when practice time was fixed [64].
- So a hint belongs before a blank attempt, not after a wrong one.

## Ore today, type by type

| Type | In Ore today | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Recall | Fact | Keep |
| Fill in the blank | Allowed inside Fact | Keep, with a tighter rule (below) |
| Explain | Explain | Keep |
| Apply, Predict, Find the mistake, Choose and justify | The changed versions an Explain exercise comes back as (D66); tries (D148) | Keep. The evidence says use need not wait for the facts [18] |
| Name it from a case | The changed version of a definition (D66) | Keep |
| Recall it all, teach it | Finish recall, goal Try it | Keep, unscheduled |
| Label the picture | Figure exercises (D166) | Keep |
| Before reading | Prequestions | Keep |
| Multiple choice | None | Not on the Mac, where typing with AI feedback is the stronger form. A candidate for the iPhone, where typing is slow |
| Order the steps, complete the example | None | Candidate once procedures and code matter more; needs its own control |
| In my head (D68) | Older Explain exercises | Watch: in-head recall of multi-part answers is weaker [58]. Ore's "which points did you get?" step is the mitigation |

## Proposed for Ore (Tyler picks; nothing here is built)

1. **Record the types and the seven-step process as a decision** with the rename, so "exercise" has a definition and each type has its writing rules and its list of what to avoid.
2. **Tighten fill in the blank** in the writing prompt: only for a term, number, name or piece of syntax; a fresh statement, never the source's sentence; one blank, one possible answer.
3. **Add two checks to the question eval** (`bun run eval:questions`): "not lifted from the source" and "the blank is the word that carries the meaning", then run it to see how today's exercises score.
4. **Later, mocked first:** order the steps for procedures, and multiple choice on the iPhone.

## Gaps

- No head-to-head study of a blank against a question for the same fact.
- Little on exercise types for adults learning on their own over months; most studies are students over days or weeks.
- No controlled study of the one-target rule.
