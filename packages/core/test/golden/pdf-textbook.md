**CHAPTER 1**

## LEARNING

We call ourselves learners because **memory** is so central to us. For thousands of years, we have tried to understand how we remember and forget, and how a handful of habits can change what stays with us. The study of **learning science** tries to say which habits work. Most of what we read is gone within a week, as the forgetting curve shows. The strongest remedy is to practise **retrieval**, pulling an idea back out of memory instead of reading it again, a move every chapter in this book returns to.

### 1.1 Why Retrieval Works

Each time we recall an idea, the path to it gets easier to find the next time. This is why a test is not only a measure of what we know but a way of learning it. Rereading feels fluent, and that fluency fools us into thinking we know the page. A quiz breaks the illusion at once: either the answer comes, or it does not.

### 1.1.1 Testing beats rereading

The **testing effect**, found again and again since the 1900s, is the gain from recalling an idea over reading it again. A test can take many forms:

- **free recall** to write down all you remember;

- **cued recall** to answer from a prompt or a question;

- **practice testing** to work through problems without the answers in view.

Each one asks the mind to rebuild the idea, and rebuilding is what makes it last.

### 1.1.2 Spacing out sessions

Practice spread over days beats the same practice crammed into one night. This **spacing** effect is among the oldest findings in psychology, and among the least used by students.
