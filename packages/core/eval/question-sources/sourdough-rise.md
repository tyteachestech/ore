title: Why sourdough rises (and why it sometimes doesn't)

A sourdough loaf rises without a packet of commercial yeast. Everything it needs comes from the starter: a paste of flour and water that has been fed for days or years until it holds a stable community of microbes. Understanding that community explains most of what goes right and wrong in a home bake.

## Two kinds of microbes

A healthy starter holds wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria, usually in a ratio of roughly one yeast cell to a hundred bacteria. They do different jobs. The yeasts eat sugars from the flour and give off carbon dioxide and a little alcohol. The bacteria eat sugars too, but they mostly produce lactic and acetic acids, which give sourdough its tang.

The two groups also protect each other. The acids the bacteria make lower the starter's pH, and most moulds and unwanted bacteria cannot grow in that acidity. The wild yeasts that live in starters tolerate it well, so the community stays stable for years as long as it is fed.

## How the gas is held

Carbon dioxide alone would simply bubble out of a wet paste. Bread rises because the dough can trap the gas. When flour is mixed with water, two proteins in wheat, glutenin and gliadin, bond into gluten: a stretchy network of strands. Kneading or folding lines those strands up and makes the network stronger.

Each bubble of gas made by the yeast inflates a tiny pocket in that network, the way breath inflates a balloon. A dough with weak gluten lets the gas escape and spreads flat. A dough with strong, well developed gluten holds the gas and rises up.

Many bakers let flour and water rest together for half an hour before adding the starter and salt. This rest, called an autolyse, lets the flour absorb water fully and lets gluten start forming on its own, so less kneading is needed later.

## Temperature sets the pace

Fermentation is a set of chemical reactions run by living cells, so temperature controls its speed. Wild yeasts are most active around 24 to 27 °C. At a cool room temperature of about 18 °C, the same dough can take twice as long to rise. The bacteria respond to temperature differently: cooler, longer fermentation tends to favour acetic acid, which tastes sharper, while warmer fermentation favours the milder lactic acid.

This is why recipe times are only guides. A dough that rises in four hours in a summer kitchen may need eight in winter. Bakers who judge by the dough rather than the clock get more reliable bread.

## Reading the dough

Two signs tell you when the dough is ready to bake. The first is volume: the dough should have grown by about half to three quarters during the bulk rise, not necessarily doubled. The second is the poke test. Press a floured finger into the dough. If the dent springs back at once, the dough needs more time. If it springs back slowly and leaves a small dent, it is ready. If it does not spring back at all, it has probably overproofed.

## When it goes wrong

Overproofing happens when fermentation runs too long. The yeast keeps making gas while the acids slowly weaken the gluten, until the network can no longer hold the bubbles. The dough collapses in the oven, and the loaf comes out flat, dense and very sour.

Underproofing is the opposite: too little gas when the loaf goes into the oven. The crust sets before the inside has finished expanding, so the loaf bursts open in odd places and the crumb is tight, with a few large holes near the top.

A weak starter causes many of these problems. If a starter does not reliably double within four to eight hours of feeding, it does not yet have enough active yeast to raise a loaf on schedule, and the bacteria have more time to sour and weaken the dough.
